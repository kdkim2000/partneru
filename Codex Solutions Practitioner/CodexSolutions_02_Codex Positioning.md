## Slide 6/49 - Understanding Codex at a seller-appropriate level

Codex is designed to support software delivery work across the SDLC. It is not limited to autocomplete or inline code suggestions.

Customers may already use coding assistants in different ways. Rather than generalizing about those tools, position Codex around the customer's priority engineering workflow: the work to delegate, the context and tools required, the expected output, and how engineers will review and verify it.

At a seller level, you can describe Codex as supporting software delivery work such as:

- Understanding unfamiliar or legacy codebases
- Moving from a task or requirement into implementation
- Testing and debugging
- Reviewing changes
- Improving documentation
- Supporting modernization and maintenance workflows
- Reducing repetitive engineering overhead

Customers may encounter Codex across several surfaces:

- The ChatGPT desktop app
- The Codex IDE extension and Codex CLI
- ChatGPT on the web and Codex cloud
- Supported repository, code review, and collaboration integrations
- Programmatic and automation surfaces, including the Codex SDK and App Server

Capabilities such as parallel agents, Git worktrees, skills, plugins, automations, and code review vary by surface and configuration. At seller level, connect the surface to the customer's workflow, then involve a technical specialist for setup, permissions, or control details.

This connects to a broader enterprise shift: moving from AI as isolated individual productivity support to intelligence embedded into how work gets done. In Codex-specific conversations, keep that idea anchored in software delivery.

Codex is relevant when intelligence can help engineering teams move work through planning, implementation, testing, review, documentation, modernization, and maintenance workflows with appropriate human oversight.

A useful seller-level explanation is:

> "Codex supports software delivery workflows by helping engineering teams delegate and complete multi-step technical work across the SDLC, while preserving human review, accountability, and workflow governance."

This framing matters because it avoids two weak extremes.

The first weak extreme is reducing Codex to "autocomplete." That makes the conversation too narrow and easy to dismiss if the customer already uses coding assistance tools.

The second weak extreme is describing Codex as fully autonomous software delivery. That can create trust concerns because enterprise customers still need review, accountability, and operational control.

Strong positioning sits between those extremes: Codex supports delegated engineering work across real software delivery workflows, while humans remain accountable for review, quality, and decisions.

## Slide 7/49 - Why Codex matters in engineering organizations

Many engineering organizations are not blocked by one single coding task. They are blocked by accumulated workflow friction.

Common sources of friction include: pull request review bottlenecks, legacy codebase complexity, testing and debugging overhead, onboarding delays, repeated CI investigation, context switching across repositories and systems, documentation gaps, coordination-heavy delivery workflows, and technical debt.

These issues matter because they affect business and operational outcomes.

Slow review cycles can delay releases. Poor documentation can slow onboarding and modernization. Repeated debugging work can absorb senior engineering time. Technical debt can limit how quickly the business responds to new customer needs.

Codex is relevant because it can support the engineering workflows where this friction appears.

The strongest conversation is not:

> "Codex helps developers code faster."

It is:

> "Codex can support the software delivery workflows where engineering teams lose time, repeat work, or struggle to move work through the SDLC consistently."

Use this distinction when positioning Codex:

| Do position Codex as... | Do not position Codex as... |
| --- | --- |
| Support for software delivery workflows | A replacement for engineering teams |
| A way to reduce repetitive engineering overhead | A guaranteed productivity multiplier |
| A capability that helps teams delegate well-scoped engineering work | Fully autonomous software delivery |
| A workflow-oriented engineering assistant | A simple autocomplete tool |
| Support that works within real engineering operating environments | A reason to remove review or governance |

The seller's job is to connect Codex to the customer's operational pain, not to overstate what AI can do.

## Slide 8/49 - How customers may initially describe the problem

Customers rarely begin by saying:

> "We need agentic software delivery."

More often, they describe symptoms:

- "Our release cycles are too slow."
- "Our engineering team is overloaded."
- "Pull request reviews are a bottleneck."
- "We have too much technical debt."
- "Debugging and testing take too much time."
- "Engineering workflows are inconsistent across teams."
- "New developers take too long to become productive."
- "We need to improve developer productivity."

These statements may indicate a Codex opportunity, but they are not enough by themselves.

Before positioning Codex, clarify what work is being performed, where the workflow slows down, which teams are involved, and which outcomes are affected.

For example, "developer productivity" could mean several different things:

| Customer says... | Possible underlying workflow friction |
| --- | --- |
| "Developers need to move faster." | Review bottlenecks, testing overhead, context switching, unclear tickets, slow implementation flow |
| "Our senior engineers are overloaded." | Knowledge concentration, review burden, onboarding support, repeated legacy code questions |

The same surface complaint can point to different workflows. That is why strong positioning starts with the workflow, not the feature.

## Slide 9/49 - Understand the workflow before positioning

Use the workflow lens to avoid jumping too quickly into product language.

A workflow lens simply means asking:

> "What work is actually happening, where does it slow down, and why does that matter?"

This is not a technical architecture model. It is a seller conversation aid. It helps you avoid generic claims and connect Codex to a repeatable business or engineering problem.

Before positioning Codex, identify four things.

1. **What is the work being performed?** What is the engineering activity? Examples include reviewing pull requests, debugging CI failures, onboarding to a legacy repository, updating tests, or documenting a code path.

2. **Where does the workflow slow down?** Is the delay happening during planning, implementation, testing, review, documentation, modernization, maintenance, or another software delivery workflow?

3. **Who is affected?** Which stakeholder feels the pain? Examples include engineering leaders, DevEx teams, platform teams, security stakeholders, product leaders, delivery leaders, or developers.

4. **Which outcome is affected?** Is the issue affecting release timing, delivery predictability, engineering capacity, onboarding, modernization, quality, or operational confidence?

A seller-level Codex conversation should also stay aware of three practical enterprise questions:

**Context** — What repository, workflow, documentation, ticket, test result, or engineering knowledge does Codex need to support the work?

**Delegated work** — What task or workflow could Codex help complete?

**Visibility and review** — How will humans review the work, assess quality, and maintain accountability?

These questions keep the conversation grounded in how work gets done. They also help you avoid positioning Codex as generic automation.

## Slide 10/49 - Seller positioning formula

Use this positioning formula when you need to move from customer pain to a credible Codex message:

> Customer workflow friction → affected SDLC stage → stakeholder priority → Codex-fit rationale → Codex positioning language → next discovery or validation step

The Codex-fit rationale is the reason Codex is the right positioning direction for this conversation. It should explain why the customer's problem is connected to recurring software delivery work, not just general AI interest, isolated coding help, or a different OpenAI solution path.

Use this flow to keep your positioning anchored in the customer's workflow before you introduce Codex language.

**Example 1: Slow PR reviews**

- Customer workflow friction: Pull requests are waiting too long for review.
- Codex-fit rationale: The issue is recurring software delivery friction in the review workflow, not broad productivity interest or isolated code generation.
- Affected SDLC stage: Review and approval.
- Codex positioning language: Position Codex around review support and workflow efficiency.
- Stakeholder priority: Engineering velocity and release timing.
- Next discovery or validation step: Validate where review bottlenecks create the most operational impact.

A seller might say:

> "It sounds like the main issue is not just writing code faster. It is the review workflow slowing down release timing. Codex may be relevant as a way to support review workflows and reduce repetitive review overhead, while preserving engineering accountability. I'd want to validate which teams or repositories feel that bottleneck most."

**Example 2: Legacy codebase onboarding delays**

- Customer workflow friction: New developers take too long to understand a legacy system.
- Codex-fit rationale: The issue is recurring software delivery friction around code understanding, documentation, maintenance, and modernization.
- Affected SDLC stage: Documentation, knowledge transfer, modernization, and maintenance.
- Codex positioning language: Position Codex around code understanding and onboarding support.
- Stakeholder priority: Developer productivity and modernization progress.
- Next discovery or validation step: Validate how onboarding friction affects delivery capacity.

A seller might say:

> "The opportunity may be less about individual coding speed and more about reducing the knowledge-transfer burden around a complex codebase. Codex may be relevant where teams need support understanding code, improving documentation, and moving modernization work forward with less repeated dependency on senior engineers."

Strong positioning focuses on workflow outcomes and operational impact before discussing features, models, or technical detail.

The next step should be to identify which workflow creates the greatest delivery impact and validate that with the engineering team."

This response is stronger because it:

- Starts with the customer's workflow friction
- Connects the pain to SDLC stages
- Avoids promising guaranteed productivity gains
- Preserves human accountability
- Proposes a concrete next step

## Slide 15/49 - Planning and implementation workflows

Planning is where teams define, prioritize, estimate, and organize work. Implementation is where engineering teams turn that work into code and system changes.

These workflows often create friction when teams have unclear context, fragmented repositories, repeated handoffs, or too much repetitive engineering work.

Common workflow friction includes:

- Slow movement from task to implementation
- Excessive context switching across repositories and systems
- Repetitive engineering work reducing delivery throughput
- Unclear technical context around tickets or requirements
- Senior engineers repeatedly explaining the same code paths or conventions

**Seller positioning direction:** Position Codex around workflow continuity, implementation movement, repository understanding, and reduced engineering overhead.

A seller-level response might sound like:

> "If the issue is that teams lose time moving from planned work into implementation, Codex may be relevant as a way to help engineers work with repository context, reduce repetitive setup and investigation, and move well-scoped tasks through implementation more efficiently."

Avoid saying:

> "Codex will take tickets and build everything automatically."

That is too broad and autonomy-heavy. Keep the positioning tied to well-scoped engineering work and human review.

## Slide 16/49 - Testing and debugging workflows

Testing and debugging workflows are often strong areas for Codex positioning because they are recurring, evidence-based, and connected to release confidence.

Common workflow friction includes:

- Troubleshooting and CI investigation consuming engineering time
- Repeated debugging and validation work
- Release delays caused by issue investigation bottlenecks
- Difficulty reproducing defects
- Engineers spending time on repetitive test updates instead of higher-value engineering work

**Seller positioning direction:** Position Codex around faster troubleshooting, reduced investigation overhead, and improved engineering workflow throughput.

A seller-level response might sound like:

> "It sounds like the team is losing time after code is written, especially in troubleshooting and validation. Codex may be relevant where engineers need support investigating failures, understanding likely causes, updating tests, or reducing repetitive debugging overhead. The goal is not to remove engineering judgment; it is to support the workflow so engineers can focus on higher-value decisions."

Avoid saying:

> "Codex will guarantee that releases have no bugs."

That overstates the capability and creates credibility risk. A better claim is that Codex can support testing and debugging workflows, while engineers continue to own quality decisions.

## Slide 17/49 - Review and documentation workflows

Review and documentation are often overlooked in AI coding conversations. They are also common bottlenecks in mature engineering organizations.

Review friction matters because code may be written faster than it can be reviewed. Documentation friction matters because missing or outdated documentation slows onboarding, modernization, and cross-team coordination.

Common workflow friction includes:

- Pull request review bottlenecks
- Documentation gaps slowing onboarding and coordination
- Knowledge concentrated with a small number of contributors
- Inconsistent review practices across teams
- Repeated questions about legacy systems or team conventions

**Seller positioning direction:** Position Codex around faster troubleshooting, reduced investigation overhead, and improved engineering workflow throughput.

A seller-level response might sound like:

> "If review and documentation are slowing the team down, Codex may be relevant as support for code understanding, review preparation, documentation updates, and onboarding continuity. This can help reduce repetitive coordination overhead while keeping engineers responsible for approval and final judgment."

Avoid saying:

> "Codex replaces code review."

That is a weak enterprise message. A stronger message is:

> "Codex can support review workflows so human reviewers spend more time on higher-judgment decisions."

## Slide 18/49 - Modernization and maintenance workflows

Modernization and maintenance work can be difficult to move forward because teams are often working inside complex legacy systems while also supporting current delivery demands.

Common workflow friction includes:

- Legacy systems slowing delivery
- Technical debt consuming engineering capacity
- Maintenance-heavy workflows reducing modernization progress
- Limited documentation around older systems
- Dependency on senior engineers who understand historical decisions

**Seller positioning direction:** Position Codex around modernization support, legacy system understanding, and reducing repetitive maintenance overhead.

A seller-level response might sound like:

> "If modernization is slowed by legacy system complexity, Codex may be relevant as support for codebase understanding, documentation, maintenance tasks, and targeted refactoring workflows. The stronger opportunity is not just faster coding; it is helping teams navigate complexity so modernization work can move forward more consistently."

Avoid saying:

> "Codex can modernize the whole system for you."

That is too broad and risky. Stronger positioning focuses on well-scoped modernization and maintenance workflows that still rely on engineering direction, validation, and approval.

## Slide 23/49 - Codex vs traditional coding assistants (Module: Codex vs traditional coding assistants)

Customers may compare Codex with tools they already know: coding copilots, IDE assistants, autocomplete tools, developer chat tools, or existing engineering automation.

That comparison is normal. Many customers already have experience with AI coding assistance, so they may initially place Codex in the same category.

Your job is not to dismiss their current tools. Your job is to move from a tool or feature comparison to the broader software delivery workflow the customer needs to improve.

## Slide 24/49 - How customers may frame the comparison

Customers may initially assume Codex is:

- Another coding copilot
- An autocomplete tool
- A developer chatbot
- A replacement for existing IDE assistance

A weak response would be:

> "Codex is better at writing code."

That response pulls the conversation into a narrow comparison and invites feature-by-feature debate.

A stronger response is:

> "Many teams already use coding assistants. The useful question here is broader: where does software delivery still slow down across planning, implementation, testing, review, debugging, documentation, onboarding, or modernization workflows?"

This reframing matters commercially because customers do not buy enterprise software only because a tool can complete a line of code. They buy when a solution connects to an operational priority, such as release predictability, developer productivity, modernization progress, quality, or engineering throughput.

## Slide 25/49 - Reframing the comparison

Do not assume what an existing coding assistant can or cannot do. Start with the customer's evidence.

Use a workflow comparison instead of a category generalization:

- Which engineering workflow needs to improve?
- What repository, tools, and context does the work require?
- Which surface or integration fits the customer's environment?
- What output or change should result?
- How will the work be reviewed, tested, and approved?

Then position Codex against those criteria:

- Support for multi-step engineering work across the SDLC
- Repository and workflow context
- Surface-specific options across local clients, web or cloud, supported integrations, and programmatic workflows
- Human review, verification, and accountability

Use this reframe:

> "From feature comparison to workflow evaluation."

This reframe keeps the conversation evidence-based without making unsupported claims about another tool.

## Slide 33/49 - Positioning Codex in mixed-tool environments

Many customers already use IDE assistants, developer copilots, ChatGPT, SDLC tooling, internal developer platforms, workflow automation systems, or a mix of all of these.

This does not make Codex irrelevant. It changes how you position it.

Avoid:

- Rip-and-replace messaging
- Positioning Codex as requiring wholesale workflow replacement
- Framing the conversation as a zero-sum tooling decision
- Implying existing tools have no value

Instead:

- Focus on workflow augmentation.
- Position Codex within existing engineering operating environments.
- Reinforce that value often comes from improving how work moves across teams, repositories, workflows, and systems rather than replacing existing tools.
- Emphasize workflow support across planning, implementation, testing, review, debugging, documentation, and maintenance.
- Connect the conversation back to software delivery friction rather than tool displacement.

A seller-level response might sound like:

## Slide 34/49 - Real-world example

A large enterprise software company already uses IDE copilots, internal developer tooling, and workflow automation systems across engineering teams.

Despite this, engineering leadership still reports slow review cycles, repeated debugging effort, inconsistent onboarding, and modernization delays tied to legacy repositories.

A weak seller response would be:

> "Codex can replace those tools with a stronger coding agent."

That response is too combative and too tool-centered.

A stronger response would be:

> "It sounds like the organization already has a mature tooling environment, but there are still workflow bottlenecks across review, debugging, onboarding, and modernization. This is stronger because it:

- Acknowledges the customer's existing environment
- Avoids attacking other tools
- Focuses on workflow friction
- Proposes a credible next step

## Slide 36/49 - When not to position Codex aggressively

Avoid pushing strong Codex positioning when:

- Workflow pain is unclear
- Business impact is weak
- The customer is only exploring AI casually
- Engineering workflow maturity is low
- Stakeholder ownership is missing
- Discovery is still incomplete

In these situations, do not force the pitch. Instead:

- Continue discovery.
- Clarify operational priorities.
- Expand stakeholder understanding.
- Assess workflow maturity.
- Reassess whether Codex is the right path later.

Strong positioning requires strong qualification.

## Slide 40/49 - Engineering leadership

Engineering leaders usually care about delivery throughput, software quality, release predictability, modernization progress, and how engineering capacity is used.

| Quality and reliability | Supporting more consistent engineering workflows and review practices |

Avoid saying:

That statement is too generic and too hard to defend. Engineering leaders are more likely to trust operationally specific language.

## Slide 41/49 - Developer productivity and platform teams

Developer productivity and platform teams often care about workflow consistency, developer experience, toolchain efficiency, standardization, onboarding, and reducing unnecessary interruptions.

## Slide 42/49 - Security and governance stakeholders

Security and governance stakeholders usually care less about productivity claims and more about control, oversight, accountability, review, and operational risk.

| Security or governance priority | Seller positioning direction |

| Review and accountability | Supporting governed review and approval workflows |
| Governed adoption | Aligning with existing operational and engineering practices |
| Operational risk visibility | Maintaining visibility into how engineering workflows operate and evolve |
| Observability and control | Maintaining visibility into AI-assisted work, review processes, workflow activity, and operational improvement over time |

Example positioning statement:

> "Given your priority around oversight, Codex should be evaluated as governed workflow support, not unrestricted autonomy. Avoid saying:

> "Codex can work independently, so engineers do not need to be involved."

That statement creates unnecessary trust risk. Enterprise positioning should reinforce governed delegation, review, accountability, and operational visibility.
