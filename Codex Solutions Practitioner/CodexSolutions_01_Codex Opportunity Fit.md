## Slide 5/43 - Introduction (Module: The new pressure on software delivery)

Software delivery is no longer only an engineering concern. When software delivery slows down, the business can feel it through delayed product launches, missed roadmap commitments, slower modernization, lower release confidence, and reduced responsiveness to customers.

That is why software delivery pressure creates commercial opportunity for Codex conversations.

## Slide 6/43 - Why software delivery pressure matters

Customers may not start by asking about Codex. They may describe the problem in broader terms:

> "Our roadmap keeps slipping."

> "We need engineering to deliver more without simply adding headcount."

> "Technical debt is slowing us down."

> "Testing and review cycles take too long."

> "Our release dates are becoming harder to predict."

These statements are useful signals, but they are not enough to qualify an opportunity by themselves. A seller still needs to understand what is actually slowing delivery, where it appears in the workflow, who owns the problem, and whether the impact is meaningful enough to justify deeper discovery.

For sellers, the strongest Codex conversations are usually not about faster individual coding.

They are about improving how engineering work moves through real software delivery workflows.

As organizations adopt AI-assisted software delivery, the conversation increasingly shifts from individual productivity to workflow transformation: how teams plan, build, review, test, document, maintain, and improve software with the right level of oversight.

Your job in early discovery is to listen for delivery pressure and decide whether it points to a meaningful software delivery improvement opportunity.

## Slide 7/43 - Common sources of delivery friction

Software delivery friction often appears in five patterns. These patterns help you recognize what kind of pressure the customer may be describing before you move into deeper discovery or product positioning.

Use the matrix below as a listening aid.

| Delivery friction pattern | What is happening | Customer language you might hear | Stronger qualification signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Throughput pressure | Engineering teams are struggling to move enough work through the delivery system. | "Our backlog keeps growing." "Roadmap items are slipping." "Teams are busy, but work is not moving fast enough." | The pressure affects roadmap execution, customer commitments, or leadership priorities. |
| Quality pressure | Teams are spending too much time finding, fixing, or preventing defects. | "We keep finding issues late." "Testing takes too long." "Release confidence is low." | The pressure affects reliability, release timing, customer trust, or operational risk. |
| Knowledge pressure | Too much expertise sits with a small group of people, or documentation is not keeping pace with engineering work. | "New engineers take too long to onboard." "Only a few people understand this system." "Documentation is incomplete or outdated." | The pressure slows onboarding, creates dependency on specialist knowledge, or increases delivery risk. |
| Coordination pressure | Work slows down because of handoffs, fragmented tools, unclear ownership, or repeated review cycles. | "Reviews take too long." "There are too many handoffs." "Teams spend too much time coordinating work." | The pressure affects delivery outcomes and maps to a concrete, verifiable software engineering task—not coordination alone. |
| Modernization pressure | Legacy systems, technical debt, or aging infrastructure make new work harder to deliver. | "Technical debt is slowing us down." "Old systems are hard to change safely." "We are spending too much time maintaining instead of building." | Leadership has a clear modernization priority, and engineering teams can describe the specific workflows being slowed by legacy complexity. |

Do not use these patterns as rigid labels. A customer may describe one pressure, but discovery may reveal several at once.

For example, a customer who says, "Release approvals depend on the same few reviewers every week," may be describing coordination pressure, knowledge pressure, and quality pressure at the same time.

When you hear one of these signals, ask:

> "What workflow does this affect most, and what happens when that workflow slows down?"

That question helps move the conversation from a surface-level complaint to qualification evidence.

## Slide 8/43 - Locate the friction in the workflow

Once you hear a possible friction pattern, place it in the customer's software delivery workflow.

This matters because broad labels can hide the real issue.

A customer may say "developer productivity," but the actual friction may sit in review cycles, testing, onboarding, documentation, modernization work, or release coordination.

A simple software delivery workflow may look like this. Use this flow to locate where customer friction appears before deciding whether the opportunity is ready to progress.

When a customer describes pressure, listen for the part of the workflow that slows down.

If the customer says "Roadmap items keep moving," listen for planning friction, review delays, testing delays, release coordination, or unclear dependencies.

If the customer says "Testing takes too long," listen for late defect discovery, manual validation, unclear acceptance criteria, or rework across teams.

If the customer says "Technical debt is slowing us down," listen for system complexity, fragile change processes, missing documentation, or modernization bottlenecks.

At this stage, you do not need to diagnose the customer's toolchain or design a technical solution. You need to understand where work slows down and how that friction affects software delivery outcomes.

A useful seller question is:

> "Where in the workflow does this problem show up most often?"

That question helps move the conversation from a broad label to a specific workflow.

A Codex-fit workflow usually involves recurring software engineering work connected to code, repositories, testing, review, documentation, modernization, maintenance, or development coordination.

Once you locate the friction, make one more seller-level judgment: can the customer translate it into a concrete, scoped software engineering task that Codex could perform?

Stronger fit exists when the required context and tools are accessible, the target artifact or environment is clear, and the customer can explain how the result would be verified.

If the conversation remains at the level of broad coordination or general SDLC friction, continue discovery before recommending a deeper Codex discussion.

## Slide 9/43 - Turn friction into qualification evidence

Software delivery friction becomes commercially meaningful when you can connect it to business impact.

Use this flow to turn surface-level customer language into qualification evidence.

**Example 1: "Our roadmap keeps slipping."**

Customer symptom: Roadmap slippage could point to backlog growth, review delays, testing delays, unclear dependencies, or coordination overhead.

Delivery consequence: Planned work does not move through the SDLC predictably.

Business impact: Test whether delayed delivery affects product launches, customer commitments, revenue opportunities, or market responsiveness.

Next discovery question: A strong seller follow-up would be: "Which workflow most often causes roadmap items to slip?"

**Example 2: "Testing takes too long."**

Customer symptom: Slow testing could point to manual validation, late defect discovery, missing test coverage, unstable environments, or release coordination.

Delivery consequence: Teams lose release confidence or need more time before work can move forward.

Business impact: Test whether testing delays affect release dates, customer commitments, quality targets, or operational risk.

Next discovery question: A strong seller follow-up would be: "When testing takes longer than expected, what does that do to release timing or roadmap commitments?"

This translation step is important because not every engineering complaint is a qualified opportunity.

A customer saying, "Testing takes too long," may describe a local inconvenience.

A customer saying, "Testing delays are causing release dates to move across three product teams," is describing a stronger business-relevant signal.

Before progressing the opportunity, check whether the customer can answer three questions:

1. What workflow is creating the friction?
2. What delivery outcome is affected?
3. Why does this matter to the business now?

If the customer cannot answer those questions yet, the opportunity may still be real, but it likely needs more discovery.

## Slide 10/43 - Seller takeaway: qualify the workflow, not the label

Customers often describe software delivery pain using broad labels: "developer productivity," "technical debt," "slow testing," or "overloaded engineers."

Do not qualify the label. Qualify the workflow behind the label.

Before you progress the opportunity, make sure you can explain:

- What workflow is slowing down.
- Where it appears in the SDLC.
- Whether the problem is recurring.
- What delivery or business outcome is affected.
- What evidence is still missing.
- What concrete software engineering task could be evaluated.
- What context and tools the task requires.
- What artifact or environment is in scope.
- How the customer would verify the result.

The commercial task is to determine whether the symptom represents a meaningful software delivery problem and whether that problem can be translated into a concrete, verifiable task before deeper Codex discovery.

## Slide 13/43 - Introduction (Module: Recognizing strong Codex opportunity signals)

In Module 2, you learned how to recognize software delivery pressure and locate where friction appears in the workflow. The next seller task is different: deciding whether that pressure is strong enough to justify a deeper Codex conversation.

## Slide 14/43 - Why sellers need an opportunity fit lens

Not every delivery complaint is a strong Codex opportunity. A customer may have real engineering frustration, but no business urgency. They may be curious about AI, but unable to name the workflow they want to improve. They may have a quality or modernization concern, but no clear owner or evaluation path.

The Codex Opportunity Fit Lens helps you make that judgment.

Use it after you hear software delivery pressure and before you move into deeper Codex positioning.

The lens helps you answer: "Do we have enough evidence to treat this as a credible Codex opportunity signal?"

It is not a product pitch, a technical design tool, or a deployment checklist. It is a seller qualification checkpoint.

It helps you avoid three common mistakes.

**Treating interest as evidence** — A customer saying, "We're interested in AI coding tools," may be a useful opening. It is not qualification evidence on its own.

**Pitching before the workflow is clear** — If the seller moves straight into product capabilities, the conversation can become a generic tool comparison instead of a customer-specific opportunity discussion.

**Progressing with missing evidence** — If there is no clear pain, business impact, owner, readiness, or repeatable workflow, the seller may be creating weak pipeline.

Use the lens to decide what evidence you have, what evidence is missing, and what you need to ask next.

## Slide 15/43 - The Codex Opportunity Fit Lens

The Codex Opportunity Fit Lens has five checks. Use this lens as a seller checkpoint before progressing from early discovery into deeper Codex positioning.

1. **Recurring pain** — Ask: "Does this problem happen repeatedly?" Look for evidence that the issue appears across teams, workflows, repositories, products, or SDLC stages. A one-time complaint may be worth noting, but a repeated operating pattern is a stronger opportunity signal.

2. **Business impact** — Ask: "What outcome is affected when this workflow slows down?" Look for impact on roadmap delivery, release predictability, quality, modernization, customer experience, engineering capacity, cost, or operational risk.

3. **Stakeholder ownership** — Ask: "Who owns improving this problem?" Look for an engaged engineering, platform, product, developer productivity, AI tooling, or technical leadership stakeholder who can influence workflow change.

4. **Readiness** — Ask: "Can the customer realistically evaluate a better way of working?" Look for whether the customer can describe the current workflow, involve the right stakeholders, make the required context and tools available, define a concrete evaluation task and target, and explain how the result would be reviewed or verified.

5. **Repeatable workflow** — Ask: "What specific software delivery workflow sits behind the request?" Look for recurring work such as review, testing, debugging, onboarding, documentation, modernization, maintenance, or release coordination. Then test whether that workflow can be translated into a concrete, scoped task with accessible context and tools, a defined target artifact or environment, and clear verification criteria.

A customer does not need perfect evidence across all five checks in the first conversation. But the more evidence you have, the more confidently you can judge whether the opportunity deserves deeper Codex discovery.

## Slide 16/43 - Applying the lens to customer language

The lens is most useful when a customer's statement sounds promising, but the opportunity is not fully clear yet.

Consider this customer statement:

> "We're exploring AI for engineering. Our release dates have become less predictable, especially when teams find issues late in validation. The platform team is looking at developer productivity this quarter, but we have not chosen a specific workflow to evaluate."

This is not a fully qualified opportunity yet. But it is more than generic AI curiosity.

**Apply the lens**

Recurring pain: There may be recurring pain because release predictability is affected when issues are found late in validation. The seller still needs to confirm how often this happens and which teams, products, or releases are affected.

Business impact: Release predictability is a business-relevant outcome. The seller should test whether delays affect customer commitments, roadmap delivery, operational risk, or quality targets.

Stakeholder ownership: The platform team is involved, which is a useful ownership signal. The seller still needs to confirm whether the platform team owns the evaluation or only has general interest.

Readiness: Readiness is incomplete. The customer has not defined a concrete evaluation task or confirmed the context, tools, target, and verification approach needed to evaluate it.

Repeatable workflow: There is likely a repeatable workflow, but it still needs to be translated into a bounded task. The seller should determine whether the most useful task is a defined test update, a bounded validation investigation, a review task, or another specific unit of software engineering work—and what inputs, target, and checks it requires.

A strong seller response would be:

> "That sounds like a meaningful delivery issue, especially if late validation is affecting release predictability. To understand fit, I'd like to narrow this to one concrete task. Which validation or review task would be most useful to evaluate first, what context and tools would be available, and how would your team verify the result?"

This response does three things well:

- It acknowledges the business-relevant signal.
- It avoids jumping directly into a Codex pitch.
- It moves the customer from a broad workflow problem toward a concrete, verifiable task.

## Slide 17/43 - Codex-fit boundary: what belongs and what does not

A strong Codex-fit opportunity usually starts with recurring software engineering work where teams need to improve how work is planned, built, reviewed, tested, documented, modernized, or maintained.

Workflow friction alone is not enough; the customer should be able to translate it into a concrete, scoped task that Codex could perform.

The opportunity is stronger when the customer can explain:

- What work is hard to scale
- Where that work slows down
- Why the delay matters
- Who owns improving it
- Whether the team can evaluate a better workflow
- What context and tools the task requires, and whether they are accessible.
- What artifact or environment is in scope.
- How the result will be checked and who will review it.

Broad software delivery pressure alone is not enough.

For example:

> "We need engineering to move faster."

This is too broad.

A more useful opportunity statement would be:

> "Release dates are slipping because late validation issues keep creating rework across three product teams, and engineering leadership wants to improve release predictability."

That statement gives the seller useful discovery evidence, but it is not yet a Codex-fit task. The seller still needs to define the task, required context and tools, target artifact or environment, and verification criteria.

At this stage, stay within the seller-level boundary.

You should identify whether the customer has a repeatable software delivery problem and enough readiness to define a concrete, verifiable evaluation task.

You should not design the implementation, configure governance controls, resolve security architecture questions, or create a deployment plan.

Security, access, governance, and review concerns may affect readiness. Detailed trust and deployment handling belong later in the pathway.

## Slide 18/43 - Pressure-test the signal

Use a small number of targeted questions to test whether the signal is strong enough to continue.

You do not need to ask every question in one conversation. Choose the questions that fill the biggest evidence gaps.

- To test recurring pain: "How often does this happen, and which teams or systems are affected?"
- To test business impact: "What happens to delivery, release timing, or customer commitments when this slows down?"
- To test stakeholder ownership: "Who owns improving this workflow?"
- To test readiness: "What would need to be true for your team to evaluate a better way of working?"
- To test repeatable workflow: "Which workflow should we examine first: review, testing, onboarding, modernization, documentation, or release coordination?"
- To test task viability: "What specific task should we evaluate first, what context and tools would it need, what artifact or environment would it act on, and how would your team verify the result?"
- To test solution direction: "Is the customer trying to improve a software delivery workflow, a broader employee productivity workflow, or an AI-powered product, application, or system?"

This helps you decide whether the evidence points toward a Codex-fit opportunity, another OpenAI direction, or more discovery before any solution is positioned strongly.

These questions help you avoid treating vague interest as qualification evidence. The goal is not to interrogate the customer. The goal is to identify whether the opportunity has enough evidence—and a concrete enough task—to justify deeper discovery.

## Slide 23/43 - Move beyond feature-led conversations

## Slide 24/43 - Use a workflow-led discovery path

A strong discovery conversation does not need dozens of questions. It needs a clear path. Use this sequence to move from broad customer pressure to useful qualification evidence.

1. **Start with the pressure** — First, understand the outcome under pressure. The customer may care about roadmap delivery, release predictability, modernization progress, quality, engineering capacity, or customer-facing commitments. A useful question is: "What delivery outcome is under the most pressure right now?"

2. **Locate the workflow** — Next, identify where that pressure appears in the software delivery workflow. Avoid accepting broad labels such as "developer productivity" or "technical debt" without understanding where the friction happens. A useful question is: "Where in the workflow does this slow down most often?"

3. **Find the repeated pattern** — Then, determine whether the problem is recurring or isolated. Stronger opportunities usually involve a repeated workflow pattern, not a one-off issue. A useful question is: "How often does this happen, and which teams or systems are affected?"

4. **Test the impact** — Now connect the workflow friction to a business or operational consequence. This is where discovery moves from "engineering frustration" to qualification evidence. A useful question is: "What happens to delivery, release timing, or customer commitments when this workflow slows down?"

5. **Identify ownership and readiness** — Finally, understand who owns the problem and whether the customer can realistically evaluate a better way of working. A useful question is: "Who owns improving this workflow, and what would need to be true for your team to evaluate a new approach?"

6. **Test task viability** — Before recommending a deeper Codex discussion, identify a bounded software engineering task, the context and tools it requires, the target artifact or environment, and the verification criteria. A useful question is: "What specific task would be useful to evaluate first, and how would your team verify the result?"

This path keeps discovery seller-first. You are not trying to design a solution. You are trying to understand whether the opportunity has enough workflow clarity, impact, ownership, readiness, and task viability to justify deeper Codex discussion.

## Slide 25/43 - Map the workflow before discussing solutions

Before evaluating potential solutions, understand how the work happens today.

A customer may say: "Testing takes too long." That statement is useful, but it is still too broad.

Slow testing could mean several different things:

- Requirements are unclear before work begins.
- Test environments are unstable.
- Engineers are manually writing or updating tests.
- Defects are found late.
- Review delays are holding up validation.
- Release coordination is creating handoffs and rework.

A seller should not try to solve the problem yet. The better move is to map the workflow.

A useful follow-up is:

> "Can you walk me through the last time this happened?"

That question invites the customer to describe the real workflow. As they answer, listen for how the work moves from start to finish.

You are trying to understand:

- What work was being done.
- Whether the work can be bounded into a concrete task.
- Where the delay appeared.
- Which teams or stakeholders were involved.
- What context and tools the task required.
- What changed because the work took longer than expected.
- What artifact or environment was affected, how the result was checked, and who reviewed it.

For example, if the customer says testing takes too long, the workflow map may reveal that testing itself is not the only issue.

The real bottleneck may be late requirements changes, missing test coverage, unclear service ownership, or review delays before release.

This matters because a Codex opportunity should be anchored in the actual workflow and a concrete task—not the first label the customer uses.

At this stage, stay focused on understanding how work moves. Do not diagnose the customer's toolchain, design the implementation, or turn the conversation into a product demo.

## Slide 26/43 - Stakeholders to listen for

Different stakeholders reveal different parts of the opportunity. Strong discovery often requires more than one view.

A product leader may describe roadmap impact. Engineering may explain why the work slows down. Platform engineering may identify workflow standardization needs. Security may clarify review expectations or readiness concerns.

Use stakeholder conversations to triangulate the opportunity.

| Stakeholder | What they usually reveal | Why it matters for qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engineering leadership | Delivery pressure, capacity constraints, quality concerns, modernization priorities. | Shows whether the issue is visible and important enough to act on. |
| Platform or developer productivity | Workflow standardization, tooling strategy, developer experience, evaluation ownership. | Shows whether there may be an owner for improving the workflow. |
| Product or delivery leadership | Roadmap impact, release timing, customer commitments, business priorities. | Connects engineering friction to business outcomes. |
| Developers and technical contributors | Where work actually slows down, what context is missing, which tasks repeat. | Grounds discovery in day-to-day workflow reality. |
| Security or governance | Review expectations, approval concerns, access questions, oversight needs. | Reveals readiness signals and potential specialist follow-up needs. |

Do not treat every stakeholder concern as something you must solve in the first conversation.

For this course, security and governance concerns should be treated as readiness signals.

If the customer raises detailed questions about access, oversight, approvals, or deployment architecture, identify the concern and route it to the right technical or trust-focused follow-up.

Your seller-level task is to understand whether those concerns affect opportunity readiness, stakeholder alignment, or next steps.

## Slide 27/43 - Build a discovery evidence snapshot

Strong discovery should produce more than notes. It should produce a clear view of what is known, what is inferred, what is missing, and what the seller should do next. After a discovery conversation, avoid writing only a long summary. Instead, create a short discovery evidence snapshot.

A good snapshot separates four things:

- **Confirmed evidence** — What did the customer actually say?
- **Working hypothesis** — What do you think may be causing the issue?
- **Qualification implication** — What does this suggest about opportunity fit, readiness, or next steps?
- **Evidence gap** — What still needs to be confirmed before the opportunity can progress?

This structure helps you avoid overstating the opportunity. It also makes your next discovery step more precise.

**Example**

The customer says:

> "Our releases are becoming harder to predict. Teams often find issues late in validation, and release managers say too much work is being coordinated manually across product, engineering, and QA. We know this is slowing us down, but we have not isolated the main bottleneck yet."

A seller could capture the discovery evidence this way:

**Confirmed evidence** — Release predictability is under pressure. The customer is seeing late validation issues and manual coordination across product, engineering, and QA.

**Working hypothesis** — The friction may sit in testing, validation, release coordination, or handoffs between teams.

**Qualification implication** — This is a meaningful software delivery discovery thread, but it is not yet a Codex-fit task. Manual coordination across teams must be translated into executable software engineering work before Codex is positioned.

**Evidence gap** — The seller still needs to confirm which concrete task creates or resolves the most delay, what context and tools are accessible, what artifact or environment is in scope, who owns it, and how the result would be verified.

The next seller question should be:

> "Which concrete task in the release process would be most useful to evaluate first, and what would your team use to verify the result?"

This turns a broad customer statement into a focused discovery path.

The qualification implication is that this is a meaningful software delivery discovery thread, but it is not yet a Codex-fit task because no bounded unit of work has been defined. A readiness signal is also present: the customer has review and oversight questions that may need to be routed to the right technical or security stakeholder before deeper evaluation.

The biggest evidence gap is the concrete task to evaluate first. The seller should clarify whether a bounded validation, review, test-update, or investigation task creates the greatest value, what context and tools are available, and how the result would be verified.

Strong follow-up questions would be:

- "Which part of the release process most often causes customer-facing updates to move?"
- "How often do late validation issues create rework, and which teams are most affected?"
- "Which bounded task should be evaluated first, what context and tools are available, and how would the result be verified?"

## Slide 30/43 - Summary

Workflow-led discovery helps you move from customer symptoms to seller-useful evidence.

Start with the outcome under pressure, locate the workflow, confirm whether the problem repeats, test the impact, identify ownership and readiness, and determine whether the workflow can be translated into a concrete, verifiable task.

A useful discovery output is not a product recommendation yet. It is an evidence snapshot that explains the workflow pain, affected SDLC stages, stakeholder priorities, business impact, readiness signals, task viability, and missing evidence. That evidence becomes the basis for a stronger progression decision in the next module.

## Slide 31/43 - Introduction (Module: Evaluating readiness and progression)

Once discovery evidence is available, the seller's job is to decide what should happen next. A customer can have real engineering pain and still not be ready for a deeper Codex discussion.

In this module, you will practice making progression decisions based on evidence. You will distinguish opportunities that should progress from opportunities that need more discovery, should pause, or should be disqualified.

## Slide 32/43 - Progress when evidence is strong

By this point, you have learned how to recognize delivery pressure, test opportunity signals, and use discovery to gather evidence. The next seller task is deciding what to do with the opportunity.

An opportunity is ready to progress when the evidence is strong enough to justify a deeper Codex conversation.

That does not mean the customer is ready for implementation planning. It means the seller has enough confidence that the opportunity is real, relevant, and worth advancing.

Progression is usually appropriate when:

- The workflow pain is recurring, not isolated.
- The business or operational impact is visible.
- A credible stakeholder owns or is actively investigating the problem.
- The customer can translate the workflow into a concrete, scoped software engineering task for deeper discussion.
- The required context and tools are accessible or can be validated with the right specialist.
- The target artifact or environment and verification criteria are clear enough for a credible next step.
- The customer is open to evaluating a better way of working.
- Any security, governance, or review concerns are identified and can be routed to the right follow-up.

**A strong progression recommendation should answer five questions:** *(click-to-complete items, all completed)*

1. What is the workflow pain?
2. Why does it matter?
3. Who owns the problem?
4. Why now?
5. What should happen next?

For example:

> "The customer is seeing recurring late-validation issues that affect release predictability for a customer-facing product. Product leadership and engineering leadership both recognize the impact. The platform team has identified a bounded validation task, can provide the relevant repository and test context, and will use agreed checks and human review to verify the result. The next step is a deeper Codex discussion with the appropriate technical stakeholder."

That is a progression-ready summary because it connects pain, impact, ownership, a concrete task, accessible context, verification, and the next step.

## Slide 33/43 - Recognize seller-level readiness signals

Sellers do not need to design the technical approach in this course. However, sellers should recognize whether the customer is ready enough for a credible Codex discussion.

Before progressing deeper Codex conversations, confirm whether:

- A concrete, scoped software engineering task exists for evaluation.
- The customer can provide the repository, ticket, documentation, test, or other context the task requires, together with the necessary tools or systems.
- The target artifact or environment is defined.
- The verification criteria are clear, including who reviews the result and what checks must pass.
- Relevant engineering, platform, or security stakeholders can support the evaluation, and any access or governance questions are identified for specialist follow-up.

You can think about seller-level readiness using three simple task-readiness questions.

**Task** — what should Codex do? Can the customer describe the workflow, codebase context, repositories, tickets, documentation, or engineering knowledge involved?

**Context and tools** — what does the task need? Can the customer identify the repositories, tickets, documentation, tests, systems, or tools required—and whether they can be made available?

**Target and verification** — what will be produced or changed, and how will it be checked? Can the customer define the target artifact or environment, the checks that must pass, the human reviewer, and any concerns that need specialist follow-up?

These questions keep the discussion at the seller level. They help you identify readiness and next-step needs without moving into deployment design.

## Slide 34/43 - Continue discovery when evidence is promising but incomplete

Some opportunities are real, but not ready to progress. Continue discovery when the customer describes meaningful pain, but the evidence is not strong enough to recommend a deeper Codex conversation yet.

This often happens when:

- The pain is credible, but the workflow or concrete task is still vague.
- The business impact is suspected, but not confirmed.
- The right stakeholders are interested, but ownership is unclear.
- The customer has urgency, but no concrete evaluation task with confirmed context, tools, target, and verification criteria.
- Security, governance, access, or review concerns exist, but have not been routed to the right stakeholders.

Further discovery is not a failure. It is how you avoid weak pipeline and poorly scoped Codex conversations.

A useful seller response is:

> "It sounds like the pain is real, but I want to make sure we qualify the right task. Which part of delivery creates the greatest repeated friction, what bounded task could we evaluate, and who would verify the result?"

Continue discovery when the opportunity has enough signal to investigate, but not enough evidence to progress confidently.

## Slide 35/43 - Pause or disqualify when fit is weak

Some opportunities should not progress. A pause decision is appropriate when interest exists, but operational need or readiness is weak. The customer may be curious about AI, but not yet ready to define a software delivery priority or evaluate workflow change.

**Pause when:** *(click-to-complete items, all completed)*

- Interest is exploratory rather than operational.
- There is no clear urgency.
- The customer cannot identify a concrete software engineering task to investigate.
- No stakeholder owns workflow improvement.
- The customer is not prepared to change how work gets done.

A pause decision can still preserve the relationship. It simply means the opportunity is not ready for deeper Codex discussion yet.

A useful seller response is:

> "It sounds like there is interest in AI-assisted engineering, but we may need a clearer task and owner before we can evaluate fit. Let's identify the software delivery priority, the bounded task that matters most, the context it requires, and how the result would be verified."

A disqualifying decision is appropriate when the problem is not connected to software delivery improvement, or there is no meaningful pain, owner, urgency, business impact, repeatable workflow, or concrete task that Codex could perform and the customer could verify.

Disqualifying is not negative. It protects sales focus and prevents the team from forcing Codex into a problem it is not suited to solve.

## Slide 36/43 - Progression decision logic

Use qualification evidence to decide whether the opportunity should progress, continue discovery, pause, or be disqualified.

**Progress** — Use this when pain is recurring, business impact is credible, an owner is engaged, and the customer can define a concrete task with accessible context and tools, a clear target, and verification criteria.

**Continue discovery** — Use this when pain appears real, but business impact, ownership, stakeholder alignment, workflow clarity, task viability, or readiness is not yet clear enough to progress confidently.

**Pause** — Use this when interest exists, but operational need, urgency, or readiness for workflow change is weak.

**Disqualify** — Use this when the problem is not connected to software delivery improvement, or there is no meaningful pain, owner, urgency, business impact, repeatable workflow, or concrete, verifiable task.

Use this decision path to make a clear recommendation after early discovery.

Not every non-Codex direction is a bad opportunity. If the customer has a real business problem but the primary need is not software delivery work, do not force Codex into the conversation.

Broad employee productivity or knowledge-work needs may point toward ChatGPT.

A need to embed intelligence into a product, application, system, or customer experience may point toward the API.

If the problem is still unclear, continue discovery before positioning any OpenAI path strongly.

A good progression decision should be evidence-based. Avoid progressing because the customer is curious, because the account is strategic, or because a demo has been requested. Progress when the workflow pain, business impact, ownership, task viability, and readiness are credible enough to justify deeper Codex discussion.

## Slide 37/43 - Real-world example: quality pressure with unclear readiness

A digital payments company is under pressure to improve release confidence for a customer-facing payments platform.

Engineering teams describe recurring late-stage defects, manual validation work, and release coordination issues.

Product leadership says several customer-facing improvements have moved because teams needed more time to validate changes before release.

The pain sounds real, and it appears connected to quality pressure and release predictability. However, readiness is still unclear.

The seller does not yet know:

- Which workflow creates the greatest delay: test coverage, defect triage, review, or release coordination.
- Whether a specific engineering or platform owner is accountable for improving the workflow.
- Whether leadership is committed to changing how the work gets done.
- How AI-assisted work would be reviewed or validated.
- What context and tools would be available to the task.
- What artifact or environment is in scope and which checks must pass.

In this case, the best progression decision is likely to continue discovery.

The seller should determine:

- Which workflow is creating the most repeated friction.
- Whether the pain affects release dates, customer commitments, or operational risk.
- Which stakeholder owns the improvement effort.
- Whether the customer can define a concrete evaluation task with accessible context and tools.
- What target artifact or environment is in scope and how the result will be verified.
- Whether review, oversight, or governance concerns require specialist follow-up.

This keeps the conversation commercially grounded and prevents the seller from advancing an opportunity based only on broad delivery pressure.
