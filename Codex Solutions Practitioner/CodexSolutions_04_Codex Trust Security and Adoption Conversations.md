## Slide 7/37 - Why enterprise buyers evaluate more than coding capability

Most enterprise Codex conversations start with a productivity question: can Codex help engineers move faster? But as the conversation progresses, it often shifts to a trust question: can this be trusted to operate inside how we already build, review, and ship software?

Customers may worry about:

- Losing operational oversight
- Disrupting established workflows
- Unclear accountability for AI-assisted changes
- Unmanaged or ungoverned AI usage
- Exposure to software quality issues
- Limited visibility into what AI is doing
- Uncertainty about access and permissions
- Difficulty monitoring how AI usage evolves over time

Your job is not to resolve every one of these concerns yourself. It's to recognize when a conversation has shifted from productivity to trust, and to frame Codex as governed engineering acceleration: AI-assisted work that operates with appropriate oversight, review, and accountability, rather than unrestricted AI autonomy.

**The trust conversation lens**

| Lens | What it means | What it helps you listen for |
|---|---|---|
| Context | What Codex can know, see, or use | Questions about scope, data access, and system boundaries |
| Agentic Runtime | What Codex can do, where it works, and how work is reviewed | Questions about autonomy, review gates, and human accountability |
| Observability | How activity is monitored, measured, controlled, or improved | Questions about visibility, auditability, and usage tracking |

**How to use the lens**

When a customer raises a trust concern, pause before responding and ask yourself:

> "What kind of concern is this really?"

Use the lens to classify the concern:

- If the customer asks what Codex can know, see, or use, treat it as a context concern.
- If the customer asks what Codex can do, where it works, or how work is reviewed, treat it as an agentic runtime concern.
- If the customer asks how activity is monitored, measured, controlled, or improved, treat it as an observability concern.

Then respond at the right level:

1. Acknowledge the concern. ✓
2. Connect the concern to governed engineering acceleration. ✓
3. Keep the conversation focused on evaluation confidence. ✓
4. Bring in the right technical specialist when the customer needs implementation-level detail. ✓

**For example:**

| Customer question | What the question is mostly about | Seller response focus |
|---|---|---|
| "What repositories or systems would be in scope?" | Context | Clarify that scope, permissions, and access expectations need to be part of the evaluation and validated with the right technical stakeholders |
| "Can Codex make changes without review?" | Agentic runtime | Reinforce that Codex should be positioned around delegated engineering support with clear review expectations and human accountability |
| "How will we know what teams are using it for?" | Observability | Acknowledge the need for visibility into usage, activity, outcomes, and adoption signals before broader rollout |

This lens helps you avoid two common mistakes: answering trust concerns with generic productivity claims, or moving too quickly into technical deployment detail before the customer's operating concern is clear.

The diagram below shows a simple way to organize trust concerns before deciding how to respond:

- **Context** — What information or systems are in scope?
- **Agentic Runtime** — How is work delegated and reviewed?
- **Observability** — How will activity and outcomes be monitored?

## Slide 8/37 - The shift from coding assistance to delegated engineering work

AI-assisted software development is evolving beyond isolated code suggestions. In many customer conversations, the shift is from code completion, to pair programming, to delegated engineering support across parts of the software development lifecycle.

That shift changes the trust conversation.

When AI is framed as a coding helper, customers may focus on developer preference, code quality, or speed.

When AI is framed as supporting multi-step engineering work, customers naturally ask more operational questions:

- What work can be delegated?
- Which systems or information may be accessed?
- How does review responsibility remain clear?
- What are teams expected to approve or inspect?
- How is activity monitored?
- What happens before AI-assisted work affects customers, systems, or business outcomes?

These are reasonable questions. They do not mean the customer is resisting AI. They often mean the customer is taking the evaluation seriously.

The seller's job is to avoid autonomy-heavy positioning and keep the conversation grounded in controlled delegation, operational review, and engineering accountability.

Codex should be positioned as part of how teams delegate and complete engineering work through agents, with the learning focus anchored in software delivery, SDLC transformation, engineering workflows, and governed agentic delegation.

## Slide 9/37 - Recognizing what stakeholders are actually worried about

Once you understand what kind of trust concern is being raised, the next step is to understand why it matters to the person asking.

This is where a second lens is useful: the stakeholder concern lens.

- **Trust conversation lens** — Helps you identify what the concern is about: context, agentic runtime, or observability.
- **Stakeholder concern lens** — Helps you identify who needs confidence and what kind of confidence they need.

This matters because different stakeholders can ask similar questions for different reasons.

For example, the question...

> "What can Codex access?"

...may mean different things depending on who asks it:

A security stakeholder may be asking about control, permissions, and policy alignment. An engineering leader may be asking whether Codex can work with the right repository context without disrupting team practices. An executive may be asking whether rollout risk is manageable.

The words may be similar, but the concern underneath is different. A strong seller response addresses the concern behind the question, not just the wording of the question.

**Why we're using a stakeholder concern lens**

Enterprise Codex opportunities often involve multiple stakeholders. Security, engineering, and executive stakeholders may all support the idea of engineering acceleration, but each group evaluates risk differently.

Use the stakeholder concern lens to avoid treating every objection as a technical blocker. Many objections are actually confidence gaps.

The customer may need confidence that adoption can be governed, that engineering accountability will remain clear, or that rollout can happen without creating operational disruption.

This lens is not a set of stereotypes. It is a practical way to listen more carefully and shape your response for the stakeholder in front of you.

**How to use the stakeholder concern lens**

When a concern comes up, ask three questions:

1. Who is raising the concern?
2. What consequence are they trying to avoid?
3. What confidence do they need before the opportunity can move forward?

| Stakeholder | They usually need confidence in | What to listen for | Strong seller response focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security stakeholders | Control, oversight, review expectations, and responsible workflow governance | Questions about access, permissions, monitoring, policy alignment, uncontrolled changes, or auditability | Acknowledge the concern, reinforce governed evaluation, and involve technical specialists for detailed validation |
| Engineering leaders | Software quality, review accountability, and operational consistency across engineering workflows | Questions about who owns outputs, how reviews work, whether standards are followed, and how teams avoid inconsistent practices | Position Codex as support for engineering workflows, not a replacement for engineering judgment or review |
| Executives | Adoption realism, rollout feasibility, business impact, and organizational readiness | Questions about value, pace of rollout, team adoption, risk, and whether the investment will scale | Recommend a focused evaluation path with clear value signals, stakeholder alignment, and phased adoption |

**Using both lenses together**

The two lenses work together. Use the trust conversation lens to understand what the concern is about. Use the stakeholder concern lens to understand why the concern matters and what confidence the stakeholder needs next.

For example, a security stakeholder asks:

> "How do we know Codex will not create uncontrolled production changes?"

The trust conversation lens tells you this is mainly an agentic runtime and observability concern. The stakeholder concern lens tells you the stakeholder needs confidence in control, review expectations, and operational oversight.

A strong seller response might be:

> "That concern makes sense, especially if you are evaluating Codex beyond a small team pilot. The responsible approach is not to frame Codex as uncontrolled autonomy. It is to evaluate a focused workflow where review responsibility, controlled execution expectations, and visibility requirements are clear. We should align on the workflow first, then bring in the technical team to validate the access, monitoring, and configuration details with your environment."

This response works because it does four things:

1. It acknowledges the stakeholder's concern. ✓
2. It avoids autonomy-heavy positioning. ✓
3. It keeps the conversation grounded in governed engineering acceleration. ✓
4. It creates a next step without pretending the seller owns deployment design. ✓

## Slide 14/37 - Positioning governed acceleration instead of unrestricted autonomy

Enterprise confidence depends on the way Codex is positioned. If a seller uses language that suggests "hands-free engineering" or broad AI autonomy, stakeholders may hear reduced control, unclear accountability, or unmanaged workflow change.

A stronger positioning theme is governed acceleration.

In this course, governed acceleration means helping engineering teams move work through the SDLC more effectively while keeping human review, organizational ownership, and operational accountability clear.

This phrase is useful because it gives sellers a safer alternative to autonomy-heavy language. It lets you speak to speed and transformation without implying that AI replaces engineering judgment or removes governance responsibility.

Enterprise-ready positioning consistently reinforces:

- Organizational control
- Operational boundaries
- Review expectations
- Governed workflows
- Human accountability
- Visibility into the work being performed
- Clear review and monitoring expectations

**A simple positioning pattern**

Use this pattern when a customer concern could become a trust blocker:

> Customer concern → affected workflow or use case → operating principle → Codex value → next step

Naming the affected workflow keeps the trust response grounded. It prevents the conversation from becoming an abstract governance discussion and helps the seller connect oversight, accountability, and phased evaluation to the real engineering work the customer wants to improve.

This pattern is not meant to make conversations scripted. It helps you respond in the right order:

1. Start with the concern so the stakeholder feels heard. ✓
2. State the operating principle that addresses the concern. ✓
3. Connect Codex to workflow value without exaggerating autonomy. ✓
4. Recommend a practical next step that advances evaluation. ✓

**Example:**

> "That concern is exactly why we recommend framing Codex as governed engineering acceleration. The goal is not to remove engineering ownership. It is to help teams move faster across the SDLC while maintaining review responsibility and operational control. A good next step is identifying one workflow where the team can evaluate value and oversight expectations together."

## Slide 15/37 - Positioning oversight naturally without creating friction

Governance conversations become harder when sellers treat them defensively or over-explain technical controls too early.

Enterprise customers expect oversight conversations as part of responsible AI adoption. The goal is to make oversight feel natural, practical, and compatible with engineering acceleration.

At the seller level, oversight can be discussed through operating principles such as:

- Human review
- Review checkpoints
- Controlled execution
- Organizational accountability
- Visibility into activity and outcomes

You do not need to prove the full technical governance model in the first sales response. Instead, acknowledge that oversight is a valid evaluation requirement and connect it to the customer's workflow.

For example:

> "Codex helps engineering teams accelerate work across the SDLC while organizations maintain operational ownership and review responsibility."

This kind of language does three things well. It keeps the value conversation alive, reassures stakeholders that accountability is not being removed, and creates a path to involve technical specialists when detailed configuration, permissions, or monitoring questions need to be validated.

## Slide 22/37 - The enterprise concerns sellers hear most often

Trust conversations often surface through direct questions. These questions may sound technical, but they usually signal that the customer is evaluating whether adoption can happen responsibly.

Common questions include:

- "What can Codex access?"
- "How do permissions work?"

Do not dismiss these questions or treat them as blockers. They are legitimate enterprise buying questions. They show that the customer is thinking about operational safety, governance confidence, rollout realism, organizational readiness, and whether the workflow being evaluated can be adopted responsibly.

Here is how to interpret the concern:

| Customer question | What the stakeholder may be evaluating |
|---|---|
| "What can Codex access?" | Whether context, systems, repositories, or information access can be bounded and explained |
| "How do permissions work?" | Whether approval expectations and access decisions can be validated by the right experts |
| "How do we avoid unsafe changes?" | Whether review responsibility and controlled execution expectations remain clear |
| "How do we monitor activity?" | Whether the organization has sufficient visibility to evaluate usage, outcomes, and policy alignment |
| "Who reviews outputs?" | Whether engineering accountability remains intact |
| "How do we know workflows remain aligned with policy?" | Whether adoption can scale without losing organizational control |

**Seller-level facts to anchor the conversation**

Start by identifying which Codex surface and configuration the customer is evaluating.

Local use—through the ChatGPT desktop app, Codex CLI, or IDE extension—is governed by the active workspace or working directory, the sandbox, network settings, and the approval policy.

In Auto, Codex can read, edit, and run commands inside the permitted workspace; actions outside that boundary or requiring network access may require approval, depending on policy.

Codex cloud runs work in isolated OpenAI-managed environments connected to repositories.

Workspace access, environment settings, source-system repository permissions and protections, secrets, internet access, and plan-dependent administrator controls shape what Codex can do. Workspace access does not bypass repository permissions.

Human review remains part of the operating model.

In GitHub workflows, Codex can post reviews and, when permitted, propose or push fixes, but the customer's branch protections, merge approvals, and deployment gates still apply.

Do not promise one universal monitoring or data-handling answer. Available analytics, audit records, review evidence, retention, and data controls vary by surface, plan, configuration, connected systems, and contract.

Validate current details with the appropriate technical specialists, workspace administrators, security stakeholders, or legal stakeholders.

These are seller-level boundaries—not implementation instructions.

## Slide 23/37 - A seller-level response framework

Use a seller-level response framework when governance or permission concerns arise. The purpose of this framework is to keep the conversation credible and moving without pretending that the seller owns deployment configuration or technical governance design.

**Step 1: Acknowledge the concern.**

Governance questions are legitimate operational questions. A strong response starts by showing the stakeholder that the concern is understood.

Example:

> "That is a reasonable question, especially if the team is evaluating Codex beyond an individual developer workflow."

**Step 2: Connect the concern to the workflow and reinforce operational principles.**

Bring the concern back to the workflow or use case being evaluated, then reinforce governed acceleration, operational oversight, review expectations, engineering accountability, visibility into activity, and responsible operational control.

Example:

> "The evaluation should name the workflow and Codex surface. It should then clarify the relevant workspace or repository boundaries, approval behavior, human review, available activity or audit evidence, and merge or deployment gates before the workflow expands."

**Step 3: Keep the conversation commercially grounded.**

Avoid moving too early into architecture diagrams, authentication design, deployment controls, or detailed implementation. Those topics may matter, but they require the right technical participants.

Keep the seller conversation focused on:

- Rollout confidence
- Stakeholder alignment
- Operational realism
- Phased adoption

**Step 4: Escalate technical detail appropriately.**

When customers require implementation-level answers, involve technical specialists. This protects credibility and helps the customer get accurate detail.

Example:

> "I can help define the workflow, Codex surface, and trust questions for the evaluation. Technical specialists and workspace administrators can then work with your team to validate the implementation details, including scope, environment, network and approval policies, available activity, audit, and review evidence, data handling, and merge or deployment gates."

## Slide 24/37 - Governance positioning versus deployment governance

A common seller mistake is to blur two different conversations: governance positioning and deployment governance.

**Governance positioning**

This is seller-level. It answers:

> "How should we talk about trust, oversight, and adoption so the opportunity can progress responsibly?"

**Deployment governance**

This is implementation-level. It answers:

> "How will the customer configure and validate the selected Codex surface, access boundaries, network and approval policy, monitoring and data-handling controls, repository protections, and merge or deployment gates?"

Both matter. They just belong at different depths.

At the seller level, you can and should explain that controls differ by surface and configuration, then discuss operating principles: human review, controlled evaluation, clear ownership, visibility expectations, required merge or deployment gates, stakeholder alignment, and phased rollout.

You should not invent configuration details, make claims about specific controls, or design the governance model in the sales conversation.

This boundary helps maintain credibility. It also helps customers understand that trust is not being ignored; it is being handled at the right level with the right people.

## Slide 29/37 - Why adoption confidence shapes enterprise buying decisions

Enterprise customers are not only evaluating whether Codex works technically. They are evaluating whether AI-assisted engineering work can become part of everyday engineering operations without creating unacceptable risk or confusion.

Adoption confidence includes several questions:

- Will developers realistically adopt this?
- Can workflows evolve responsibly?
- Can the organization maintain visibility, accountability, and operational confidence as AI-assisted work becomes part of engineering processes?
- Can organizational resistance be managed?
- Does rollout risk feel acceptable?

Executive stakeholders rarely fund large-scale workflow transformation based on technical capability alone.

They need confidence that adoption can happen gradually, that the first use case is meaningful, and that operational risk can remain manageable as the evaluation expands.

That is why dramatic transformation narratives can backfire.

A more credible executive conversation positions Codex around practical workflow integration, governed rollout, and engineering collaboration.

The partner role is important here. Partners help customers move from interest to impact by diagnosing priority workflows, identifying where intelligence should be embedded, shaping the right solution path, planning governance and adoption, measuring value, and scaling from pilots toward production when the organization is ready.

For this course, keep that conversation at the readiness and trust level. You are not creating the deployment plan. You are helping the customer define the right evaluation path and the right confidence questions before deeper technical planning begins.

## Slide 30/37 - Positioning phased adoption instead of "full transformation"

Organization-wide transformation narratives often increase stakeholder anxiety. Enterprise buyers know that workflow change is rarely immediate or frictionless.

A better sales conversation starts with a focused evaluation path:

1. One priority workflow or use case with clear business value ✓
2. One pilot team or champion group ✓
3. Clear human review and any required merge approvals or deployment gates ✓
4. A small set of stakeholder confidence questions to validate before expansion ✓

This phased approach is not a lack of ambition. It is how enterprise buyers build confidence before scaling.

At seller level, phased adoption means helping the customer answer:

- Which workflow matters enough to evaluate first?
- Which team is best positioned to test it responsibly?
- Which stakeholders need alignment?
- What value signal would make the evaluation meaningful?
- What trust or readiness questions must be answered before broader adoption?

Use this response pattern when an executive wants acceleration and a security stakeholder wants more confidence:

> "Let's start with a focused workflow where the business value is clear and the oversight expectations can be evaluated with the right stakeholders. That gives the organization a practical way to validate value, adoption readiness, and operational confidence before making broader rollout decisions."

Detailed rollout planning should involve the appropriate technical specialists, workspace administrators, security stakeholders, and deployment teams. They can validate surface-specific requirements for access, environments, network and approval policies, monitoring, data handling, and governance.

Use these redirects when the conversation becomes too feature-led:

| Feature-led question | Better enterprise evaluation question |
