## Slide 6/50 - Why more findings do not equal more protection (Module: Why cyber is becoming a tempo problem)

Finding a possible vulnerability is only the beginning. A scanner alert, bug-bounty report, penetration-test result, dependency warning, or researcher lead creates potential value. It does not protect the organization by itself.

To reduce risk, the customer still needs to answer questions like:

- Is the finding real in this environment?
- Is the affected code reachable?
- How severe is the issue in the customer's actual context?
- What system, repository, service, or team owns the issue?
- What remediation is safe?
- What tests or review steps are needed?
- What evidence should be retained?
- Where should that evidence be recorded?

This is why more findings can sometimes increase pressure instead of reducing risk. If a customer receives more alerts than the team can validate, prioritize, remediate, and prove, the backlog grows.

Engineering teams may lose confidence. Security teams may spend more time on triage than on risk reduction. Leadership may see activity without a clear view of what risk was actually reduced.

A better positioning message is:

> Security protection improves when teams can validate what matters, prioritize the right work, remediate safely, and produce evidence of what changed.

That is the workflow pressure Daybreak is designed to address.

## Slide 7/50 - Where traditional workflows slow down (Module: Why cyber is becoming a tempo problem)

Many customer security workflows slow down between signal and action. This is rarely just one step. It often appears across several types of latency.

**Validation latency**

Happens when teams struggle to decide whether a finding is real, reachable, exploitable, relevant, or important. This can happen when findings lack context, when multiple tools disagree, or when reviewers need scarce expertise to interpret the result.

**Remediation latency**

Happens when engineering teams need safe, reviewable fixes that preserve expected behavior. Even when a vulnerability is real, a proposed fix still needs to be reviewed, tested, accepted, and safely merged into the customer's development process.

**Evidence latency**

Happens when teams need documentation, proof, reviewer notes, and system-of-record updates to show that the issue was reviewed and addressed. Without evidence, it is hard to communicate progress to leadership, auditors, governance teams, or other stakeholders.

**Handoff latency**

Happens when security, engineering, compliance, and leadership teams work from different tools, priorities, and definitions of success.

| Team | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| AppSec (application security) teams | Focus on whether a finding is real, exploitable, and important enough to send to engineering. |
| Engineering | Focuses on whether the fix is safe and preserves intended behavior. Leadership focuses on exposure and proof of risk reduction. Governance teams focus on auditability and control. |

## Slide 13/50 - From signal to reviewed action (Module: The defensive workflow Daybreak is built around)

Security teams reduce risk through a chain of work. Daybreak positioning should connect to that chain.

The defensive workflow is:

**Find > Validate > Test > Remediate > Prove**

Use this flow to see why Daybreak positioning should focus on the full path from security signal to reviewable action, not only on finding more issues.

Each stage has a different purpose:

**Find**

A potential issue surfaces. It may come from a scanner alert, code change, dependency warning, vulnerability class, external report, penetration-test result, bug-bounty report, or researcher lead.

**Validate**

The team determines whether the issue is real, reachable, exploitable, relevant, and important in the customer's environment. This is where many teams spend significant time separating signal from noise.

**Test**

The team confirms behavior safely through approved methods. Depending on the customer's environment and rules of engagement, this may involve reproduction steps, unit tests, integration checks, regression boundaries, or other approved validation methods.

**Remediate**

The team applies a patch, dependency update, configuration change, mitigation, workaround, or other corrective action. Remediation still needs engineering review and acceptance.

**Prove**

The team creates evidence that the issue was reviewed, addressed, accepted, and recorded in the right system. Proof may include reviewer notes, test results, pull request records, ticket updates, risk decisions, or other evidence the customer's governance process requires.

This workflow helps partners avoid vague statements like AI can help with cyber. Instead, you can ask: where is the customer stuck between finding and proving?

## Slide 20/50 - The three layers of Daybreak positioning (Module: What Daybreak is)

You can explain Daybreak through three connected layers.

This stack shows why Daybreak should not be described as a standalone cyber model.

**Layer 1: Model reasoning**

Models help reason across code, security context, complex systems, vulnerability patterns, and remediation paths.

In a defensive cyber workflow, reasoning matters because security work is rarely just pattern matching.

Reviewers need to understand context: where code is used, whether a finding is reachable, which controls exist, what impact is plausible, and what evidence supports the conclusion.

**Layer 2: Codex as the harness**

Codex brings model reasoning into engineering workflows where tools, tests, files, repositories, and human review matter.

This matters because defensive cyber work often happens inside software delivery workflows.

Findings may connect to code, pull requests, tests, dependency files, reports, tickets, or release processes. Codex provides the agentic work layer where model reasoning can interact with that engineering context under defined boundaries.

**Layer 3: Codex Security workflows**

Codex Security packages repeatable security workflows for scanning, validation, investigation, reporting, remediation support, and evidence generation.

At a positioning level, think of Codex Security as the workflow-native layer that helps security teams move from raw signal toward contextual risk, validated evidence, and reviewable remediation.

**How this connects to enterprise operating model language.**

You can also describe Daybreak using three enterprise operating-model ideas:

**Context** — What the AI is allowed to know or use, such as repositories, findings, policies, tests, system documentation, and customer security context.

**Agentic runtime** — Where and how work happens, such as Codex and Codex Security operating within approved workflows, tools, repositories, and review surfaces.

**Observability** — How the customer monitors, reviews, records, and improves the work, including evidence, logs, approvals, reviewer decisions, and systems of record.

This layered explanation prevents a common mistake: reducing Daybreak to a cyber model. Model capability matters, but it becomes operationally useful when paired with tools, context, governance, evidence, and human review.

## Slide 22/50 - What Daybreak is not (Module: What Daybreak is)

Clear positioning includes clear guardrails.

Daybreak is not:

- A standalone model endpoint.
- A model-access pitch.
- A replacement for scanners, AppSec teams, security teams, governance processes, legal review, privacy review, or customer accountability.
- An offensive cyber training course or red-team tactics product (an authorized testing approach that simulates attacker behavior to evaluate defenses.)
- A promise of cyber-specialized model access, reduced refusals, special data handling terms, special availability, pricing, or unsupported deployment paths.
- A fully autonomous vulnerability-fixing system without review.

These guardrails are not just risk language. They help you sell more credibly.

A sophisticated security customer will not trust a message that overpromises. They know security work requires authorization, evidence, review, and accountability. Strong Daybreak positioning respects that operating reality.

## Slide 28/50 - Why governed access matters (Module: Governed access and human review)

Cybersecurity work is often dual-use. The same type of task can be legitimate defensive work in one context and harmful or unauthorized in another.

**Example**

Analyzing whether a vulnerability is reachable might be appropriate when performed by an approved defender on a customer-owned application in an authorized environment. Similar activity against systems the customer does not own or is not authorized to test would not be appropriate.

That is why responsible defensive acceleration depends on clear answers to questions like:

- Who is using the capability?
- What systems, repositories, applications, or environments are in scope?
- What is the defensive purpose?
- What activities are permitted?
- What approvals are required?
- What evidence should be produced and retained?
- Who reviews outputs before decisions are made?

Trusted Access for Cyber is OpenAI's governed access path for qualified, authorized defensive cybersecurity work.

It may be relevant when an approved defensive workflow needs reduced cyber-related friction, but it does not remove the need for scope, safeguards, review, or customer accountability.

It should not be the opening pitch for every Daybreak conversation. The opening pitch should be the workflow: What defensive workflow are you trying to improve, and what evidence would show that it improved?

## Slide 42/50 - Strong first-conversation signals (Module: Recognizing a good first Daybreak conversation)

Daybreak may be relevant when the customer is experiencing pressure in a defensive cyber workflow.

Strong first-conversation signals include:

- The customer has a large vulnerability backlog.
- The customer has many scanner findings but limited validation capacity.
- AppSec and engineering teams disagree about which findings are real or urgent.
- Patching windows are slow, risky, or hard to coordinate.
- Security teams need better evidence for governance, audit, or executive communication.
- The customer is accelerating software delivery and wants security embedded earlier in the SDLC.
- The customer wants to improve secure code review, dependency review, or remediation handoff.

These signals are useful because they point to a workflow. They suggest that the customer may need help moving from signal to action.

A strong early conversation should help you understand:

- Where the security signal comes from.
- Where the workflow slows down.
- Who owns review.
- What evidence would show improvement.
- Whether the workflow is authorized, bounded, and appropriate for a responsible next step.

## Slide 43/50 - Early warning signs and escalation points (Module: Recognizing a good first Daybreak conversation)

Some customer signals require caution or escalation.

Watch for conversation patterns like:

- The customer starts with cyber-specialized model access rather than a defensive workflow.
- The customer asks for broad offensive testing, red-team automation, exploit-heavy workflows, malware analysis, or live-target testing.
- The customer asks about critical infrastructure, production testing, custom harnesses, CI/CD automation, API gateways, or complex deployment claims before scope is clear.
- The customer asks for special data handling terms, internal access tiers, pricing, special availability, or unsupported deployment paths.
- Authorization, environment ownership, user approval, reviewer ownership, or systems of record are unclear.

These signals do not always mean the opportunity is wrong. They just mean the partner should not improvise.

A safer response is to pause, clarify the authorized defensive workflow, and involve OpenAI SMEs or the appropriate account process before discussing access, advanced workflows, special terms, or deployment paths.

## Slide 44/50 - Responsible next-step language (Module: Recognizing a good first Daybreak conversation)

Use next-step language that keeps the conversation grounded in workflow, scope, review, proof, and escalation.

Useful questions and phrases include:

- Let's clarify the defensive workflow you want to improve.
- Which owned repository, application, backlog slice, or approved environment would be the safest place to evaluate value?
- Who would review findings and decide whether proposed remediation is acceptable?
- What evidence would show that this workflow improved?
- This may require OpenAI SME support before we discuss access or advanced workflow details.

These questions are intentionally simple. They do not attempt to design the full solution. They help you move from broad interest to responsible next-step clarity.

A useful mental model is:

> Workflow first. Access second. Automation last.

Start with what the customer is trying to defend, where the workflow slows down, and who reviews the outputs. Only then should the conversation move toward access paths, evaluation design, or advanced workflow support.
