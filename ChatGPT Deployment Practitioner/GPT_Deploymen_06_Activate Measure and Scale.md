## Slide 2/40 - Introduction

Successful deployment requires ongoing evaluation. Once an approved ChatGPT workflow moves into real use, the deployment team needs to understand whether the intended users are adopting it, producing useful outputs, and generating enough evidence to guide the next decision.

In this course, you'll review evidence from an active or recently completed workflow activation. Your input may be a User Enablement and Adoption Plan, Use Case Activation Plan, approved customer handoff, sanitized customer example, or course practice example.

Your goal is to turn those observations into a clear recommendation: continue, adjust, expand, or pause.

## Slide 5/40 - Introduction (Module: Evaluate Activation Outcomes)

In this module, you'll review what happened during activation and compare it to what the customer expected.

A strong activation review looks beyond platform access or login activity. It asks whether intended users applied ChatGPT in the approved workflow, used the right business context or source material, followed review expectations, and surfaced feedback or issues through the right routes.

Use this review to separate planned activation from observed activity. That fact base will help you document the key indicators, issues, and open questions that shape the next deployment decision.

## Slide 6/40 - Review activation activity

Activation review begins with a simple question: Did the approved workflow launch as planned?

Before you assess value, expansion, or scale, confirm what actually happened during activation. Do not assume that because a workflow was approved, it was used as intended.

Review activation activity across six areas:

- **Approved workflows activated** — Identify which approved workflows were introduced to users. Be specific. "ChatGPT for operations" is too broad. "Procedure summarization for regional operations managers using approved SOP documents" is specific enough to review.
- **Target user participation** — Check whether the intended user group participated. Participation may include attending enablement, joining office hours, using the approved workflow, submitting feedback, or completing a first-use task.
- **First-use completion** — Look for evidence that users completed the expected first-use activity. For example, did users create the first renewal brief, draft the first procedure summary, analyze the first approved dataset, or prepare the first human-reviewed customer response?
- **Manager or champion observations** — Managers and champions often see things that usage numbers alone cannot show. They may notice whether users are confident, confused, skeptical, improvising, or applying the workflow correctly.
- **Support issues raised** — Review questions, help requests, blockers, and escalation patterns. Support issues are not always negative. They can show engagement. But they also reveal where enablement, context, source guidance, or governance expectations may need reinforcement.
- **Activation timeline progress** — Compare the planned activation timeline to actual progress. Did enablement, first-use activities, review checkpoints, feedback collection, and decision meetings happen when expected?

A useful activation review separates planned activity from observed activity. That gives the customer a fact base for deciding what to do next.

## Slide 7/40 - Compare outcomes to expectations

Once you know what happened, compare it to what was expected.

A strong activation review includes: planned outcomes, observed outcomes, areas of alignment, and areas requiring attention.

Use this comparison to avoid jumping to a conclusion too quickly.

For example:

- **Planned:** Operations managers use ChatGPT to summarize procedures from approved SOPs. **Observed:** Managers are using ChatGPT, but many are pasting informal notes instead of approved SOPs. **What it may indicate:** Users are engaged, but source guidance needs reinforcement.
- **Planned:** Champions collect feedback after first use. **Observed:** Feedback is coming through side conversations instead of the official channel. **What it may indicate:** Feedback exists, but the route is unclear.
- **Planned:** Managers review a sample of outputs before wider use. **Observed:** Managers reviewed only a small sample. **What it may indicate:** Human review expectations may need clearer ownership.
- **Planned:** Users complete the workflow during the first two weeks. **Observed:** Users completed the workflow, but only after office-hour support. **What it may indicate:** Adoption is possible, but the workflow may still be too dependent on live support.

The comparison should stay anchored in the approved workflow. High usage does not automatically mean activation success. Low usage does not automatically mean activation failure. The key is whether observed activity creates useful evidence about the intended workflow.

Ask:

- What matched the plan?
- What changed?
- What surprised us?
- What requires attention before the next decision?
- What evidence is strong enough to act on?
- What evidence is still too limited or unclear?

## Slide 8/40 - Document key observations

After comparing planned and observed outcomes, document the most important observations. Good observations are specific, evidence-based, and tied to the workflow. Weak observations are vague or based only on general impressions.

Document observations across these categories:

- **Positive indicators** — What suggests activation is working? Examples include strong target-user participation, repeat use, manager support, useful outputs, constructive feedback, or fewer support issues over time.
- **Unexpected behaviors** — What did users do differently than planned? For example, users may apply ChatGPT to an adjacent workflow, use unapproved source material, skip the review step, or rely on champions more heavily than expected.
- **Emerging challenges** — What may limit adoption or value? Examples include low confidence, unclear prompts, source confusion, missing workflow ownership, inconsistent manager reinforcement, or too many open support questions.
- **Context or source-quality issues** — Does ChatGPT have the right business context? Are users working from approved sources? Are source materials current, complete, and accessible? Are users clear on what they may and may not use?
- **Review or approval issues** — Were outputs reviewed before they influenced business decisions, customer communications, regulated processes, or operational changes? If review was required but did not happen, that issue should be visible.
- **Governance or access concerns** — Did any data-use, permission, access, policy, audit, or escalation concern appear? These concerns do not always require stopping activation, but they do require ownership and follow-up.

A practical observation should connect evidence to meaning.

Weak observation: "Users are confused."

Stronger observation: "Users are completing the procedure-summary workflow, but several are unsure which SOP documents are approved. The workflow is promising, but source guidance should be clarified before expanding to another region."

The stronger version helps the team act.

## Slide 12/40 - Introduction (Module: Assess Adoption and Early Value Signals)

In this module, you'll assess whether users are beginning to adopt the approved ChatGPT workflow and whether early value signals are emerging.

Adoption is not the same as access. A strong review looks for evidence that users are applying ChatGPT in the intended workflow moment, returning to the workflow, engaging with support, and producing outputs that can be reviewed or improved.

You'll also distinguish adoption indicators from early value evidence. This helps the customer understand what the evidence suggests, what remains directional, and what should be reviewed before making a decision about next steps.

## Slide 13/40 - Review adoption signals

Adoption is more than access. A user who logs in once is not necessarily adopting the workflow. A user who repeatedly uses ChatGPT to support an approved work process is showing a stronger adoption signal.

Review adoption signals such as:

- **Target-user participation** — Are the intended users involved, or is activity coming from a different group?
- **Repeat use of approved workflows** — Are users returning to the workflow after first use? Repeat use often suggests the workflow is relevant enough to try again.
- **Engagement with enablement or support** — Are users attending office hours, asking useful questions, viewing job aids, using examples, or joining champion-led support channels?
- **Manager or champion feedback** — Are managers and champions seeing signs that users understand the workflow, apply it correctly, and know when to ask for help?
- **User confidence or sentiment** — Do users feel able and willing to use the workflow? Are they confident in what ChatGPT can help with and where human judgment remains required?
- **Use in the intended workflow moment** — Are users applying ChatGPT when the work actually happens, not only experimenting outside the process?

This last signal is especially important.

If the approved workflow is "summarize approved procedures before regional operations planning," adoption means users apply ChatGPT during procedure review or planning.

If they are only asking general questions or trying unrelated prompts, they may be building general familiarity, but the approved workflow is not yet being adopted.

A healthy adoption review combines numbers and observations. Quantitative signals may show participation, repeat use, and completion rates. Qualitative signals may show confidence, confusion, usefulness, and workflow fit.

## Slide 14/40 - Review success signals

Success signals are the practical indicators the customer agreed to review during activation.

They are not always final ROI measures. Early success signals often show direction. They help the team understand whether the workflow is becoming useful, whether outputs are reviewable, and whether users are building confidence.

Review success signals such as:

- **Expected workflow output** — Did the workflow produce the intended artifact? For example, a procedure summary, account brief, policy summary, support response draft, meeting prep brief, or analysis memo.
- **Workflow quality or usefulness** — Were outputs useful enough for a human reviewer to edit, approve, or improve?
- **Reduced friction or effort** — Did users report less manual searching, drafting, synthesis, rework, or blank-page effort?
- **User confidence** — Are users more confident applying ChatGPT to the approved workflow?
- **Customer-approved value indicator** — Does the evidence connect to the value signal the customer wanted to review?
- **Output review completion** — Were outputs reviewed by the right person or role where review was required?
- **Appropriate human validation** — Did users validate outputs before applying them in business decisions, customer-facing work, regulated workflows, or operational processes?
- **Useful feedback captured for workflow improvement** — Was feedback collected in a way that helps the deployment team improve the workflow?

Where the approved workflow uses ChatGPT Work or another delegated, scheduled, or action-taking ChatGPT experience, review more than return frequency. Confirm whether the work reached the agreed completion state, produced a usable or review-ready artifact, used approved context and tools, followed required review or approval steps, and surfaced failed actions or exceptions through the agreed support route.

A success signal should answer: What did we expect to improve, and what evidence tells us whether it is moving in the right direction?

For example, in a procedure summarization workflow, a useful early success signal might be:

"Operations managers can produce first-pass procedure summaries that are useful for manager review, with fewer manual source checks and clear feedback on source quality."

That is stronger than: "Users like ChatGPT."

The stronger signal connects to the workflow, the output, the review process, and the improvement opportunity.

## Slide 15/40 - Review early value evidence

Early value evidence helps the customer decide what to do next. It should be directional, credible, and tied to the approved workflow.

Common forms of early value evidence include:

- **Directional efficiency signal** — Users report that the workflow takes less time, or early timing comparisons suggest reduced effort.
- **Reduced effort or rework** — Users spend less time searching across sources, starting from scratch, rewriting avoidable errors, or reworking inconsistent outputs.
- **Improved consistency** — Outputs follow a more consistent format, tone, checklist, or review pattern.
- **Improved output usefulness** — Managers or reviewers find the output more useful, complete, or decision-ready.
- **Limitation or assumption in the evidence** — The team documents what the evidence does and does not prove.

The final point matters. Early value evidence is often incomplete. It may come from a small user group, a short time window, self-reported feedback, or a limited sample of outputs.

A credible deployment review should state the limitation rather than overstate the result.

For example:

"Early evidence suggests the procedure-summary workflow reduces manual synthesis effort and improves consistency. However, the sample covers only two weeks and 12 manager-reviewed outputs, so the team should treat this as directional evidence rather than final ROI proof."

This type of language builds trust. It shows that the partner team can help the customer interpret evidence responsibly.

## Slide 19/40 - Introduction (Module: Identify Barriers and Improvement Opportunities)

In this module, you'll identify what may be limiting deployment success and what practical improvements should be made.

Mixed activation results do not always mean the workflow is failing. They may show that users need clearer workflow guidance, better source instructions, stronger manager reinforcement, a more visible support route, or a clearer review process.

You'll learn to connect observed barriers to likely root causes, then recommend improvement actions that are specific, owned, and tied to the next evidence review. This keeps the deployment moving forward responsibly instead of expanding before issues are understood.

## Slide 20/40 - Identify deployment barriers

When activation results are mixed, the next step is not always expansion.

Often, the best next step is to identify what is limiting success and adjust the deployment approach.

Common deployment barriers include:

- **Low awareness** — Users may not know the workflow exists, why it matters, how it connects to their role, or where to begin.
- **Workflow confusion** — Users may understand ChatGPT generally but not know how to apply it in the approved workflow. They may be unclear on the right prompt pattern, source material, output format, or review expectation.
- **Insufficient support** — Users may get stuck but not know where to ask questions. Support questions may be scattered across private messages, email threads, meetings, and informal conversations.
- **Stakeholder challenges** — Managers, champions, business owners, governance owners, or support owners may not be reinforcing the workflow consistently.
- **Resource constraints** — The team may lack enough time, support coverage, reviewer capacity, source ownership, or operational attention to sustain the workflow.

Also watch for barriers related to context, review, and operating visibility:

- Is ChatGPT being used with the approved business context?
- Are users clear on which sources they may use?
- Are outputs reviewed before they affect customers, systems, or business decisions?
- Are issues visible to the right owner?
- Is there a feedback loop for improvement?

A good barrier statement should be specific enough to act on.

Weak barrier statement: "Users are not adopting."

Stronger barrier statement: "Operations managers are interested in the procedure-summary workflow, but adoption is inconsistent because users are unsure which SOP documents are approved and support questions are not consistently routed through the champion channel."

The stronger version points toward action.

## Slide 21/40 - Identify root causes

When you identify a deployment barrier, treat it as a visible symptom, not the full explanation.

A barrier is the pattern you can observe in the activation evidence. For example, users may not repeat the workflow, questions may appear in the wrong channels, outputs may vary in quality, or users may be unsure which sources are approved.

A root cause is the likely reason that barrier is happening. The same barrier can have different root causes. For example, low repeat use could be caused by weak manager reinforcement, unclear workflow instructions, poor source quality, low user confidence, or a support route that users do not trust.

Before recommending an improvement action, ask: What is most likely causing this barrier, and what evidence supports that explanation?

Then review root causes across three categories:

- **Enablement gaps** — Users were not taught what they needed to understand, practice, or reinforce. Examples: users do not know the approved workflow steps; users do not understand review expectations; users do not know where to get support; users have not seen examples close enough to their actual work.
- **Workflow issues** — The workflow itself may be too broad, too complex, poorly timed, or misaligned with how work actually happens. Examples: the workflow requires too many manual steps; the output format does not match what managers need; the workflow depends on source material users cannot easily locate; users are asked to use ChatGPT outside the moment when the work normally happens.
- **Support limitations** — Users may not know where to go for help, support owners may be unclear, or questions may not be captured in a usable way. Examples: questions are scattered across private channels; champions are active but not empowered to route issues; support needs are increasing faster than the team can respond; there is no issue-escalation path for governance or source questions.

A useful root-cause question is: What would need to change for this workflow to become easier, safer, or more valuable for the intended users?

## Slide 22/40 - Identify improvement opportunities

Once you identify the likely root cause, match it to a practical improvement action.

Common improvement opportunities include:

- Users are unaware of the workflow → Communication reinforcement.
- Users understand ChatGPT but not the approved workflow → Clearer workflow guidance.
- Users lack confidence applying the workflow → Targeted enablement adjustment.
- Users ask questions in scattered channels → Support-route clarification.
- Managers are not reinforcing review expectations → Champion or manager support.
- Source material is outdated, unclear, or not ready → Confirm the source owner, update the approved source set, or escalate the readiness issue before expansion.
- Outputs vary in quality → Workflow guidance, source clarification, or review calibration.

Do not treat every issue as an enablement issue.

If users are interested but confused, they may need clearer workflow guidance and examples.

If output quality is low because source material is outdated, another training session may not solve the problem. The team may need to update the approved source set and confirm ownership.

If users are producing outputs but no one reviews them, the issue is not adoption. It is review ownership.

A practical improvement recommendation should include: the barrier, the likely root cause, the recommended action, the owner, and the evidence the team will review next.

Example: "Users are completing the workflow, but source-use confusion is affecting output quality. The likely root cause is unclear approved-source guidance. The deployment owner should confirm the approved SOP set, update the workflow job aid, and review another sample of manager-approved summaries before expanding to a second region."

## Slide 24/40 - Summary

Barriers are not always signs of failure. They are signals that something may need to be clarified, reinforced, supported, or escalated. A strong improvement recommendation connects the observed barrier to the likely root cause, assigns ownership, and identifies what evidence should be reviewed next.

This helps the customer improve the workflow before expanding unresolved issues.

## Slide 25/40 - Introduction (Module: Evaluate Expansion Opportunities)

In this module, you'll evaluate whether the customer is ready to expand ChatGPT adoption beyond the initial activation group.

Expansion should be based on evidence, not enthusiasm alone. You'll review adoption, early value evidence, stakeholder support, support capacity, unresolved risks, governance expectations, and readiness of the next users, teams, or workflows.

You'll also compare potential expansion candidates and determine whether the next step should be to continue, adjust, expand, or pause. The goal is to recommend expansion only when it is practical, supported, and responsible.

## Slide 26/40 - Review readiness for expansion

Expansion should be evidence-based. Do not recommend expansion simply because users are active or stakeholders are excited. Before recommending expansion, review whether the deployment has enough evidence and readiness to move forward responsibly.

Consider these readiness factors:

- **Adoption indicators** — Are intended users applying ChatGPT in the approved workflow? Is there evidence of repeat use or workflow-specific engagement?
- **Early value evidence** — Is there credible evidence that the workflow is beginning to improve effort, quality, consistency, risk, speed, or another customer-approved outcome?
- **Stakeholder support** — Are business owners, managers, champions, and relevant governance owners supporting the workflow?
- **Support capacity** — Can the customer support more users, teams, or workflows without overwhelming champions, managers, or support teams?
- **Unresolved risks with clear issue-escalation route** — Are open risks named, owned, and routed for follow-up?
- **Readiness of additional users, teams, or workflows** — Are expansion audiences prepared, or would they require new enablement, source access, review ownership, or governance approval?
- **Operating visibility** — Can the team see activity, completed or failed work, review or approval outcomes, feedback, issues, and improvement actions as adoption expands? For delegated, scheduled, or action-taking workflows, can exceptions and unresolved actions be routed to an owner?
- **Evidence that governance expectations are working in practice** — Are human review, acceptable-use boundaries, source guidance, and escalation expectations being followed?

Expansion readiness does not require perfection. It means the team understands what is working, what remains unresolved, and what conditions must be in place for the next step.

Use this test: Would expanding now make the workflow more valuable and manageable, or would it multiply an unresolved issue?

## Slide 27/40 - Identify expansion candidates

Expansion candidates should be selected based on similarity, readiness, supportability, and manageable dependency risk.

Potential expansion candidates include:

- **Additional users** — More users in the same team, role, or region.
- **Additional teams** — A related team with similar work and similar context needs.
- **Additional workflows** — A related workflow that uses the same source set, review path, or output pattern.
- **Additional business functions** — A broader function where the workflow pattern can be reused with appropriate readiness checks.

The safest expansion candidate is often close to the successful workflow.

For example: If regional operations managers are successfully preparing human-reviewed procedure summaries, a reasonable expansion candidate might be another operations region using the same approved SOP structure and the same manager review process.

A riskier expansion candidate might be a customer-facing incident-response workflow, because it may introduce new data boundaries, new review requirements, new risk owners, and a different support model.

When identifying candidates, ask:

- Is the workflow similar to what has already worked?
- Does the audience have a clear reason to adopt?
- Are the required sources approved and ready?
- Is the review path clear?
- Is support capacity available?
- Are governance expectations understood?
- Can the team monitor usage, feedback, issues, and improvement actions?

Expansion should build from evidence, not from pressure to move quickly.

## Slide 28/40 - Review expansion considerations

After identifying expansion candidates, review the considerations that should shape the recommendation.

Key considerations include:

- **Support requirements** — What enablement, office hours, manager guidance, champion coverage, or self-service support is needed?
- **Resource requirements** — What time, people, or operational capacity is needed to support expansion?
- **Unresolved dependencies** — What source, access, governance, review, reporting, or approval dependencies remain?
- **Governance or approval needs** — What policy, human-review, acceptable-use, audit, or escalation expectations need to be confirmed?
- **Recommended next action** — Should the customer continue, adjust, expand, or pause?

Use these four decision options:

- **Continue** — Use when the workflow is promising and should keep running while the team gathers more evidence.
- **Adjust** — Use when the workflow has potential, but specific issues should be fixed before expansion.
- **Expand** — Use when adoption, early value evidence, readiness, support capacity, governance, and operating visibility are strong enough, and unresolved risks are understood, owned, and manageable.
- **Pause** — Use when a blocker, risk, weak evidence base, or ownership gap makes continuation or expansion irresponsible.

A strong recommendation explains why the selected action fits the evidence.

Weak recommendation: "Expand because adoption is good."

Stronger recommendation: "Adjust before expanding. Adoption is promising, with repeat use and positive manager feedback, but users are still unclear on approved source material. The next action is to clarify source guidance, update the workflow job aid, and review another sample of manager-approved outputs before adding a second region."

The stronger recommendation protects customer trust and gives the team a practical next step.

## Slide 33/40 - Confirm activation outcomes

The Activation Review and Expansion Plan brings your review findings into one practical artifact.

Start by confirming activation outcomes. This section should summarize what happened during activation and what the deployment team observed.

Include:

- **Key observations** — What happened during activation? Focus on the approved workflow, target users, first-use activity, timeline, support activity, and review activity.
- **Success indicators** — What suggests the workflow is working or beginning to work? Include evidence such as repeat use, useful outputs, positive manager feedback, strong champion engagement, or reduced workflow friction.
- **Areas requiring attention** — What needs follow-up before the next decision? Include source issues, review gaps, low confidence, unclear support routes, unresolved governance questions, or weak evidence.

A strong activation outcomes section is concise but specific.

Example: "Activation launched with 60 regional operations managers. Participation was strong, with 52 users attending enablement and 39 completing the approved procedure-summary workflow in the first two weeks. Early review suggests the workflow is useful when users follow the approved SOP guidance, but source-use reinforcement and support routing need attention before expansion."

This section should help the customer quickly understand activation status without digging through every data point.

## Slide 34/40 - Confirm deployment findings

Next, consolidate the deployment findings.

This section should include:

- **Adoption observations** — Who is using the workflow, how often, and in what moments?
- **Early value evidence** — What evidence suggests the workflow is creating useful outcomes?
- **Improvement opportunities** — What should be adjusted before the next decision?
- **Review and monitoring observations** — How are outputs, feedback, issues, and next-step decisions being reviewed?
- **Context, access, or governance issues affecting workflow success** — What source, permission, review, policy, or escalation issues may affect workflow success?

The deployment findings section should not overstate value. Distinguish what is known from what is still directional.

For example: "Users report faster first drafts, and managers found 9 of 12 reviewed summaries useful with light edits. This is promising directional evidence, but no baseline timing study was completed, so the team should avoid positioning this as confirmed ROI. The next review should collect either a small baseline comparison or structured manager feedback sample."

This finding is useful because it connects evidence, limitation, and next action.

## Slide 35/40 - Confirm expansion recommendations

The final section of the plan recommends what should happen next.

Include:

- **Expansion opportunities** — Which users, teams, workflows, or functions may be considered next?
- **Risks and dependencies** — What must be resolved or monitored?
- **Recommended next actions** — Should the customer continue, adjust, expand, or pause? Why?

A strong expansion recommendation should be practical enough for partner and customer teams to act on.

- **Continue** if the workflow is producing useful evidence and should keep running while the team gathers more data.
- **Adjust** if the workflow is promising but needs improvement before expansion.
- **Expand** if adoption, early value evidence, readiness, support capacity, governance, and operating visibility are strong enough, and unresolved risks are understood, owned, and manageable.
- **Pause** if unresolved risk, lack of ownership, weak evidence, or governance concerns make continuation or expansion irresponsible.

The recommendation should also identify ownership.

For example: "The recommended next action is to adjust before expanding. The customer deployment owner should confirm review expectations, the operations workflow owner should update the SOP guidance, the champion lead should reinforce the support route, and the partner deployment lead should schedule a two-week evidence review before adding the second region."

Expansion decisions work best when they are tied to owners and a near-term decision point.

Then prepare a concise executive readout for the sponsor or decision group. State the approved workflow and activation status, the strongest adoption, output-quality, and early-value evidence, the main limitation, risk, or governance issue, the recommended decision (continue, adjust, expand, or pause), and the next action, owner, and review point. The readout should make the decision clear without requiring the audience to review every data point.

Use it as a quick reference when deciding whether the workflow should continue as planned, be adjusted, be expanded, or be paused.
