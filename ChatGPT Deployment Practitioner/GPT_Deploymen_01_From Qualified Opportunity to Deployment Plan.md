## Slide 11/52 - Start from the sales handoff evidence

This module connects a selling ChatGPT handoff to the deployment work that follows.

A good seller handoff does not need to be a final deployment plan. But it should make the opportunity concrete enough for the deployment team to start the right conversation.

The key question is: what do we actually know, and what still needs to be confirmed before deployment planning begins?

Remember the deployment-readiness principle: Confirm the evidence before you commit the motion.

A useful deployment handoff should make the opportunity concrete enough for planning. At minimum, the deployment team should look for five types of evidence.

**1. Opportunity summary** — This explains the customer's objectives, scope, intended outcomes, and decision context. Ask: what customer problem was identified? Why does it matter now? What outcome is the customer trying to improve? What decision or next step has the customer already agreed to?

**2. Workflow and user evidence** — This explains what is known about the workflow and the first user group. Ask: what work will ChatGPT support? Who performs that work today? Which team or cohort may use ChatGPT first? What adoption signals, user feedback, or interest already exist?

**3. Value hypothesis** — This explains how the workflow may connect to measurable customer value or operational improvement. Ask: is the value about time, quality, capacity, consistency, risk reduction, employee experience, or customer experience? What evidence would show that the workflow is improving? Is the value hypothesis specific enough to test?

**4. Recommended next step** — This explains what the deployment team should confirm, plan, or escalate before launch work begins. Ask: is the next step a planning conversation? A stakeholder alignment meeting? A focused validation step? A pilot design conversation? A governance or workspace-readiness review?

**5. Product experience, context, and control needs** — This explains which ChatGPT experience and workflow components may be required for the first motion, and which access, permission, approval, and review dependencies still need validation. Ask: will the workflow use collaborative Chat, ChatGPT Work, or a Workspace Agent? What approved files, Company Knowledge, Apps, or systems might the workflow need? Which users or roles need access, and does the workspace admin need to enable the required experience or component? What actions, if any, may be allowed, and where are approval and human-review gates required? Which product, connection, data, or governance dependencies are not yet approved?

For regulated or policy-sensitive customers, the handoff should also identify known access surfaces, data boundaries, action boundaries, review requirements, and explicitly excluded uses where they are already known.

The handoff is not a final deployment plan. But it should be clear enough to guide the first deployment conversation.

## Slide 12/52 - Separate evidence from assumptions

A deployment handoff usually contains a mix of confirmed evidence, reasonable assumptions, and unknowns. Your job is to separate them before the team makes deployment commitments.

**Confirmed evidence** is information the customer has stated, agreed, documented, or assigned. Examples include: named stakeholders; agreed next steps; known workflows; documented success criteria; customer statements about pain points; confirmed user group; confirmed business owner; confirmed policy or access constraint.

**Reasonable assumptions** are plausible but not yet validated. Examples include: initial user group; likely data or knowledge source needs; supported devices or access surfaces; governance considerations; review expectations; rollout shape; measurement approach; change-management needs. Reasonable assumptions are useful, but they should not be treated as facts.

**Unknowns** are missing or unresolved details that could affect deployment planning. Examples include: missing stakeholder input; unclear source access; regional or regulated-deployment requirements; unresolved policy questions; data-use boundaries; unvalidated adoption risks; missing workspace owner; unclear success criteria.

This distinction prevents deployment teams from treating seller assumptions as confirmed implementation facts.

A simple deployment habit is: **ABC: Assume nothing. Believe nothing. Confirm everything.**

That does not mean the seller handoff is untrusted. It means deployment planning needs evidence strong enough to support customer commitments.

## Slide 13/52 - Handoff quality check

Use this handoff quality check before moving into deployment planning.

A strong handoff should answer six questions. These are the six questions, including a weak and a more effective answer for each:

1. **What work is changing?** The handoff should name a real workflow, not a generic productivity goal. Weak: "The team wants to use AI to save time." Stronger: "Customer success managers want to prepare renewal briefs more consistently from approved account notes, support history, and usage context."

2. **Who will use ChatGPT first?** The handoff should identify the first user group or the gap that must be validated. Weak: "The whole company may use it." Stronger: "The first user group is likely 15 customer success managers supporting enterprise renewals, pending confirmation with the business owner."

3. **Who owns the business result?** The handoff should name the role accountable for the outcome. Weak: "Leadership is interested." Stronger: "The VP of Customer Success owns the renewal-readiness outcome and will attend the first-motion decision review."

4. **What does success look like?** The handoff should include a measurable or observable success signal. Weak: "Users like ChatGPT." Stronger: "Users and managers can compare renewal brief drafts against the current process and assess usefulness, consistency, and preparation quality during the first validation window."

5. **What must be validated before launch?** The handoff should identify important assumptions or constraints. Examples include: source access; workspace readiness; data boundaries; security review; legal or compliance review; user cohort; manager support; success evidence; output review expectations.

6. **What should happen next?** The handoff should point to a practical next step. Examples include: confirm the first workflow; align business owner and workspace admin; validate approved source access; hold a deployment kickoff; run a focused validation step; re-scope before planning advances.

A weak handoff usually sounds broad, feature-led, or disconnected from a measurable workflow.

If the handoff is ambiguous, the deployment team should flag the gap for targeted re-scope rather than redoing sales discovery inside this course.

## Slide 18/52 - Translate the customer problem

Once you understand the handoff evidence, the next step is to translate the opportunity into a deployment-grade problem statement.

A deployment problem statement should be specific enough to guide planning without overpromising the final solution. It should connect the customer's current workflow friction to the ChatGPT-supported workflow and the outcome the customer wants to improve.

A deployment-grade problem statement should include five elements:

- **Current workflow friction** — What is slow, inconsistent, costly, risky, or difficult today?
- **Affected team or user group** — Who experiences the problem?
- **Operational impact** — Why does the friction matter?
- **Intended ChatGPT-supported workflow** — What should ChatGPT help users do?
- **Business outcome** — What customer outcome should the deployment support?

## Slide 19/52 - From seller-level to deployment-grade

Compare these two versions.

**Seller-level version**

"The customer wants ChatGPT to help customer success teams save time."

This is useful for interest qualification, but it is not specific enough for deployment planning. It does not define: the workflow, the user group, the business output, the source context, the success evidence, the owner, the first decision.

**Deployment-grade version**

"Customer success managers spend several hours preparing renewal briefs because account notes, support history, usage signals, and renewal risks are spread across sources. The first ChatGPT-supported workflow will help a small group of customer success managers prepare human-reviewed renewal brief drafts from approved account context. During the first motion, the team will collect user feedback, manager feedback, and workflow-value signals to decide whether to proceed, refine, or re-scope."

This version is stronger because it identifies: the workflow, the user group, the friction, the intended output, the review requirement, the evidence needed for the next decision.

A good deployment-grade problem statement keeps the team grounded in the work and supports the priorities to focus on.

**Confirm the priority workflow**

A deployment plan needs a first workflow. The first workflow should be specific enough to activate. It should describe what users will do with ChatGPT.

Example: "Prepare a renewal brief draft from approved account context." Not: "Improve customer success productivity."

The first workflow should be relevant to the customer problem. If the problem is inconsistent renewal preparation, the first workflow should support renewal preparation.

The first workflow should be owned by a visible team. Someone should own the workflow and the business result. Example: the customer success operations lead owns the workflow; the VP of Customer Success owns the business outcome.

The first workflow should be measurable enough to validate. The team should be able to collect evidence. Example: use a short feedback form, manager review checklist, and sample output comparison.

The first workflow should be practical enough to launch without solving every related use case. It should be narrow enough for a controlled starting motion. Example: start with enterprise account managers preparing quarterly renewal briefs. Do not include all account planning, customer support summaries, QBR preparation, renewal negotiation, and executive reporting at once.

A good first workflow does not need to solve every adjacent problem. It needs to create useful evidence for the next decision.

## Slide 20/52 - Connect success criteria to observable evidence

Success criteria should help the customer decide what happens after the first motion.

That decision may be: proceed, refine, re-scope, pause, expand, defer.

At this stage, success criteria should be planning targets, not final ROI commitments.

Use three evidence categories.

1. **Adoption or engagement** — This asks whether the intended users try the workflow in the expected work moment. Examples: did the intended cohort use ChatGPT for the renewal brief workflow? Did users try the workflow during real preparation work? Did users return to the workflow more than once? Did managers see enough use to evaluate the motion?

2. **User and manager feedback** — This asks whether users and managers find the workflow useful enough to keep refining. Examples: did users find the output useful? Did managers find the briefs consistent enough to review? What concerns, blockers, or improvement requests appeared? Did users trust the workflow enough to use it again?

3. **Workflow-value signal** — This asks whether time, quality, capacity, consistency, risk, or experience appears to improve. Examples: does the draft brief reduce preparation friction? Is the output more consistent than the current process? Are managers seeing better evidence? Are users spending less time gathering scattered context? Are risks and open questions surfaced more reliably?

Each evidence category should have a planning threshold, evidence source, and owner:

- **Adoption or engagement** — Planning threshold: at least most of the first cohort tries the workflow during the validation window. Evidence source: workspace usage signal, user check-in, manager confirmation. Owner: deployment lead and customer workflow owner.
- **User and manager feedback** — Planning threshold: users and managers can explain whether the workflow is useful enough to refine. Evidence source: short survey, interview, office hours notes. Owner: customer workflow owner.
- **Workflow-value signal** — Planning threshold: briefs appear more consistent or easier to review. Evidence source: sample output review, manager feedback, quality checklist. Owner: business outcome owner.

Do not overstate these thresholds. They are planning targets, not guaranteed results.

## Slide 21/52 - Keep solution language practical

Deployment framing should avoid language that is too broad, feature-led, or unsupported.

Avoid: generic productivity claims; unsupported ROI claims; long product feature lists; premature workspace configuration details; claims about internal source access that have not been validated; promises that ChatGPT will fully automate a customer-owned decision; statements that imply outputs can affect customers, systems, or business decisions without review.

Use practical workflow language instead:

- Too broad: "ChatGPT will transform customer success productivity." Stronger: "ChatGPT will support a first cohort of customer success managers in preparing human-reviewed renewal brief drafts from approved account context."
- Too feature-led: "Use ChatGPT Work, Apps, Company Knowledge, Workspace Agents, and analytics to power renewal operations." Stronger: "The first motion should confirm which account context is approved, how users will prepare the brief, what managers will review, and what evidence will support the next decision."
- Too much certainty: "This will reduce preparation time by 50%." Stronger: "The first motion will collect evidence on whether the workflow reduces preparation friction and improves brief consistency."

Good deployment language is clear, practical, and evidence-based.

## Slide 27/52 - Confirm executive sponsorship and decision ownership

A deployment plan cannot move forward on workflow clarity alone. It also needs stakeholder alignment and scope boundaries.

The key question is: who owns the deployment decision, and what is in or out of scope for the first motion?

A named sponsor is helpful, but it is not enough. The deployment plan should confirm whether the sponsor or decision owner is engaged in the moments that matter.

Ask:

- **Who is the executive sponsor or decision owner?** This person supports urgency, alignment, and the customer decision.
- **What decision does the customer expect to make after the first motion?** Examples: proceed to broader rollout, refine the workflow, re-scope the cohort, validate source access, pause or defer.
- **What decision moment is required?** Examples: executive planning session, deployment kickoff, pilot review, first-motion readout, governance approval, budget or resource decision.
- **Who must attend or approve each decision point?** The decision may require more than one stakeholder. Examples: executive sponsor, business outcome owner, workspace admin, security or legal owner, IT owner, regional or compliance stakeholder, partner deployment lead.

A sponsor who is generally supportive but absent from required decision moments may not be enough to advance planning.

A practical deployment brief should say: who owns the decision, what decision is expected, when the decision happens, what evidence the decision will use.

## Slide 28/52 - Identify deployment stakeholder roles

Common deployment stakeholder roles include the following:

- **Executive sponsor** — supports urgency, prioritization, and organizational alignment.
- **Business outcome owner** — owns the workflow result or operational metric.
- **Workflow owner** — understands the day-to-day work and user needs.
- **Workspace owner or admin** — controls relevant workspace settings, access decisions, and setup dependencies.
- **IT, security, legal, compliance, or data owner** — validates source access, policy, risk, regional requirements, and governance needs.
- **Partner deployment lead** — coordinates the deployment plan and customer-facing next steps.

Not every deployment needs every role at the same depth. A low-risk, narrow workflow may need lighter involvement from governance stakeholders. A regulated, source-connected, or policy-sensitive workflow may need deeper review before launch planning advances.

The important point is visibility. Unresolved ownership should not be hidden. It should be captured in the Deployment Brief as a readiness gap or dependency.

## Slide 29/52 - Confirm scope boundaries

Scope boundaries define what the first motion includes and what it does not include.

- A clear scope boundary should state which workflows are in scope. Example: "Renewal brief preparation for enterprise customer success managers."
- A clear scope boundary should state which users or cohort are in scope. Example: "A first cohort of 10–15 customer success managers."
- A clear scope boundary should state which functions, regions, or teams are out of scope. Example: "Support operations, sales forecasting, and global account planning are out of scope for the first motion."
- A clear scope boundary should state which data categories, access surfaces, or feature behaviors are out of scope. Example: "Customer PHI, unapproved source systems, and automated customer-facing communications are out of scope."
- A clear scope boundary should state which dependencies must be complete before go-live. Example: "Workspace owner confirmation, approved source list, user cohort confirmation, and manager review checklist."
- A clear scope boundary should state which topics are parked for post-pilot or expansion. Example: "Automated renewal-risk scoring, CRM write-back, and global rollout are parked for later review." A clear scope boundary makes related topics visible for later planning without including them in the first motion.

Over-scoping warning signs include: too many functions, too many use cases, too broad a user group, success defined as broad exposure rather than useful launch evidence, unconfirmed source access, missing workspace owner, governance or data-source owner not engaged, no clear first decision point.

A strong first motion should be narrow enough to learn from and credible enough to matter.

## Slide 30/52 - Spot stakeholder, scope, and governance gaps

Use this checklist to identify gaps before deployment planning advances.

**Ownership gaps:** no named workspace admin or customer deployment owner; a business owner is engaged, but the actual users are unknown; a workflow owner has not confirmed the day-to-day process; no one owns review, escalation, or improvement after first use.

**Decision gaps:** required executive alignment is not confirmed; the first-motion decision point is unclear; the customer has not defined what happens after the first motion; the deployment team does not know who must approve the next step.

**Scope gaps:** the first user group is too broad for a controlled start; too many functions or use cases are included; in-scope and out-of-scope workflows are not separated; dependencies before launch are missing; post-pilot or expansion topics are not parked.

**Governance or source-access gaps:** governance or data-source owners are likely needed but not engaged; access to internal knowledge, files, apps, or systems has not been validated; regional, regulated, data residency, or inference residency requirements may apply but are unresolved; human review requirements for outputs are unclear.

A useful Deployment Brief does not need every gap resolved immediately. But it should make the gaps visible.

## Slide 35/52 - Apply first motion use case quality criteria

At this point, you have inspected the handoff, clarified the deployment problem, and identified stakeholders and scope.

Now you need to decide what kind of starting motion fits the evidence. The key question is: should this opportunity proceed, move into focused validation, be re-scoped, or be deferred?

Remember the deployment-readiness principle: confirm the evidence before you commit the motion.

A first-motion use case should be strong enough to create useful evidence, but narrow enough to support closely. A first-motion use case is:

- **A real workflow** — it should describe work people already do, not a broad interest area. Stronger: "Prepare renewal brief drafts." Weaker: "Improve productivity."
- **High-frequency or high-friction** — the workflow should happen often enough, or create enough pain, to justify focused validation. Examples: repeated briefing preparation, recurring analysis, repeated document review, repeated internal knowledge search.
- **Observable for value inside the first agreed validation window** — the customer should be able to observe whether the workflow is useful within the first motion. Examples: user feedback, manager feedback, output quality review, preparation friction, consistency signals.
- **Suited to the first cohort** — the first users should be reachable, supported, and close enough to the workflow to provide useful feedback.
- **Supported by manageable dependencies** — the use case should not require too many unresolved source, policy, access, governance, or change-management dependencies.
- **Capable of producing evidence for a later scale decision** — the first motion should help the customer decide whether to proceed, refine, re-scope, or pause.

Use cases that require months of change management, unclear source access, or bespoke edge-case effort should be re-scoped, deferred, or parked for a later phase.

## Slide 36/52 - Identify rollout assumptions

Rollout assumptions describe what the team currently believes, but has not fully confirmed. At this stage, assumptions should be made visible, not hidden inside the plan.

Common rollout assumptions include: first workflow, first user group, required source material or internal knowledge, governance or review requirements, required ChatGPT experience and any related Company Knowledge, App, Workspace Agent, identity, access, connection, action, or approval dependencies, enablement and adoption needs, measurement approach, context required for the first workflow, where activity, outputs, usage, feedback, and risks will be monitored, who owns review, escalation, and improvement after first use.

Each assumption should be marked as confirmed, likely, or unresolved:

- **Confirmed** — the customer has agreed, assigned, documented, or validated it.
- **Likely** — it is plausible and useful for planning, but still needs confirmation.
- **Unresolved** — it could materially affect the motion and should be resolved before launch planning advances.

Example assumption table:

- "Customer success managers are the first user group." — Likely. The seller mentioned this group, but the business owner has not confirmed the cohort.
- "Renewal brief drafts are the first workflow." — Likely. The workflow fits the business problem, but the exact brief format is not yet confirmed.
- "Account notes and support history are approved sources." — Unresolved. Source access and policy boundaries must be validated before the workflow can be planned.
- "VP of Customer Success owns the result." — Unresolved. The VP is supportive, but ownership of the business result has not been confirmed.

A good Deployment Brief does not pretend that assumptions are facts. It makes the assumptions visible so the team can choose the right starting motion.

## Slide 37/52 - Compare pilot, phased rollout, and broader deployment

The starting motion should match the evidence.

**A pilot** is usually appropriate when: the workflow is promising but still needs validation; the user group is small enough to support closely; governance or source-access questions need controlled testing; the customer needs proof before expanding; the team is prepared for a controlled motion that produces credible evidence for the next customer decision.

**A phased rollout** may be appropriate when: the workflow is clear and owned, but adoption, governance, or support readiness should expand in stages; the customer wants to sequence cohorts, functions, regions, or workflows rather than launch broadly at once; the team needs staged evidence before each expansion decision; different groups need different enablement, support, or governance validation before expansion.

**A broader deployment motion** may be appropriate when: the workflow and users are clear; the customer has aligned owners; workspace and policy readiness are understood; success criteria and launch scope are specific; the customer is ready to plan enablement, adoption, and measurement at a broader scale.

**A focused validation step** may be appropriate when the workflow has potential, but the team still needs to confirm one or two important assumptions before choosing a pilot or broader deployment motion. Examples: first user group, source or access dependency, governance requirement, measurable value signal, business owner, workspace owner, review expectation.

**A targeted re-scope** may be appropriate when the opportunity is too broad, under-owned, or disconnected from a measurable workflow.

**A no-go or defer decision** may be appropriate when a non-negotiable gate is missing.

The goal is not to force every opportunity into a pilot. The goal is to choose the motion that fits the evidence.

## Slide 38/52 - Check constraints before recommending the motion

Before recommending the starting motion, check whether unresolved constraints could block deployment planning.

Common constraints include: workspace ownership or admin availability; user provisioning and access model; Company Knowledge, file, App, or other connected-context access; supported devices, SSO, or workspace access path; required ChatGPT experience or Workspace Agent availability for the first cohort; sensitive or regulated data boundaries, such as PHI or customer-specific policy restrictions; legal, security, or compliance review; human review requirements for ChatGPT outputs; user readiness and manager support; monitoring or reporting expectations; auditability or evidence requirements; approval steps for outputs and connected actions before they affect customers, systems, or business decisions.

Do not try and solve all constraints yet. Your job for now is to decide whether they are: already resolved, manageable during planning, important enough to require focused validation, serious enough to require re-scope or deferral.

A clear starting-motion recommendation names the blocking constraints instead of burying them.

## Slide 39/52 - Apply the starting-motion decision gate

The starting-motion decision gate helps you avoid mixing two different decisions:

1. **Readiness decision** — Is the opportunity ready to proceed, or does it need validation, re-scope, or deferral?
2. **Rollout structure** — If it is ready to proceed, what structure should the next motion use?

Choose the readiness decision first. Name the rollout structure only when the opportunity is ready to proceed.

**Step 1: Choose the readiness decision**

- **Proceed** — the Deployment Brief has enough confirmed evidence, ownership, governance readiness, and measurement visibility to move into deployment success planning.
- **Focused validation** — the opportunity is promising, but one or two specific assumptions must be tested before choosing a pilot, phased rollout, or broader deployment motion.
- **Targeted re-scope** — the opportunity is too broad or under-owned, so the team should narrow the workflow, cohort, owner, success criteria, or data/source scope before planning advances.
- **No-go or defer** — a non-negotiable gate is missing, such as executive sponsorship, a decision moment, workspace/admin path, identity/access readiness, required review, approved source/app dependency, operating cadence, or measurement approach.

**Step 2: If the decision is "Proceed," name the rollout structure**

- **Pilot** — use when the workflow is clear enough to run with defined users, evidence, safeguards, and review points, but the customer needs proof before expanding.
- **Phased rollout** — use when the workflow is clear and owned, but adoption, governance, support, or regional readiness should expand in stages.
- **Broader deployment planning** — use when workflow, users, ownership, workspace and policy readiness, success criteria, and launch scope are specific enough to support broader planning.

This keeps the decision practical. The goal is not to force every opportunity into a pilot. The goal is to choose the starting motion that fits the evidence.

## Slide 40/52 - Starting motion: real-world example

A customer success team has a clear renewal-brief workflow, a named business owner, and a small group of account managers. However, source-access questions are unresolved.

A weak recommendation would say: "Proceed to deployment because the use case is clear." That skips a critical dependency.

A stronger recommendation would say: "Use focused validation before pilot design. Confirm which account notes, support history, and usage signals are approved for the first workflow. Once source access and review expectations are confirmed, proceed to a small pilot with the first account manager cohort."

In another situation, the workflow may be too broad. For example: "Use ChatGPT for all account planning." This is not yet a first-motion use case.

The better next step is targeted re-scope: "Narrow the opportunity to one account-planning workflow, one user group, one business owner, and one evidence plan before choosing the rollout motion."

The starting motion should match the readiness evidence.

## Slide 45/52 - What the Deployment Brief includes

You have now built the main parts of the deployment-readiness decision. You inspected the handoff. You translated the opportunity into a deployment-grade problem statement. You confirmed stakeholders and scope. You checked use case quality, assumptions, constraints, and the starting motion.

Now you will consolidate those decisions into the main course output: a ChatGPT Deployment Brief.

The key question is: can this brief help a deployment team move from a qualified opportunity to the right next planning action?

A strong ChatGPT Deployment Brief includes the following sections:

1. **Deployment problem statement** — names the workflow problem, affected users, operational impact, intended ChatGPT-supported workflow, and business outcome.
2. **Success criteria** — names the adoption, feedback, and workflow-value evidence that will help the customer decide what happens after the first motion.
3. **Stakeholder and scope section** — names confirmed owners, required decision-makers, fixed decision moments, and in-scope and out-of-scope boundaries. Product experience and control dependencies should also be explicit: name the intended ChatGPT experience and any required Company Knowledge, Apps, Workspace Agent, identity, access, connection, action, approval, or human-review dependencies that still need validation.
4. **Use case quality check** — explains whether the use case is real, high-friction, observable, suited to the first cohort, supported by manageable dependencies, and capable of producing evidence.
5. **Starting-motion decision gate** — states whether the recommendation is proceed, focused validation, targeted re-scope, or no-go or defer, with rationale.
6. **Rollout structure** — explains whether the next motion is a pilot, focused validation, or broader deployment plan, when the decision gate supports moving forward.
7. **Open risks** — names what must be resolved before launch planning proceeds.
8. **Immediate next step** — names the meeting, decision, or artifact needed next.
9. **Observability and ownership** — explains how activity, outputs, feedback, issues, and next-step decisions will be reviewed during the first motion.

The Deployment Brief should not be a long implementation plan. It should be a concise deployment starting artifact.

## Slide 46/52 - Keep the brief useful for deployment planning

A useful brief should help the next team or customer stakeholder understand what to do next.

It should clarify: launch scope, success criteria, owners, evidence needs, constraints, open readiness items, intended ChatGPT experience and product, access, or control dependencies, immediate next action.

It should not include: detailed workspace setup, full activation plan, enablement messaging, executive readout content, product configuration instructions, detailed measurement execution, broad feature exploration, unsupported value claims.

A good test is this: could a deployment lead use this brief to plan the next customer conversation without rereading the entire sales history?

If the answer is no, the brief needs more clarity. If the brief is long but still unclear, it needs sharpening.
