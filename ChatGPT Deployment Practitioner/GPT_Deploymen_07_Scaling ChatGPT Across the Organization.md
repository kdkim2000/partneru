## Slide 7/43 - Start from the scaling decision

Before you prioritize expansion areas, review the latest activation review or expansion recommendation. This may come from an Activation Review and Expansion Plan, approved customer handoff, sanitized customer example, or course practice example.

The Activation Review and Expansion Plan should tell you whether the customer should:

- Continue the current workflow while gathering more evidence.
- Adjust the workflow, enablement, source guidance, review process, or support model before expansion.
- Expand because adoption, early value evidence, readiness, support, and governance expectations are strong enough.
- Pause because unresolved risk, lack of ownership, weak evidence, or governance concerns make continuation or expansion irresponsible.

A strong scaling strategy helps the customer move from early evidence to broader adoption without multiplying unresolved issues.

That does not mean every issue is resolved. It means the customer has enough evidence to make a responsible scaling decision.

Ask:

- What evidence supports expansion?
- What evidence is still directional?
- What issues were resolved after activation?
- What issues remain open but manageable?
- Which expansion option builds on what has already worked?
- Which expansion option would multiply an unresolved issue?

## Slide 8/43 - Identify potential expansion areas

Once there is enough evidence to consider scale, identify possible expansion areas.

Common expansion areas include:

- **Adjacent user groups:** Users in a similar role, team, or region who perform the same or closely related workflow.
- **Similar workflows:** A workflow that uses a similar input, output, review pattern, or source set.
- **Additional teams or departments:** A team with related needs and enough readiness to adopt without redesigning the deployment.
- **Business functions with related needs:** A broader function where the same workflow pattern can be reused with appropriate context, ownership, and governance.
- **Regions or populations with readiness signals:** A new geography, business unit, or user population that has stakeholder support, source readiness, and support capacity.

The safest scaling candidate is often close to the workflow that has already produced evidence.

For example, if regional operations managers successfully used ChatGPT to create human-reviewed procedure summaries from approved SOP documents, a strong next candidate may be another operations region using the same SOP structure, review process, and champion model.

A riskier candidate might be a customer-facing incident response workflow. That could introduce new data boundaries, higher review expectations, different risk owners, and a different support model.

Scale should build from evidence, not pressure.

## Slide 9/43 - Review scaling criteria

Use a consistent set of criteria to compare expansion opportunities.

- **Business relevance:** Does the expansion area connect to a meaningful customer priority? Is the workflow important enough to justify broader rollout?
- **Organizational and context readiness:** Does the team have the right business context, source material, workflow guidance, and stakeholder support? Are the required policies, documents, systems, or knowledge sources ready enough for use?
- **Operational feasibility:** Can the customer support this expansion with the available people, timing, enablement resources, champions, and review capacity?
- **Expected impact:** Is there a credible reason to believe the expansion will improve effort, quality, consistency, speed, risk, customer experience, or another customer-approved outcome?
- **Governance and review readiness:** Are acceptable-use expectations, source boundaries, review requirements, and escalation paths clear enough for the next audience?
- **Observability or feedback visibility:** Can the customer see what is happening as adoption expands? Can the team review activity, feedback, issues, risks, and improvement actions?

Observability matters because scale creates more activity, more variation, and more potential for issues to become harder to detect. In learner-facing terms, observability means how the business monitors, controls, and improves AI-supported work as it grows.

A strong expansion candidate does not need to be perfect. It does need to be ready enough that broader rollout is likely to increase value rather than increase confusion.

## Slide 14/43 - Introduction (Module: Define Ownership and Governance for Scale)

In this module, you'll define the ownership and governance needed to support broader adoption. As ChatGPT adoption expands, informal ownership is not enough. More teams, workflows, regions, and support needs mean more decisions to coordinate.

At scale, the customer needs to know:

- Who owns each expansion area.
- Who owns rollout coordination.
- Who reinforces governance expectations.
- Who manages support and escalation.
- Who reviews activity, feedback, risks, and improvement actions.
- Who decides whether to continue expanding.

Ownership and governance do not need to be heavy. They do need to be visible.

## Slide 15/43 - Review ownership considerations

Scaling requires a clear ownership model.

Review these ownership areas before recommending broader rollout:

- **Executive sponsorship for broader prioritization:** An executive sponsor helps maintain organizational alignment, prioritize expansion areas, and resolve competing demands. The sponsor does not need to manage day-to-day rollout, but they should support the scaling direction and decision cadence.
- **Business ownership for each expansion area:** Each team, function, region, or workflow needs a business owner who understands the work and owns the intended outcome.
- **Operational ownership for rollout coordination:** Someone needs to coordinate the rollout plan, timing, communications, enablement, dependency tracking, and decision checkpoints.
- **Workspace and product-control ownership:** Workspace or administrative owners should identify who maintains role-based and SCIM-managed group access, who receives Analytics Viewer access, who governs plugins and underlying Apps, Company Knowledge, and Workspace Agent building and publishing, who approves end-user or agent-owned shared connections and actions, and who may access compliance records where those capabilities are in scope. Exact availability and controls depend on the customer's plan and workspace configuration.
- **Champion-network leadership:** Champions help reinforce workflow use, answer peer questions, surface blockers, and share practical examples. At scale, champion coverage should be planned rather than assumed.
- **Escalation ownership:** Issues will appear during scale. The question is whether the customer knows where those issues go. Escalation ownership should cover adoption blockers, source questions, review issues, governance concerns, and support capacity constraints.

A scaling strategy should not say "the customer will own this." It should name the roles or groups that own each major part of the work.

## Slide 16/43 - Review governance considerations

Governance becomes more important as adoption expands. A workflow that is manageable in one cohort can become inconsistent when many teams adapt it differently. Governance helps the customer preserve trust, consistency, and appropriate oversight.

Review these governance considerations:

- **Policy consistency across user groups:** Are the same acceptable-use, data-use, review, and escalation expectations understood across teams?
- **Acceptable-use and human-review expectations:** Do users know when ChatGPT outputs must be reviewed before they affect customers, systems, business decisions, regulated processes, or operational changes?
- **Source or workflow boundaries:** Are users clear on which sources, documents, systems, or business context they should use? Are they clear on what is out of scope?
- **Risk and issue escalation:** Do users, managers, champions, and support teams know how to surface concerns?
- **Oversight and decision-making cadence:** How often will the customer review adoption, workflow quality, feedback, governance issues, and scaling readiness?
- **Auditability or evidence requirements:** What evidence does the customer need to show that the workflow is being used responsibly? This may include review samples, support logs, feedback records, decision notes, or other approved evidence sources.
- **Roles, groups, and analytics access:** Use role-based access and, where configured, SCIM-managed groups to align product access and reporting segments with approved rollout cohorts. Assign Analytics Viewer access to the appropriate owners, and confirm who maintains group membership as people join, move, or leave.
- **Plugins, Apps, and Company Knowledge:** Confirm which workflow capabilities and underlying connections or knowledge sources are approved, which roles or groups may use them, what source, domain, sync, or action boundaries apply, and who owns access, approvals, and source quality.
- **Workspace Agents and shared connections:** Confirm who may build, edit, publish, and run Workspace Agents; whether connected Apps use each end user's account or an agent-owned shared connection; which actions require confirmation or constraints; and who owns publishing, versions, and review.
- **Analytics and compliance records:** Use Task Insights and group-level analytics, where available, to identify adoption and task patterns that inform enablement and scaling decisions. Keep compliance records and logs separate: they support audit, investigation, retention, and policy review, while analytics do not by themselves prove output quality, compliance, or business value.

A practical governance review asks:

- What data, tools, or systems can users access for this workflow?
- Which permissions or approvals are required?
- Which roles or SCIM-managed groups should receive access, and who maintains membership?
- Are plugins, Apps, Company Knowledge, or Workspace Agents in scope, and who owns access, publishing, connection type, action controls, and approvals?
- Which adoption questions should use Analytics Viewer or Task Insights, and which audit, investigation, retention, or policy questions require compliance records?
- Who reviews outputs before they affect business decisions?
- How will policy adherence be checked?
- What reporting or evidence is needed?
- How will issues be escalated, corrected, and improved over time?

Governance should support the workflow. It should not become a separate checklist disconnected from how people actually work.

## Slide 17/43 - Establish governance expectations

After reviewing governance considerations, define the expectations that need to be reinforced during scale.

A useful governance summary should include:

- **Accountability:** Who owns workflow quality, source readiness, user behavior, output review, escalation, and scaling decisions?
- **Product-control ownership:** Who owns role and SCIM-group access, approved plugins and Apps, Company Knowledge, Workspace Agent publishing and connection choices, action approvals, Analytics Viewer access, and compliance-record access where relevant?
- **Escalation paths:** Where do users, champions, managers, or support teams send issues? Which issues go to business owners? Which issues go to governance, security, legal, IT, or compliance owners?
- **Governance reinforcement needs:** What needs to be repeated during onboarding, manager briefings, champion sessions, communications, and support activities?
- **Evidence boundaries:** Which adoption and task-pattern questions will be reviewed through Analytics Viewer or Task Insights, and which audit, investigation, retention, or policy questions require compliance records or logs?

For example: "Before expanding the procedure-summary workflow to the second operations region, Middleton should confirm that regional managers understand approved SOP source use, required human review, and escalation expectations. The operations workflow owner should own source guidance, regional managers should own output review, the champion lead should collect recurring issues, and the governance owner should attend the first expansion review checkpoint."

This statement is useful because it connects governance to the actual workflow, roles, and decision point.

## Slide 21/43 - Introduction (Module: Scale Enablement and Support)

In this module, you'll review how enablement, champion coverage, and support structures need to adapt for broader adoption. The enablement approach that works for one activation group may not be enough for a larger rollout.

When scale begins, the customer needs support that is repeatable. Users should not depend only on live sessions, informal coaching, or a small group of overextended champions.

A strong scaled support approach helps users understand:

- Which workflows are approved.
- How ChatGPT should support the work.
- What context and sources to use.
- Where human review is required.
- Where to get help.
- How to surface feedback, blockers, or issues.

## Slide 22/43 - Review enablement requirements

Scaled enablement should help users adopt ChatGPT inside real work, not just learn general product concepts.

Review these enablement requirements:

- **Repeatable onboarding approach:** New teams should receive a consistent introduction to the approved workflow, expected behaviors, source guidance, review requirements, and support routes.
- **Reusable workflow guidance:** The customer should have guidance that can be reused across teams. This may include workflow steps, example prompts, approved source instructions, output examples, review expectations, and escalation guidance.
- **Manager or champion enablement:** Managers and champions often reinforce adoption. They may need their own enablement so they can answer questions, model the workflow, and route issues.
- **Self-service resources:** As the rollout grows, users need resources they can access without waiting for a live session. These might include job aids, short demos, FAQs, example workflows, or support channel guidance.
- **Ongoing learning or refresh needs:** Scaling is not a one-time launch moment. Users may need reinforcement after the first week, after the first review checkpoint, or when the workflow changes.

For example: Middleton's first cohort needed repeated clarification about approved SOP sources. Before expanding, the team should update the workflow guidance so the second region does not repeat the same confusion.

Good success signals connect the selected workflow to observable behavior, review, and early outcome evidence.

## Slide 23/43 - Review champion network requirements

Champions can help scale adoption, but champion networks need structure.

Review these requirements:

- **Champion coverage by team or function:** Does each expansion area have enough champion coverage? A single champion may be enough for a small pilot, but not for multiple regions or functions.
- **Champion responsibilities:** Champions should know what they are expected to do. Responsibilities may include demonstrating the approved workflow, answering peer questions, collecting feedback, surfacing blockers, and reinforcing source and review expectations.
- **Community participation:** A community channel, recurring session, or shared support space can help champions learn from each other and prevent local teams from solving the same problem separately.
- **Feedback collection:** Champions often see adoption patterns before formal reports do. They need a clear way to capture recurring questions, workflow issues, user sentiment, and improvement ideas.
- **Escalation route for recurring adoption blockers:** Champions should not be expected to solve every issue. Source problems, review ownership gaps, governance concerns, or capacity constraints should route to the right owner.

Watch for champion-network fatigue. If the same people are expected to train users, answer questions, collect feedback, manage escalation, and support expansion without enough time or authority, scale may become fragile.

## Slide 24/43 - Review support requirements

Support structures also need to scale.

Review whether the customer has:

- **Office hours:** Scheduled sessions where users can ask workflow-specific questions and get help applying ChatGPT in context.
- **Community channels:** A shared place for questions, examples, updates, and recurring issues.
- **Support resources:** Job aids, FAQs, workflow guides, examples, decision trees, or short demo recordings.
- **Escalation mechanisms:** Clear routes for issues that champions or frontline support cannot resolve.

A support model should identify the types of questions users may ask and where each type should go.

For example:

- Workflow questions go to the champion channel.
- Source questions go to the workflow owner or source owner.
- Review questions go to the manager or business owner.
- Access questions go to the workspace admin or IT owner.
- Governance concerns go to the governance owner.
- Repeated adoption blockers go to the deployment owner for review.

Support is not only about answering questions. It is also a way to keep adoption visible as the customer scales.

## Slide 27/43 - Introduction (Module: Evaluate Scaling Risks and Dependencies)

In this module, you'll evaluate risks and dependencies that may affect broader organizational expansion.

Scaling often reveals issues that were manageable during a smaller activation. A source-quality issue that affected a handful of users may become a larger quality problem. A support channel that worked for one region may become overloaded. A review process that depended on one engaged manager may become inconsistent across teams.

A strong scaling strategy identifies these risks before rollout expands.

By the end of this module, you should be able to name common scaling risks, identify dependencies, and recommend mitigation actions.

## Slide 28/43 - Identify scaling risks

Review common scaling risks before recommending broader rollout.

- **Inconsistent adoption across teams:** Some teams may adopt the workflow quickly while others use ChatGPT only occasionally or outside the intended workflow moment.
- **Inconsistent source or context quality across teams:** Different teams may use different documents, outdated sources, incomplete context, or local materials that have not been approved.
- **Uneven manager support and review discipline:** Some managers may reinforce the workflow and review outputs. Others may not have time, clarity, or confidence to do so.
- **Governance gaps:** Acceptable-use boundaries, review expectations, source rules, or escalation paths may not be applied consistently.
- **Champion-network fatigue:** Champions may become overloaded as more teams ask for support.
- **Workflow quality variation:** Outputs may vary by team if guidance, source material, prompt patterns, or review expectations are inconsistent.
- **Resource constraints:** The customer may lack enough enablement time, support coverage, reviewer capacity, or operational attention.
- **Limited visibility into workflow activity or issues:** If feedback, support questions, review findings, and adoption signals are scattered, the customer may not see problems until they affect trust.

A useful risk statement connects the risk to the scaling decision.

Weak risk statement: "Governance could be an issue."

Stronger risk statement: "Expanding the procedure-summary workflow to a second region may create inconsistent review practices unless regional managers receive clear review expectations and the governance owner joins the first expansion checkpoint."

The stronger version points toward a mitigation.

## Slide 29/43 - Identify dependencies

A dependency is something that must be ready, confirmed, or resolved for scale to work.

Review these common dependencies:

- **Stakeholder support** — Are the right business, operational, and governance stakeholders aligned?
- **Governance readiness** — Are acceptable-use, source, review, and escalation expectations clear enough?
- **Support capacity** — Can the customer support more users without overwhelming champions or support teams?
- **Champion coverage** — Are champions available in the next team, function, or region?
- **Repeatable enablement resources** — Are onboarding materials, workflow guidance, examples, and support resources ready to reuse?
- **Organizational alignment** — Do executive priorities, business-unit goals, and rollout timing support the expansion?
- **Workspace and product-control dependencies** — Are role and SCIM-group access, approved plugins and Apps, Company Knowledge permissions, Workspace Agent publishing and connection choices, and action approvals ready for the next cohort where relevant?
- **Analytics approach** — Can the customer assign Analytics Viewer access and use Task Insights or SCIM-group segmentation, where available, to review adoption and task patterns, feedback, and improvement actions without treating usage as proof of quality or value?
- **Compliance evidence approach** — Which compliance records or logs are required for audit, investigation, retention, or policy review, and who is authorized to access them?
- **Issue-escalation and improvement loop** — Are issues routed to owners and reviewed in a cadence that leads to improvement?
- **Approved source or workflow guidance** — Do users know what to use, what not to use, and how to complete the workflow?

Dependencies do not always block scale. Some can be resolved before launch. Others can be monitored during a phased rollout. The important thing is to name them and assign ownership.

## Slide 30/43 - Plan mitigation approaches

Once you identify risks and dependencies, define mitigation actions.

Common mitigation approaches include:

- **Risk reduction activities:** Update workflow guidance, clarify source instructions, confirm review expectations, or reduce rollout scope.
- **Readiness reviews:** Hold a checkpoint before expansion to confirm source readiness, owner alignment, support capacity, and governance expectations.
- **Governance reinforcement:** Add governance reminders to onboarding, manager briefings, champion sessions, and support materials.
- **Champion and support capacity planning:** Add champions, adjust office hours, route support questions more clearly, or define which issues require escalation.

A useful mitigation action includes: the risk or dependency, the action, the owner, the timing, and the evidence or signal the team will review.

For example: "Support questions increased during the first activation. Before expanding to the second region, the champion lead should add a weekly office hour, update the FAQ with approved-source guidance, and review recurring questions after two weeks. If support volume continues to rise, the deployment owner should slow expansion until support capacity improves."

This protects the customer from scaling faster than the operating support model can handle.

## Slide 35/43 - Confirm scaling priorities

Start by confirming the scaling priorities.

This section should include:

- **Expansion targets:** Which teams, functions, workflows, regions, or user populations are being considered for expansion?
- **Rollout priorities:** Which expansion areas should come first, second, or later? Which should not move forward yet?
- **Scaling objectives:** What is the customer trying to achieve through broader adoption?

For example: "Middleton should prioritize expanding the approved procedure-summary workflow to a second operations region before introducing new functions or customer-facing workflows. This expansion builds on first-cohort evidence, uses a similar SOP source structure, and supports the COO's objective of improving operational consistency. The rollout should remain phased until second-region enablement, review ownership, and champion support are confirmed."

This section should make clear why the selected expansion priority is stronger than other options.

## Slide 36/43 - Confirm governance and ownership

Next, confirm the ownership and governance model.

This section should include:

- **Ownership model:** Who owns the scaling decision, each expansion area, rollout coordination, workspace or admin dependencies, champions, support, review, and escalation?
- **Governance considerations:** What source, context, acceptable-use, review, escalation, policy, or evidence expectations need to be reinforced?
- **Oversight requirements:** What review cadence will the customer use to evaluate adoption, workflow quality, support issues, governance concerns, and scaling readiness?
- **Observability approach:** How will activity, feedback, risks, and improvement actions be reviewed as adoption expands?
- **Operating controls:** Which roles or SCIM-managed groups should receive access? Which plugins, underlying Apps, Company Knowledge sources, or Workspace Agents are in scope? Who owns publishing, connection type, action approvals, and control changes?
- **Analytics and compliance boundary:** Who receives Analytics Viewer access, how will Task Insights or group segmentation inform the scale decision, and which audit, investigation, retention, or policy questions require separate compliance records or logs?

For example: "Middleton should hold a two-week expansion checkpoint after the second region begins using the procedure-summary workflow. The workspace owner should confirm the approved group for the cohort and, if the workflow uses plugins, Apps, Company Knowledge, or a Workspace Agent, confirm access, publishing, connection type, action, and approval controls before rollout. The checkpoint should review adoption and task patterns through Analytics Viewer or Task Insights where available, manager-reviewed output samples, support questions, source-use issues, and unresolved escalation items. Compliance records should be reviewed separately when a policy, audit, retention, or investigation question requires them. The operations workflow owner, regional manager, workspace owner, champion lead, governance owner, and partner deployment lead should attend."

This keeps scale connected to operating visibility, not just rollout activity.

## Slide 37/43 - Confirm support requirements

Next, confirm how the expansion will be enabled and supported.

This section should include:

- **Enablement approach:** What onboarding, workflow guidance, examples, manager briefing, or refresh activity is needed?
- **Champion network approach:** Which champions will support which teams? What are they responsible for? How will they collect feedback and route blockers?
- **Support structures:** What office hours, channels, resources, FAQs, or escalation mechanisms will support users?

For example: "Before second-region rollout, Middleton should update the procedure-summary job aid with approved SOP guidance, examples of region-specific safety notes, required manager review steps, and the champion support route. The champion lead should run one pre-launch session for second-region managers and two office-hour sessions during the first two weeks of expansion."

This helps the customer scale what worked in the first activation while correcting what did not.

## Slide 38/43 - Confirm scaling recommendation and next actions

The final section of the strategy recommends the scaling approach and next actions.

Common scaling approaches include:

- **Phased expansion:** Roll out to one adjacent team, region, or workflow at a time, with review checkpoints before the next expansion.
- **Repeat and extend:** Repeat the successful workflow in a similar context, then extend to related workflows after evidence and readiness improve.
- **Adjust before scaling:** Resolve specific barriers before expansion, such as source guidance, review ownership, support capacity, or governance clarity.
- **Hold expansion:** Delay broader rollout when evidence, readiness, ownership, governance, or support is too weak.

The recommendation should include: recommended scaling approach, risks and dependencies, owners, next actions, timing or decision checkpoint, and evidence to review.

For example: "The recommended scaling approach is phased expansion. Middleton should expand the procedure-summary workflow to one additional operations region after second-region enablement, manager review expectations, champion support coverage, and the approved cohort group are confirmed. If the workflow uses plugins, Apps, Company Knowledge, or a Workspace Agent, the workspace and governance owners should confirm access, publishing, connection type, action, and approval controls before rollout. The deployment team should not yet expand into safety incident summaries or customer-facing response drafting because those options introduce new governance, review, and risk considerations.

The next checkpoint should occur two weeks after rollout begins and should review adoption and task patterns through Analytics Viewer or Task Insights where available, manager-reviewed outputs, support questions, source-use issues, and unresolved governance concerns. Compliance records should be reviewed separately if a policy, audit, retention, or investigation issue is raised."

This recommendation is specific, evidence-based, and practical.
