## Slide 6/59 - What success planning makes concrete

A success plan turns a deployment-ready opportunity into a practical launch frame.

Success planning connects the customer's AI vision to: a priority workflow; a first user group or cohort; the intended ChatGPT experience and supported surface, plus any required plugins, apps, Workspace Agents, connected tools, or organizational context; clear launch ownership; practical KPIs and evidence sources; readiness and governance assumptions; a 30-day sequence of launch actions; handoff notes for setup and activation.

This matters because deployment planning should not become a generic task list.

Each launch action should support the workflow, clarify ownership, produce useful evidence, validate a readiness or governance assumption, or prepare the next customer decision.

By the end of the plan, the team should understand what will launch first, who owns each part of the launch, what evidence will be reviewed, which assumptions still need validation, and what decision the customer should make after the first 30 days.

## Slide 7/59 - Planning principle

Use this planning principle throughout the course: connect every launch action to workflow, owner, KPI, readiness dependency, governance assumption, or next-step decision.

This principle keeps planning from becoming a disconnected checklist.

A checklist can tell a team what tasks exist. A success plan explains why each task matters, who owns it, what evidence it should produce, and how it supports the customer's next decision.

Before adding an action to the plan, ask: which workflow does this support? Who owns it? What evidence or decision does it support? Does it validate a readiness or governance assumption?

If an action does not connect to at least one of those answers, it may be outside the launch plan or belong in a later workstream.

## Slide 10/59 - Introduction (Module: Define the deployment vision and launch scope)

A success plan begins with a clear deployment vision and launch scope.

The deployment vision explains why ChatGPT is being introduced and what the first launch should prove. The launch scope explains where the first launch begins, who is included, what context is needed, which governance assumptions matter, and how the launch will be measured.

This module turns the Deployment Brief into a practical planning frame. It helps you avoid treating the plan as a general project checklist by anchoring each planning decision in the customer's workflow and intended outcome.

The key question is: what specific workflow should the customer activate first, and what should the first 30 days prove?

## Slide 11/59 - Define the deployment vision

A deployment vision should be short, specific, and useful. It should explain why ChatGPT is being deployed, where it will support work, and what the first launch should prove.

A useful deployment vision connects six elements: customer business priority; priority workflow; first user group or cohort; intended workflow improvement; success evidence for the first launch period; governance expectations that shape how ChatGPT will be used.

A weak deployment vision sounds like general access: "Launch ChatGPT for the legal team."

A stronger deployment vision connects AI vision to workflow: "Use ChatGPT to help the legal operations team prepare human-reviewed summaries for routine vendor-contract intake, so the team can reduce review friction, improve consistency, and gather evidence on summary quality, user confidence, and review efficiency during the first 30 days."

The stronger version makes the launch concrete. It names the team, workflow, output, value direction, evidence, and review expectation.

## Slide 12/59 - Move from vision to workflow

A customer's AI vision is often broad. That is normal at the start of deployment planning.

For example, a customer might say: "We want to use AI to make legal operations faster and more consistent."

That statement is useful, but it is not yet a launch plan. You need to translate it into a workflow that can be activated, supported, measured, and governed.

Use this bridge:

- What business priority is driving the vision? — Reduce legal operations friction and improve consistency.
- Which workflow will activate the vision first? — Routine vendor-contract intake summaries.
- Who will use ChatGPT first? — A defined legal operations cohort.
- What will users produce? — Human-reviewed contract intake summaries.
- What evidence will show progress? — Summary completeness, review time, user confidence, and manager feedback.
- What governance expectation shapes the workflow? — Approved source materials, human review, sensitive-data boundaries, and escalation for uncertain cases.

This bridge keeps planning practical. It shows how an AI vision becomes a workflow-specific launch plan.

## Slide 13/59 - Set the launch scope

Launch scope defines what the first launch includes and what it does not include.

Your launch scope should define: first workflow; first user group or cohort; intended ChatGPT experience and supported surface—for example, ChatGPT Chat or ChatGPT Work on web, mobile, or desktop—where this affects readiness; initial business unit or function; customer-side owner; partner-side owner; known dependencies; first validation period; required business context, such as approved knowledge sources, policies, systems, or workflow guidance; required plugins, apps, Workspace Agents, or connected tools, including who can access them and what approvals are needed; governance expectations that must be validated before or during launch; measurement approach for the first launch period.

The scope should be narrow enough to activate but meaningful enough to prove value.

Too narrow, and the launch may not create useful evidence. Too broad, and the team may lose control of ownership, measurement, and governance.

A strong launch scope answers: where exactly will ChatGPT support work? Who will participate first? Who owns the workflow and the result? What is intentionally out of scope? What must be validated before activation? What decision will the customer make after the first launch period?

## Slide 14/59 - Protect against scope drift

Scope drift happens when the launch starts expanding before the plan is stable. It can make the launch look ambitious while making it harder to measure, govern, and support.

Common scope drift patterns include: adding every adjacent workflow to the launch; treating all employees as the first user group; expanding into admin setup decisions before scope is stable; turning the launch into a general AI enablement program; measuring usage without tying it back to the original workflow; separating governance from the workflow instead of planning governance expectations around how ChatGPT will actually be used; creating a 30-day checklist that does not connect to owners, KPIs, or decision points.

A good way to test for scope drift is to ask: "Can every launch action be traced to the priority workflow, owner, KPI, readiness dependency, governance assumption, or next-step decision?"

When the answer is no, the plan is probably drifting.

## Slide 15/59 - Launch planning example

A legal operations team wants ChatGPT to help summarize vendor contracts faster.

A weak plan would say: "Launch ChatGPT for the legal team and train users on summaries."

A stronger plan would say: "Launch ChatGPT for a defined legal operations cohort to prepare human-reviewed summaries for routine vendor-contract intake from approved materials. The first 30 days will test whether summaries are complete, useful, reviewed appropriately, and trusted enough for continued use."

The launch scope should start with: one contract-review workflow; one approved legal operations user group; one customer-side workflow owner; one partner-side deployment owner; a small set of success signals, such as summary completeness, review time, and user confidence; clear assumptions about source approval, human review, sensitive-data boundaries, and escalation routes.

The launch should not immediately expand to all legal knowledge work. That expansion may become appropriate later, but only after the first workflow produces useful evidence and the readiness assumptions are validated.

## Slide 19/59 - Introduction (Module: Identify the launch team and operating rhythm)

A launch scope is only useful if the right people own the right decisions.

This module turns stakeholder alignment into a practical launch team model. You'll identify who owns the workflow, who owns readiness, who validates governance assumptions, who reviews KPI evidence, and who escalates blockers.

The key question is: who needs to do what during launch, and how will the team stay aligned without turning the plan into unnecessary project management?

## Slide 20/59 - Name the core launch team

A ChatGPT deployment launch team may include several roles. For smaller divisional deployments, one person may hold more than one role. The plan should still make the responsibilities visible.

- **Executive sponsor** — supports prioritization, visibility, and executive alignment.
- **Business outcome owner** — owns success for the target workflow and the launch decision after the first period.
- **Workflow owner or team lead** — coordinates practical user needs and confirms how the workflow should run.
- **Workspace owner or admin** — supports access, settings, and workspace readiness decisions.
- **Champion or champion-network lead** — supports peer advocacy, early feedback, and practical examples without owning technical setup.
- **HR/L&D or enablement lead** — coordinates user readiness inputs and training dependencies.
- **IT, security, legal, compliance, or data stakeholders** — validate governance, source access, policy considerations, and risk expectations.
- **Partner deployment lead** — coordinates the launch plan and partner actions.

Not every role needs to attend every meeting. But every critical responsibility should have an owner.

## Slide 21/59 - Clarify responsibilities

The responsibility model should answer five ownership questions: who owns the workflow? Who owns readiness? Who owns governance validation? Who owns measurement review? Who owns next-step decisions?

Assign responsibilities for: confirming launch scope; validating access and workspace readiness; approving governance assumptions; preparing user-facing enablement inputs; monitoring launch progress; reviewing early success signals; reviewing workflow outputs, user feedback, and issue patterns; escalating blockers.

A common mistake is to name stakeholders but not decisions. A stronger plan states what each person or role must decide, validate, review, or escalate.

## Slide 22/59 - Set the launch operating rhythm

The operating rhythm is the lightweight cadence that keeps the launch moving. It should make ownership visible without adding unnecessary project-management detail.

- **Kickoff meeting** — Confirm vision, scope, roles, first workflow, and launch decision point. Owners: executive sponsor, business outcome owner, workflow owner, partner deployment lead.
- **Setup readiness checkpoint** — Confirm the selected ChatGPT experience and supported surface, workspace and access readiness, source and product dependencies, plan or regional availability, admin enablement, and governance assumptions. Owners: workspace owner, IT/security/legal/compliance owners, partner deployment lead.
- **Activation checkpoint** — Confirm users, enablement inputs, acceptable-use reminders, feedback route, and support route. Owners: workflow owner, champion lead, enablement lead, partner deployment lead.
- **Early feedback review** — Review whether users are trying the workflow and what friction appears. Owners: workflow owner, champion lead, business outcome owner.
- **Governance or issue review checkpoint** — Review source-use, sharing, review behavior, escalation issues, and unresolved risks. Owners: governance stakeholders, workspace owner, workflow owner.
- **30-day review** — Review KPI evidence and decide whether to continue, adjust, expand, or pause. Owners: executive sponsor, business outcome owner, workflow owner, partner deployment lead.

The operating rhythm is the lightweight cadence that keeps the launch moving. It should make ownership visible without adding unnecessary project-management detail.

## Slide 27/59 - Introduction (Module: Convert success criteria into KPIs)

A strong success plan needs practical KPIs. But KPIs should not be generic usage targets disconnected from the customer's workflow.

In this module, you'll translate success criteria into launch measures that connect the customer's vision and priority workflow to evidence, review points, and next-step decisions.

The key question is: what evidence will help the customer decide whether to continue, adjust, expand, or pause after the first launch period?

## Slide 28/59 - Start from the workflow outcome

KPIs should connect to the workflow and launch goal.

For the legal operations example, the launch goal is not simply "increase ChatGPT usage." The goal is to test whether ChatGPT can help a defined user group prepare useful, human-reviewed vendor-contract intake summaries from approved materials.

Useful KPI categories include:

- **Adoption** — whether the target users are actively trying the workflow.
- **Engagement** — whether usage continues beyond first exposure.
- **Workflow output** — whether users produce the intended draft, summary, analysis, or decision-support output.
- **Quality** — whether outputs are complete, useful, and reviewed appropriately.
- **Efficiency** — whether the workflow feels faster or less burdensome.
- **Confidence or sentiment** — whether users and managers trust the new workflow enough to continue.
- **Governance signal** — whether users follow expected review, source-use, sharing, and escalation behaviors during the launch period.

If baseline data does not exist, the plan should define how to gather directional evidence before or during launch.

For example, the legal operations team may not know the exact current review time for every contract type. That does not block planning.

## Slide 30/59 - Define leading and lagging signals

Leading signals help the launch team see whether activation is starting: users join the workspace or launch cohort; users complete enablement; users try the target workflow; managers observe early behavior change; users follow human-review and source-use expectations.

Lagging signals help the team judge whether the workflow is producing value: reduced time spent on the task; improved consistency or quality; fewer revision cycles; better stakeholder confidence; stronger adoption over the first 30 days; clearer decision evidence for whether to continue, adjust, expand, or pause.

A good success plan usually includes both. Leading signals show whether the launch is moving. Lagging signals show whether the workflow is worth continuing or expanding.

## Slide 31/59 - Tie KPIs to available evidence

Avoid KPIs the customer cannot reasonably observe.

Each KPI should have: a clear definition; a likely data source or feedback source; an owner; a review point; a limitation or assumption if evidence is incomplete.

Workspace analytics may support some adoption and engagement questions, but plan-specific access and dashboard details must be validated against current OpenAI documentation during drafting.

Treat workspace analytics as adoption and engagement evidence, not as proof of workflow quality, workflow value, compliance, or causal ROI.

Workflow quality and value measures should pair analytics, where available, with surveys, manager review, output sampling, operational data, or customer-approved feedback sources.

Raw-log, eDiscovery, legal, security, or compliance evidence should be routed to the appropriate customer owner and current OpenAI compliance documentation rather than assumed from workspace analytics.

## Slide 32/59 - KPI evidence map

Use a KPI evidence map to make measurement practical:

- **Workflow use** — Measure: legal operations users use ChatGPT for vendor-contract intake summaries. Evidence: workspace data, if available; user check-ins; manager confirmation. Owner: workflow owner; partner deployment lead. Review: early feedback; Day 30. Caveat: analytics access and reporting detail require validation.
- **Summary quality** — Measure: summaries are complete enough for human review and follow the expected structure. Evidence: sample review; manager checklist; reviewer notes. Owner: workflow owner. Review: Week 4. Caveat: usage alone does not show quality.
- **User confidence** — Measure: users find the workflow useful enough to continue. Evidence: pulse survey; interviews; office-hours notes. Owner: champion or enablement lead. Review: Weeks 3 and 4. Caveat: early feedback is directional.
- **Governance behavior** — Measure: users follow source, review, sharing, and escalation rules. Evidence: issue log; manager feedback; governance review. Owner: governance and workflow owners. Review: governance or issue review. Caveat: planning signal, not compliance certification.

This table keeps KPIs tied to workflow, evidence, owner, and review timing.

## Slide 36/59 - Introduction (Module: Capture readiness and governance assumptions)

A launch plan should not treat readiness and governance as separate checklists. They should be connected to how ChatGPT will actually be used in the priority workflow.

In this module, you'll document the conditions that must be true for the launch to proceed responsibly. You'll connect readiness and governance assumptions to workflow behavior, owners, timing, launch actions, and first-month review evidence.

The key question is: what assumptions must be validated so this workflow can launch responsibly?

## Slide 37/59 - Identify readiness checkpoints

Readiness should cover what ChatGPT needs to know, where it will be used in the workflow, who will use it, and how activity and outcomes will be reviewed.

Capture readiness checkpoints as planning assumptions in three groups:

1. **Launch ownership and user readiness** — confirmed launch owner; target users; enablement needs; feedback route; review timing.
2. **Workspace and regional requirements** — plan and workspace path; region requirements; access path; data residency or inference residency requirements that must be validated through official documentation and customer owners.
3. **ChatGPT experience, source, plugin, app, Workspace Agent, and governance dependencies** — intended ChatGPT experience and supported surface; plan, region, workspace, and role availability for the selected experience; internal sources or Company Knowledge; plugins, apps, Workspace Agents, and permissions; admin enablement, tool or app action boundaries, and required user approvals, where relevant; sensitive-data expectations; human review expectations; which dependencies are required for the first launch and which can be deferred to setup readiness.

Each readiness checkpoint should connect back to the priority workflow. The plan should explain which items are required for the first workflow and which can wait.

## Slide 38/59 - Capture governance assumptions

Governance assumptions should be framed as assumptions to validate, not universal ChatGPT rules.

Possible governance assumptions include: data sensitivity and acceptable-use boundaries; human review expectations; source material or app approval requirements; GPT, project, or workspace-sharing expectations; restrictions on sensitive or regulated data, such as customer-specific PHI or confidential-data boundaries; limits on automations, sharing, or source-backed workflows during the first motion; legal, compliance, or security review needs; workspace policy or user guidance requirements; monitoring and reporting expectations; auditability or evidence requirements; issue escalation and improvement process; approval expectations before outputs affect customers, systems, or business decisions.

If an assumption involves plugins, apps, Company Knowledge, Workspace Agents, custom apps, source-backed workflows, sharing, actions, or automations, the plan should name the customer owner who will confirm availability, admin enablement, permissions, approval boundaries, data boundaries, and whether external app data handling changes the risk profile.

## Slide 39/59 - Connect governance to the launch plan

A success plan should show how governance expectations will be used during launch. Avoid listing governance assumptions without connecting them to workflow behavior, ownership, or measurement.

If a workflow depends on ChatGPT Work, a Workspace Agent, a plugin, or an app, treat the selected surface, plan and regional availability, workspace or role access, admin enablement, connected-tool permissions, action boundaries, and required approvals as assumptions to validate—not as confirmed capabilities.

For each major governance assumption, identify: which workflow behavior it affects; who owns validation; when validation must happen; what launch action depends on it; what evidence or feedback will be reviewed during the first 30 days.

Examples:

- **"Only approved contract materials may be used"** — Workflow behavior: drafting vendor-contract intake summaries. Owner: legal operations owner; compliance stakeholder. Timing: before activation. Launch dependency: activate the first cohort with approved-source guidance. Review evidence: sample review and issue log.
- **"Human review is required before use"** — Workflow behavior: using summaries in legal intake. Owner: workflow owner. Timing: before activation; Week 4. Launch dependency: publish review guidance and checklist. Review evidence: completed checklists and manager feedback.
- **"Uncertain or sensitive cases must be escalated"** — Workflow behavior: handling unusual terms or sensitive data. Owner: legal or compliance owner. Timing: before launch. Launch dependency: set the escalation path and user guidance. Review evidence: escalation log and issue trends.

This connects governance to the workflow instead of treating it as a separate administrative topic.

## Slide 40/59 - Identify escalation points

Escalation should be based on concrete blockers, not general uncertainty.

The plan should identify when to escalate to: customer workspace owner or admin; IT or security; legal or compliance; OpenAI account or technical support; partner leadership.

For the legal operations example, escalation may be needed when users encounter unapproved source materials, sensitive data, unclear review expectations, app or source-access issues, or output quality concerns that could affect customer-owned decisions.

A good escalation note states the trigger, owner, and expected response.

An actionable governance checkpoint identifies the workflow behavior being validated, the responsible owner, the timing, the evidence required, and the launch action that depends on the result. A general review or workspace-setup confirmation does not replace workflow-specific governance validation.

## Slide 44/59 - Introduction (Module: Build the 30-day launch plan)

The 30-day launch plan turns the success plan into a time-bound view of what happens next.

A weak 30-day plan is just a list of tasks. A strong 30-day plan explains how the team moves from readiness to activation to feedback and next-step decision.

In this module, you'll build a practical 30-day plan that connects AI vision, workflow activation, ownership, governance, measurement, and review decisions.

The key question is: How should the first 30 days sequence readiness, activation, evidence review, and the next customer decision?

## Slide 45/59 - Structure the 30-day plan

A simple 30-day launch plan should include four phases:

- **Week 1 — Strategic alignment and readiness confirmation** — Confirm business goals, launch scope, owners, success metrics, access path, workflow context, and governance validation needs.
- **Week 2 — Workspace and access readiness** — Confirm setup dependencies, owner readiness, the selected ChatGPT experience and supported surface, and plan or workspace availability. Validate admin enablement, source, plugin, app, or Workspace Agent paths, permission and approval assumptions, and the legal, security, or compliance review path.
- **Week 3 — User readiness and activation preparation** — Prepare and upskill users, support champions or managers, reinforce acceptable-use reminders, confirm human-review expectations, and prepare early feedback collection.
- **Week 4 — Reinforcement, feedback review, and next-step decision** — Reinforce repeatable workflow use. Review progress, governance observations, feedback, issue patterns, output quality, and improvement actions before deciding whether to continue, adjust, expand, or pause.

For a first launch or validation motion, the 30-day plan should align with the agreed launch or validation window and customer decision moments.

Access setup should not be described as compliance by itself. Compliance remains an assumption to validate with the customer's policy, data, legal, security, compliance, and workspace owners.

The diagram shows a 4-step cycle (Week 1 Confirm planning foundations, Week 2 Validate readiness, Week 3 Activate the first workflow, Week 4 Review evidence and decide next step), each step tagged with Owner/Dependency/Evidence/Decision point, with a loop back to "iterate and repeat the cycle for the next workflow or expansion area."

## Slide 46/59 - Link actions to owners

Every major launch action should have: named owner or role owner; intended outcome; dependency; timing; success signal; governance or readiness assumption, where relevant; decision point or escalation route.

This makes the plan usable by the partner and customer team, not only by the course learner.

Example action:

- **Action** — Confirm approved source materials for vendor-contract summaries.
- **Owner** — Users know which materials are approved for the first workflow.
- **Outcome** — Users know which materials are approved for the first workflow.
- **Dependency** — Source approval and sensitive-data boundary assumption.
- **Timing** — Week 1 or before activation.
- **Success signal** — Approved source list documented and included in user guidance.
- **Decision or escalation** — Escalate to legal/compliance if source approval is unclear.

## Slide 47/59 - Build in feedback and decision points

The 30-day plan should include feedback and decision points from the beginning.

Include: user feedback collection; manager or workflow owner feedback; support route for blockers; governance or access issue review; output-quality or workflow-value review; decision point for next action.

For the legal operations example, Week 4 should not simply say "review results." It should specify what evidence is reviewed, who reviews it, and what decision the customer should make.

A strong Week 4 review might ask: Did the target cohort use ChatGPT for the intended workflow? Were summaries complete enough for human review? Did users follow source-use and human-review expectations? What issues or escalation patterns appeared? Should the customer continue, adjust, expand, or pause?

## Slide 51/59 - Introduction (Module: Package the success plan for setup and activation)

## Slide 52/59 - What the success plan includes

A complete ChatGPT deployment success plan should include: deployment vision; launch scope; intended ChatGPT experience and supported surface, with required plugin, app, Workspace Agent, or connected-tool dependencies; priority workflow and first user group; launch team and responsibilities; KPIs and evidence sources; readiness checkpoints; governance assumptions; workspace and source validation assumptions; 30-day launch plan; open risks and escalation points; handoff notes for workspace setup and activation design; observability approach — how usage, outputs, feedback, risks, and improvement actions will be reviewed during the first launch period.

This looks like a lot of elements, but your focus should be specific to the customer you're working with. Don't force a plan for every element unless it's relevant and ready. Remember that the plan should be concise enough to use and specific enough to guide action.

## Slide 53/59 - Make the handoff useful

The plan should clearly separate what each later workstream needs:

- **Workspace readiness** — Intended ChatGPT experience and supported surface; plan or workspace availability; access path; owner; source, plugin, app, or Workspace Agent assumptions; regional or residency considerations; admin-validation needs; permission or approval boundaries; and escalation route.
- **Use case activation** — Priority workflow, first user group, output expectation, review expectation, feedback route, and launch timing.
- **Enablement and adoption support** — User cohort, workflow-specific guidance, champion or manager role, acceptable-use reminders, and support route.
- **Measurement and review** — KPIs, evidence sources, owners, review points, limitations, and 30-day decision.
- **Governance validation** — Governance assumptions, owner, timing, workflow behavior affected, and evidence or issue patterns to review.

## Slide 54/59 - Success plan quality check

A strong plan is: workflow-specific; owner-aware; measurable; governed by clear assumptions; connected to readiness dependencies; time-bound; realistic for the first 30 days.

A weak plan is too broad, too feature-led, too checklist-like, or disconnected from user behavior, workflow value, governance expectations, and decision evidence.

Use this quick check before sharing the plan: Can a workspace owner see what needs validation? Can an activation lead see which workflow and user group to support? Can an enablement lead see what users need to practice? Can a governance stakeholder see which assumptions matter? Can a business owner see what evidence will support the next decision?
