## Slide 6/50 - Customer context: Brightline Advisory and Rowan Retail

Throughout the course, you'll work with Brightline Advisory, a fictional partner organization supporting a customer called Rowan Retail.

Rowan Retail has completed activation planning and selected two initial ChatGPT workflows:

- **Customer support leads** — Will use ChatGPT to draft human-reviewed case resolution summaries from approved support notes and policy guidance.
- **Regional operations managers** — Will use ChatGPT to prepare weekly store performance brief drafts from approved business context and manager notes.

The workspace readiness work is mostly on track, but adoption is not guaranteed.

Some users are excited. Others are unsure how ChatGPT fits into their daily work.

Managers want clear expectations. Champions want examples they can demonstrate.

IT and risk stakeholders want users to follow approved source-use, review, and escalation behaviors.

Brightline Advisory needs to help Rowan Retail move from activation planning to workflow adoption.

A weak enablement plan would say:

"Run ChatGPT training for all users and hold office hours."

That may sound useful, but it is too broad. It does not say which workflow users should practice, what behavior should change, what support they need, or how the team will reinforce adoption.

A stronger plan would say:

"Prepare support leads to use ChatGPT for approved case resolution summary drafts. Use role-based demos, a short workflow guide, manager reinforcement, champion examples, and office hours tied to the first two weeks of live use. Reinforce source-use boundaries, human review, feedback routes, and issue escalation."

That version is more useful because it connects enablement to real work.

## Slide 8/50 - Start with the approved workflow

A common mistake is to start enablement planning with product topics:

"We need a session on prompts, a demo of ChatGPT, and office hours."

Those activities may be useful, but they should not be the starting point.

The stronger starting point is the approved workflow from activation planning.

For each workflow, ask:

- What work will users complete with ChatGPT?
- Which approved ChatGPT experience should users use, and is the workflow collaborative or delegated?
- Which user group or cohort will use it first?
- What approved business context or source material should users rely on?
- What output should users produce?
- What human-review expectation applies?
- What safe-use or acceptable-use reminder matters?
- Where should users ask for help or report an issue?
- What adoption behavior should managers reinforce?

For Rowan Retail, "use ChatGPT for customer support" is not specific enough.

A better workflow definition is:

"Support leads use ChatGPT to draft human-reviewed case resolution summaries from approved case notes and policy guidance, then review and edit the draft before it is added to the customer record or shared internally."

That workflow gives enablement something concrete to support. Match enablement to the approved interaction pattern.

In Chat, users collaborate with ChatGPT by providing context, reviewing intermediate work, and refining the result.

In ChatGPT Work, users define the outcome, context, boundaries, and review standard, then monitor progress, steer the work, approve important actions, and review the finished output.

If activation planning has not confirmed the experience, record it as an open question rather than making an assumption.

## Slide 9/50 - Identify target audiences

Different audiences need different types of enablement because they interact with the workflow differently.

For each approved workflow, identify the people who will use, manage, support, or reinforce the behavior.

- **Primary users** — What they need from enablement: How to use ChatGPT in the approved workflow and how to review outputs. Example: Support leads drafting case resolution summaries.
- **Managers or workflow owners** — What they need from enablement: How to reinforce the behavior, review quality, and respond to adoption blockers. Example: Support managers checking whether summaries are complete and reviewed.
- **Champions** — What they need from enablement: How to demonstrate practical examples, answer common peer questions, and surface blockers. Example: Experienced support leads showing peers how to use the summary workflow.
- **Enablement or L&D** — What they need from enablement: What guidance, resources, and practice materials need to be prepared. Example: A short workflow guide, demo script, and practice exercise.
- **IT, security, legal, or compliance stakeholders** — What they need from enablement: Which expectations users need to follow around source use, sensitive information, review, and issue escalation. Example: Guidance on approved support notes, policy documents, and escalation for sensitive cases.
- **Executive or business sponsor** — What they need from enablement: What adoption behavior and early signals show whether the workflow is taking hold. Example: Participation, repeated use, summary quality feedback, and issue patterns.

One person may hold more than one role in a smaller deployment. The plan should still make each responsibility visible.

## Slide 10/50 - Define learning requirements

Learning requirements describe what users need to understand and practice before they can adopt the workflow.

- **Workflow purpose** — Why this workflow matters and what problem it is intended to solve.
- **Approved use pattern** — How ChatGPT should be used in this workflow.
- **Practical application guidance** — What users should do step by step during the task.
- **Expected user behavior** — What good usage looks like during daily work.
- **Human-review or acceptable-use expectations** — What users must review, verify, edit, escalate, or avoid.
- **Support and escalation route** — Where users should go for questions, issues, feedback, or blockers.

Add a seventh area when relevant:

- **Approved business context, knowledge sources, and data-use boundaries for the workflow** — Which sources, documents, notes, systems, or business context are approved for the workflow, and what users should avoid.

This keeps enablement focused on behavior change. Users should leave enablement knowing what to do in the work moment, not just what ChatGPT can do in general.

## Slide 11/50 - Example: Enablement requirements for a support workflow

Look at how Brightline Advisory defines requirements for Rowan Retail's support workflow.

- **Workflow purpose** — Help support leads prepare clearer case resolution summaries with less rework.
- **Approved use pattern** — Use ChatGPT to draft a summary from approved case notes and policy guidance. Do not treat the first draft as final.
- **Practical application guidance** — Paste or reference only approved, sanitized case context according to customer policy. Ask ChatGPT for a summary, missing information, and suggested follow-up questions.
- **Expected behavior** — Review, edit, and verify the summary before using it in the workflow.
- **Human review** — A support lead remains accountable for accuracy, tone, completeness, and customer-specific decisions.
- **Support route** — Use the dedicated adoption channel for workflow questions; escalate sensitive or policy questions to the workflow owner.
- **Feedback route** — Share examples where the summary was useful, incomplete, inaccurate, or unclear so the workflow can be improved.

Notice what this does not do. It does not list every ChatGPT feature. It describes how one group should use ChatGPT inside one approved workflow.

## Slide 12/50 - Common adoption barriers

Adoption barriers often appear when enablement is too generic or too disconnected from daily work.

Common barriers include:

- Users understand ChatGPT conceptually but do not know when to use it in their workflow.
- Users try the workflow once but do not repeat it because managers do not reinforce the behavior.
- Users are unsure which information or sources are approved.
- Users over-trust first drafts and skip human review.
- Users do not know where to ask questions or report issues.
- Champions are enthusiastic but do not have role-specific examples.
- Office hours become generic Q&A rather than workflow support.
- Communications announce availability but do not explain expected behavior.

Your enablement requirements should reduce these barriers before they become adoption problems. Users are more likely to adopt ChatGPT when they know when to use it, what good use looks like, how managers will reinforce the behavior, which examples apply to their role, and where to get support.

## Slide 17/50 - Match enablement to the work moment

Enablement should help users at the point where behavior needs to change.

Before adding an enablement activity to the plan, check whether it supports a specific audience, approved workflow, expected behavior, support route, reinforcement need, or feedback loop. If it does not, revise it before adding it to the plan.

**Example:** Support leads at Rowan Retail do not only need to know that ChatGPT can summarize. They need to know how to use ChatGPT during case closeout, what context they may use, how to review the draft, and how to surface issues.

Use this planning pattern:

- **Users need to understand why the workflow matters** — Useful enablement activity: Launch briefing or manager-led kickoff. Why it fits: Connects the workflow to the business purpose and team expectations.
- **Users need to see the workflow in action** — Useful enablement activity: Role-based demonstration. Why it fits: Shows the exact task, context, review behavior, and support route.
- **Users need to practice safely** — Useful enablement activity: Guided hands-on session. Why it fits: Builds confidence with approved examples before live use.
- **Users need help after activation** — Useful enablement activity: Office hours or support channel. Why it fits: Provides timely support when real workflow questions emerge.
- **Users need a reminder at the work moment** — Useful enablement activity: Job aid, checklist, prompt starter, or workflow guide. Why it fits: Makes expected behavior easy to repeat.
- **Managers need to reinforce adoption** — Useful enablement activity: Manager guide or talking points. Why it fits: Helps managers coach behavior, not just announce availability.

A strong enablement plan usually uses a small mix of activities rather than one large training event.

## Slide 18/50 - Review enablement options

Use these options as building blocks.

- **Workshop** — Best fit: Building shared understanding and practicing a workflow with facilitation. Watch out for: Can become too broad if it covers too many workflows at once.
- **Guided session** — Best fit: Helping users complete a real task with support. Watch out for: Needs approved example inputs and clear safe-use guidance.
- **Demonstration** — Best fit: Showing the workflow quickly to create confidence and clarity. Watch out for: Should show role-specific work, not generic feature exploration.
- **Office hours** — Best fit: Office hours. Watch out for: Should be structured around workflow blockers, not open-ended product Q&A only.
- **Self-service resources** — Best fit: Providing repeatable guidance at the moment of need. Watch out for: Should stay short, role-specific, and tied to the workflow.
- **Manager reinforcement** — Best fit: Helping managers coach adoption and review behavior. Watch out for: Managers need a clear expectation, not just a reminder to encourage usage.
- **Champion examples** — Best fit: Sharing practical examples from credible peers. Watch out for: Champions need approved examples and a route to surface blockers.

## Slide 19/50 - Role-based enablement examples

Different audiences need different examples.

- **Support leads** — Role-based example: Draft a case resolution summary from approved notes and policy guidance. Enablement focus: How to structure the request, review the draft, and escalate sensitive cases.
- **Support managers** — Role-based example: Review summary quality and identify patterns in user feedback. Enablement focus: How to reinforce behavior and monitor quality signals.
- **Champions** — Role-based example: Demonstrate two examples: a strong summary and a summary that needs revision. Enablement focus: How to model good behavior and answer common peer questions.
- **Operations managers** — Role-based example: Prepare a weekly store performance brief draft from approved notes. Enablement focus: How to use approved context and review for accuracy before sharing.
- **Enablement lead** — Role-based example: Package role-based examples into short guides and practice tasks. Enablement focus: How to make resources practical and reusable.

Role-based examples reduce the distance between "I know ChatGPT exists" and "I know how to use it in my work."

## Slide 20/50 - Make practice realistic

Practice should use realistic work moments, but it should not use sensitive or unapproved information.

A practical enablement activity should include:

- A short workflow scenario
- Approved or fictionalized input material
- A clear task for the learner
- A review step
- A support or escalation reminder
- A reflection question about how the workflow would apply in daily work

For the support summary workflow, a practice task could ask users to draft a summary from fictional case notes, review it against a checklist, identify missing information, and decide whether the issue should be escalated.

Once the experience is confirmed, match the practice task to the interaction pattern.

Collaborative Chat practice should include providing context, reviewing intermediate work, and refining the result.

ChatGPT Work practice should include defining the outcome, context, boundaries, and review standard; monitoring progress; steering the work; approving important actions; and reviewing the finished output.

This is more useful than a generic prompt-writing exercise because it rehearses the behavior the user needs after activation.

## Slide 25/50 - Introduction (Module: Plan Adoption Communications)

Communications can create momentum, but only if they explain what users should do and why it matters.

In this module, you'll define communication objectives, key messages, timing, channels, and reinforcement needs for approved ChatGPT workflows. You'll also identify what message will help the audience understand, try, repeat, or improve the approved workflow.

## Slide 26/50 - Define communication objectives

Adoption communications usually need to move users through four objectives.

- **Awareness** — What the communication should do: Make users aware that the approved workflow is launching. Example message focus: What is launching, who it is for, and why it matters.
- **Understanding** — What the communication should do: Explain what users are expected to do. Example message focus: How the workflow works, what context is approved, and what review expectations apply.
- **Participation** — What the communication should do: Encourage users to attend, try, or complete the first workflow action. Example message focus: What to do next, when to join a session, and where to get guidance.
- **Reinforcement** — What the communication should do: Keep the behavior going after first use. Example message focus: Share examples, reminders, office hours, common questions, and feedback requests.

Different audiences may need different communication objectives at different times.

Executives may need a concise value and progress message. Primary users need practical instructions. Managers need reinforcement expectations. Champions need examples and support details.

## Slide 27/50 - Develop workflow-specific key messages

A useful adoption message should answer five questions:

- Why are we using ChatGPT for this workflow?
- What should users do differently?
- What approved context or sources should users use?
- How should outputs be reviewed?
- Where should users ask questions, share feedback, or surface issues?

For Rowan Retail's support workflow, a generic message would say:

"ChatGPT is now available for support teams. Please attend training and start using it."

A stronger message would say:

"Support leads in the pilot regions will use ChatGPT to draft human-reviewed case resolution summaries from approved case notes and policy guidance. You remain responsible for reviewing and editing each summary before use. Join the guided session on Tuesday, use the summary checklist during the first two weeks, and raise questions in the support adoption channel."

The stronger message is more actionable because it tells users what is changing in the workflow.

## Slide 28/50 - Plan communication activities

Use communication timing to support the adoption journey.

- **Before activation** — Communication activity: Manager or sponsor announcement. Purpose: Explain business purpose, workflow scope, and who is included.
- **Before training** — Communication activity: Audience-specific invitation. Purpose: Tell users what they will practice and what preparation is needed.
- **Launch week** — Communication activity: Workflow reminder. Purpose: Reinforce approved context, human review, support route, and first-use expectation.
- **Week 2** — Communication activity: Common questions and examples. Purpose: Address early blockers and share approved practical examples.
- **Week 3 or 4** — Communication activity: Reinforcement and feedback request. Purpose: Ask users and managers for feedback on adoption barriers, output quality, and workflow fit.
- **After first review** — Communication activity: Progress update. Purpose: Share what was learned, what will be improved, and what continues next.

Communications should not overwhelm users. A few well-timed messages connected to workflow behavior are usually better than many generic reminders.

## Slide 29/50 - Avoid communication traps

Watch for these traps:

- Announcing ChatGPT access without explaining the approved workflow.
- Using the same message for executives, managers, champions, and end users.
- Focusing on excitement instead of expected behavior.
- Promising productivity gains without adoption evidence.
- Forgetting safe-use, human-review, or source-use expectations.
- Sending launch communications without a support route.
- Stopping communications after the first training event.

The goal is not to make users aware of ChatGPT in general.

The goal is to help them adopt the selected workflows responsibly and confidently.

## Slide 33/50 - Introduction (Module: Establish Champion and Support Structures)

Users often need support after training, when they try ChatGPT in real work and run into practical questions. Champion and support structures help adoption continue beyond launch. They also help the deployment team see what is working, what is confusing, and what needs to be improved.

In this module, you'll identify how users will get help, see credible examples, and surface adoption blockers during live workflow use.

## Slide 34/50 - Champions reinforce workflow behavior

Champions are not only enthusiastic users. In a workflow-based adoption plan, champions help peers practice and repeat approved behaviors.

Champion responsibilities may include:

- Demonstrating approved workflows.
- Sharing practical role-based examples.
- Supporting peer questions.
- Surfacing adoption blockers.
- Encouraging participation.
- Routing issues to the right owner.
- Reinforcing human-review and safe-use expectations.

For Rowan Retail, a strong champion is not simply someone who likes ChatGPT. A strong champion is a credible support lead who understands the case summary workflow, follows review expectations, and can help peers use the workflow responsibly.

## Slide 35/50 - Select champions intentionally

Choose champions based on workflow credibility, peer trust, and willingness to surface issues.

- **Close to the workflow** — Champions should understand the actual work users are trying to complete.
- **Trusted by peers** — Users are more likely to ask practical questions and accept examples from credible colleagues.
- **Clear on expected behavior** — Champions should model approved context use, human review, and escalation behavior.
- **Willing to share feedback** — Champions should surface adoption blockers, confusing guidance, or support gaps.
- **Supported by managers** — Champions need time and permission to help peers, not just informal enthusiasm.

Avoid overloading champions. Their role is to reinforce adoption, not to become the only support mechanism.

## Slide 36/50 - Select support mechanisms

Support mechanisms should match the workflow and user need.

- **Office hours** — Best fit: Users need live help after trying the workflow. Workflow adoption use: Discuss real blockers, review examples, and clarify expected behavior.
- **Community channel** — Best fit: Users need a visible place to ask questions and share examples. Workflow adoption use: Collect common issues and route unresolved questions.
- **Peer learning** — Best fit: Teams benefit from seeing examples from colleagues. Workflow adoption use: Champions share workflow examples and lessons learned.
- **Q&A sessions** — Best fit: Users have common questions after launch. Workflow adoption use: Address repeated confusion without creating one-off support burden.
- **Feedback form or route** — Best fit: Deployment teams need structured adoption signals. Workflow adoption use: Capture workflow issues, support gaps, and improvement ideas.
- **Escalation path** — Best fit: Questions involve policy, access, sensitive data, or workflow risk. Workflow adoption use: Route issues to the right owner instead of leaving users to guess.

A strong support structure makes it easy for users to continue, pause, or escalate when needed.

## Slide 37/50 - Feedback and issue escalation

Adoption support should not only answer questions. It should also improve the workflow.

Define how the team will capture:

- User questions
- Common mistakes
- Workflow confusion
- Source-use or review issues
- Prompt or guidance gaps
- Manager feedback
- Champion observations
- Escalation events

Then define who reviews that feedback and what happens next.

**Example:** If support leads repeatedly ask whether a certain case type can be summarized with ChatGPT, that is not just a training question. It may be a guidance or governance question that should be routed to the workflow owner or policy owner.

This is how support becomes a learning loop for the deployment team.

## Slide 38/50 - Plan ongoing reinforcement

Adoption reinforcement helps users repeat the workflow after the first training event.

Useful reinforcement activities include:

- Sharing approved examples from the workflow.
- Highlighting common questions and improved guidance.
- Reminding managers what behavior to coach.
- Refreshing office hours around high-friction moments.
- Asking champions to demonstrate a strong example.
- Updating job aids based on user feedback.
- Celebrating careful, reviewed workflow use rather than raw usage volume.

Reinforcement should be specific:

Weak: "Use ChatGPT more" is not an adoption strategy.

Strong: "Use the summary checklist during case closeout and bring unclear examples to office hours" is actionable.

## Slide 44/50 - Adoption workstream summary

Use the adoption workstream summary to connect the moving parts of the plan.

Each approved workflow should become a clear adoption workstream. This helps the team see who will use ChatGPT, what behavior should change, what users need to rely on, how outputs should be reviewed, what support is available, and how adoption signals will be captured.

- **Approved workflow** — Question: What work will users complete with ChatGPT? Example: Draft human-reviewed case resolution summaries.
- **Activation cohort** — Question: Who will use it first? Example: Support leads in two pilot regions.
- **Expected behavior** — Question: What should users do differently? Example: Use approved context, generate a draft, review and edit before use, and share feedback.
- **Approved context or sources** — Question: What information, documents, notes, systems, or guidance should users rely on? Example: Approved case notes and policy guidance.
- **Human-review expectation** — Question: What must users review, verify, edit, or escalate before using outputs? Example: Support leads review each draft for accuracy, completeness, tone, and policy alignment before use.
- **Enablement asset** — Question: What resource helps users practice or repeat the behavior? Example: Workflow guide, summary checklist, demo recording, and practice scenario.
- **Communication** — Question: What message moves users toward adoption? Example: Launch announcement, role-specific invitation, and week-two reinforcement message.
- **Champion or support** — Question: Who helps users after activation? Example: Regional champions, office hours, and adoption channel.
- **Feedback route** — Question: How will blockers and improvement ideas be captured? Example: Weekly champion huddle and support channel issue review.
- **Adoption signal** — Question: What early signal shows whether behavior is taking hold? Example: Users complete first summaries, managers see improved consistency, and issues are escalated appropriately.

This table is the bridge between activation planning and adoption support. Use it to check whether the plan is still anchored in the approved workflow, not drifting into generic training, communications, or support activities.

## Slide 45/50 - Scan for adoption risks

Before finalizing the plan, scan for adoption risks.

Common risks include:

- The workflow is not specific enough for users to practice.
- The target audience is too broad or undefined.
- Managers are not prepared to reinforce the behavior.
- Enablement activities teach features but not work moments.
- Role-based examples are missing or unrealistic.
- Users are unclear about approved context or source-use boundaries.
- Human-review expectations are not emphasized.
- Champions lack guidance, time, or escalation routes.
- Office hours are not connected to real workflow blockers.
- Feedback is collected but not reviewed by an owner.

For each risk, define a mitigation or next action. The plan should make adoption risks visible before they affect the launch.
