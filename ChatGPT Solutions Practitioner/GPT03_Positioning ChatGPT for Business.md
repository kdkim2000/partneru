## Slide 5/48 - Identify ChatGPT-specific value levers

In this module, you'll learn how to describe the main ways ChatGPT can create business value in customer workflows.

This matters because many ChatGPT conversations start with broad value language, such as "save time," "improve productivity," or "help employees work faster." Those ideas may be directionally useful, but they are not specific enough to support strong customer positioning.

Your goal is to connect ChatGPT value to a real workflow pain. That means identifying what improves for the customer and how ChatGPT helps the work change.

## Slide 6/48 - Identifying ChatGPT-specific value levers

ChatGPT value should be tied to how work changes for users and teams.

When customers say, "ChatGPT will help us save time," they may be directionally right—but that statement is too broad to support a strong business conversation. It does not explain which workflow improves, which team benefits, what output changes, or what value the customer should validate.

Before naming the value lever, separate two things:

**The business outcome** — what improves for the customer? For example: faster renewal preparation, more consistent support responses, better visibility into project blockers, or improved policy review discipline.

**The ChatGPT mechanism** — how does ChatGPT help the workflow change? For example: synthesizing approved sources, drafting a first-pass brief, standardizing a response format, helping users reason through information, or supporting human review.

A strong value lever connects both sides. For example, a weak statement is simply, "ChatGPT saves time." A stronger statement would be, "ChatGPT could help account managers prepare renewal briefs faster by synthesizing approved account notes, support history, and renewal risks into a review-ready first draft."

The stronger version is more useful because it names the workflow, the user, the business output, and how ChatGPT supports the work.

## Slide 7/48 - Common ChatGPT value levers

ChatGPT can create business value in different ways depending on the workflow. These common value levers help you move from a broad claim like "ChatGPT saves time" to a clearer explanation of what improves, how the work changes, and why that improvement matters to the customer.

Some of the most common ChatGPT value levers include:

- **Speed** — Reducing time spent drafting, researching, summarizing, preparing, or analyzing.
- **Consistency** — Helping teams produce more standardized outputs, follow preferred formats, or apply shared guidance.
- **Knowledge access** — Helping users find, synthesize, and apply trusted company information across sources.
- **Quality** — Improving first drafts, briefs, analysis, and recommendations through iteration and review.
- **Capacity** — Helping teams complete more knowledge work—including delegated, multi-step work that moves toward a usable, review-ready deliverable—while people retain direction, review, and accountability.
- **Governed use** — Supporting controlled, policy-aware use of AI for business work, with appropriate oversight.

As you use these levers, keep them close to the customer's operating context. That means understanding what knowledge, source materials, user groups, review expectations, and governance considerations shape the workflow.

## Slide 8/48 - Connecting value levers to workflow pains

A value lever is only useful when it connects to a real customer problem.

If the customer's pain is vague, your positioning will also be vague. A customer might say, "Our teams are overloaded," or "We need better productivity," or "We want to use AI for knowledge work." Those statements may be useful starting points, but they do not yet tell you what to position.

To strengthen the conversation, ask what work is actually breaking down: what workflow takes too long? What output is inconsistent? What information is hard to find or reuse? Where do specialists get pulled into repetitive work? Where does the team need more reliable preparation, analysis, or review?

Once the workflow pain is clearer, the value lever becomes easier to explain.

Instead of saying "ChatGPT improves productivity," say "ChatGPT could reduce time spent preparing renewal briefs."

Instead of saying "ChatGPT helps teams communicate better," say "ChatGPT could improve consistency of customer follow-up summaries by helping teams use a shared format."

Instead of saying "ChatGPT helps with policy work," say "ChatGPT could help analysts synthesize policy updates from approved internal sources before human review."

Each stronger statement ties ChatGPT to a specific business output. That is what makes the value easier for the customer to understand, test, and defend internally.

## Slide 9/48 - Value levers: real-world example

A regional operations team prepares a weekly leadership update every Friday. Today, the team collects information from spreadsheets, meeting notes, project trackers, and email threads. The work is repeated every week, but the output varies depending on who prepares it.

At first, the customer says: "We want ChatGPT to help write the update." That is a task-level description. It is not yet strong business positioning.

A more effective view starts with the workflow tension: the team spends too much time collecting and synthesizing inputs; different team members use different formats; blockers are not always visible until the leadership review; managers want a clearer, more consistent update without adding more manual work.

In this workflow, ChatGPT's value is not just "writing faster." The stronger value levers are:

- **Speed** — Faster synthesis of inputs into a first draft.
- **Consistency** — More standardized weekly update structure.
- **Knowledge access** — Easier use of relevant notes, trackers, and source materials.
- **Quality** — Clearer visibility into blockers before leadership review.

A stronger positioning statement might then be: "ChatGPT could help the regional operations team prepare weekly leadership updates faster and more consistently by synthesizing approved project notes, spreadsheets, and meeting inputs into a review-ready draft. The value to validate is reduced preparation time, clearer blocker visibility, and more consistent update quality."

Notice that this statement does not start with a feature. It starts with the workflow and the business output.

## Slide 13/48 - Translating workflow value based on different buyers

The same ChatGPT workflow can matter for different reasons depending on who owns the outcome.

A business sponsor, technology sponsor, transformation lead, and operational leader may all care about the same workflow—but each will listen for a different value story. Start with the workflow output, then translate why that output matters to the buyer.

For example, imagine a customer who wants ChatGPT to support the preparation of renewal briefs. The workflow output in this instance would be a review-ready renewal brief. But the value language changes depending on the buyer:

**Business sponsor** — A business sponsor may care about stronger renewal readiness, better account planning, and improved team productivity. Positioning angle: "ChatGPT could help customer success teams prepare renewal briefs faster and more consistently, giving leaders better visibility into renewal risks and account readiness."

**Technology or security sponsor** — A technology or security sponsor may care about governed access, approved source materials, permissions, and responsible use. Positioning angle: "ChatGPT could support renewal brief preparation in a governed workspace, using approved source materials and review expectations that align with the customer's data and access policies."

**Transformation or AI lead** — A transformation or AI lead may care about whether the workflow becomes a repeatable pattern that can scale. Positioning angle: "Renewal brief preparation could be a strong first workflow because it is repeatable, depends on business context across systems, produces a clear output, and can help the customer learn how to scale ChatGPT into similar knowledge workflows."

**Operational or team leader** — An operational leader may care about whether the workflow becomes easier, faster, and more consistent for the team. Positioning angle: "ChatGPT could help managers and account owners reduce manual preparation, follow a more consistent brief format, and spend more time reviewing customer risks instead of assembling the first draft."

For any buyer type, effective positioning should show how ChatGPT helps teams use shared context and review practices to improve how their work gets done, and how ChatGPT can strengthen business results over time.

## Slide 14/48 - Avoid one-size-fits-all value language

One-size-fits-all value language makes ChatGPT sound generic. For example: "ChatGPT will make your team more productive."

That may be true in a broad sense, but it doesn't show that you understand the buyer's priorities, the customer's workflow pain, or how the value connects to a business outcome. It also does not help the customer make an internal case.

A better approach is to tailor the value language to the person in front of you. A seller should use ChatGPT value positioning to connect the customer's workflow pain to buyer-specific outcomes.

- **For business sponsors** — Connect ChatGPT to outcomes such as productivity, quality, speed, revenue readiness, cost reduction, customer experience, risk reduction, or better knowledge access.
- **For technology and security sponsors** — Connect ChatGPT to responsible adoption, access boundaries, data handling, approved sources, review expectations, and validated controls.
- **For transformation or AI leads** — Connect ChatGPT to repeatable workflow patterns, adoption readiness, and the path from isolated pilots to embedded intelligence in business operations.
- **For operational or team leaders** — Connect ChatGPT to the daily work: less manual effort, easier preparation, more consistent outputs, clearer handoffs, better knowledge access, and better use of expert time.

(Click to complete item, checked ✓): "Remember: you do not need a completely different story for every buyer. You need to take your consistent workflow story, and translate it in a way that speaks to their specific needs or pains."

Use this simple pattern: "Because you care about [buyer priority], this workflow matters because [workflow pain or business output]. ChatGPT could help by [workflow mechanism]. The value to validate is [measurable outcome]."

Here's an example for a customer support leader: "Because your team cares about faster, more consistent support responses, this workflow matters because agents need to prepare accurate replies from approved knowledge sources. ChatGPT could help by synthesizing relevant guidance and drafting response options for agent review. The value to validate is reduced response preparation time and more consistent response quality."

Now, here's a different example for a technology sponsor: "Because your team cares about responsible adoption, this workflow matters because support responses may depend on approved knowledge sources and customer information. ChatGPT could help within a governed workspace, but the next step is to confirm source permissions, access boundaries, and review expectations before expanding use."

Both statements discuss the same workflow. The difference is the buyer lens.

## Slide 19/48 - How to position a workflow transformation

In this module, you'll learn how to position ChatGPT as a workflow transformation rather than a feature set or one-off productivity tool.

Customers often understand individual capabilities like drafting, summarizing, analysis, or search. But business value becomes clearer when you explain how those capabilities change a repeatable way of working.

The goal is not to overstate automation or imply that human judgment disappears. The goal is to describe a grounded before-and-after shift: how work happens today, how ChatGPT could support that work, and what improves after the workflow changes.

## Slide 20/48 - Position the before and after

Effective ChatGPT positioning should explain the shift from current work to improved work.

This does not mean overpromising full automation. In many customer workflows, ChatGPT is best positioned as a way to help people complete knowledge work more effectively—with human review, approved context, and clear expectations.

Use a simple before-and-after frame:

**Before** — What happens today? Manual, fragmented, inconsistent, slow, or expert-dependent work.

**With ChatGPT** — How does the workflow change? AI-assisted synthesis, drafting, analysis, preparation, reasoning, or knowledge access.

**After** — What improves? Faster, more consistent, easier-to-scale, or higher-quality workflow output.

Here's an example that focuses on how ChatGPT can better support account managers:

**Before:** Account managers manually search through call notes, CRM exports, and support history before preparing renewal briefs. Brief quality varies by account owner.

**With ChatGPT:** ChatGPT helps synthesize approved account context into a structured first draft that the account manager reviews and improves.

**After:** Managers can prepare briefs more consistently, spend less time assembling information, and focus more attention on renewal risks and customer strategy.

This before-and-after frame helps buyers see the business transformation, not just the task.

A feature-first statement might be: "ChatGPT can summarize documents and draft briefs."

A workflow-transformation statement would be: "Today, account managers spend hours assembling renewal briefs from fragmented notes and systems. ChatGPT could help synthesize approved account context into a structured first draft, so managers can spend more time reviewing renewal risks and preparing for customer conversations."

The second statement is a better option because it explains how work changes.

Position the experience that fits the work. ChatGPT Chat is collaborative: users stay closely involved as they explore, draft, critique, and refine. ChatGPT Work is delegative: users define an outcome, provide context and boundaries, and review the result. Workspace Agents can turn repeatable tasks into shared workflows, but eligibility, publishing, actions, schedules, triggers, permissions, shared connections, and approvals depend on plan and workspace configuration. Codex remains the dedicated experience for software engineering.

## Slide 21/48 - Keep any transformation grounded

Workflow transformation should stay close to the customer's actual work.

Avoid language that suggests ChatGPT will replace a team, fully automate judgment-heavy decisions, or remove the need for review. That kind of language can reduce trust, especially with enterprise buyers who are responsible for accuracy, governance, and business risk.

Better positioning is grounded and specific: "ChatGPT could help create a first draft for human review." "ChatGPT could help synthesize approved sources so the team starts from a stronger baseline." "ChatGPT could help standardize the format of weekly updates." "ChatGPT could help analysts compare policy changes before they draft a recommendation." "ChatGPT could help employees find and apply trusted company knowledge faster."

Avoid overextended claims: "ChatGPT will automate the whole workflow." "ChatGPT will eliminate the need for specialist review." "ChatGPT will guarantee accurate outputs." "ChatGPT will deliver ROI immediately."

Remember that a grounded transformation statement should include: the current workflow pain; the ChatGPT-supported work pattern; the improved output; the role of human review where appropriate; the measurable outcome the customer should validate.

This is especially important when the workflow involves sensitive information, customer-facing outputs, legal or compliance review, or business decisions that require accountable human judgment.

A grounded workflow transformation statement might be: "Today, legal specialists manually prepare first-pass contract summaries for internal stakeholders. ChatGPT could help shift the workflow toward AI-assisted extraction and first-pass summary creation from approved contract materials, with legal review before use. The business value to validate is faster triage, more consistent summaries, and better use of legal team capacity."

The business story is not "legal uses AI." The business story is faster triage, more consistent summary quality, and better use of specialist time.

## Slide 25/48 - Framing measurable value

You'll now learn how to frame ChatGPT value in a way that is measurable and defensible.

Effective value positioning does not require you to build a full ROI model in the sales conversation. You need to instead focus on explaining what the customer could validate if they tested the workflow. That means moving from broad benefits to a value hypothesis: a clear statement of what may improve and what evidence the customer could use to confirm it.

By the end of this module, you should be able to turn a general benefit like "save time" into a workflow-specific value hypothesis.

## Slide 26/48 - Move from benefits to measurable value

A benefit describes what improves. A measurable value hypothesis describes how the customer might validate that improvement.

Here's an example of a simple benefit statement: "ChatGPT could help the finance team work faster."

Now, here's a superior example of a measurable value hypothesis: "ChatGPT could help the finance team reduce first-draft preparation time for monthly variance commentary. The customer can validate value by comparing preparation time, revision cycles, and finance leader satisfaction before and after a workflow test."

The second statement is more useful because it gives the customer a way to evaluate whether the workflow is worth expanding.

A measurable value hypothesis does not need to be a full ROI model. The seller-level goal is to frame value clearly enough that the customer understands what should be tested. Use customer-specific assumptions, not generic claims.

Another weak benefit statement might be: "ChatGPT will save employees hours every week." This is turned into a stronger measurable value hypothesis like so: "For the weekly leadership update workflow, ChatGPT could help reduce preparation time and improve consistency. The customer can validate this by comparing time spent preparing the update, number of revision cycles, and leader feedback on update quality."

Remember that strong value framing is credible because it makes the evidence visible.

## Slide 27/48 - Use measurable value categories

ChatGPT workflows can connect to several measurable value categories. Choose the category that best fits the customer's workflow and buyer priority.

Some common examples include:

- **Time** — Reduced preparation, research, drafting, or review time. "Reduce time spent preparing executive meeting briefs."
- **Quality** — Fewer errors, more complete outputs, clearer structure, or better consistency. "Improve the completeness and consistency of account review summaries."
- **Capacity** — More work completed by the same team, or less reliance on scarce specialists for first-pass work. "Help legal specialists spend less time drafting first-pass summaries and more time reviewing higher-risk issues."
- **Speed** — Faster response, cycle time, decision support, or handoff. "Help support agents prepare response drafts more quickly from approved knowledge sources."
- **Risk** — Better policy adherence, review discipline, or controlled use of information. "Help analysts use approved policy sources and review steps when preparing risk summaries."
- **Experience** — Improved employee or customer experience. "Give employees a faster way to find and apply trusted internal guidance."
- **Adoption and improvement** — Whether the workflow is reused by the intended team, whether outputs are reviewed consistently, and whether the customer can see improvement over time. "Track whether managers continue using the workflow and whether review feedback shows output quality improving."

You do not need to force every workflow into every category. Pick the one or two categories that best match the customer's pain and the buyer's priorities, then focus on them.

## Slide 28/48 - Keep any value claims defensible

Value claims should be credible, cautious, and customer-specific.

To achieve this, use language like: "could help," "is designed to support," "aims to," "the customer can validate," "the value hypothesis is."

Avoid unsupported percentages, dollar values, or ROI claims unless the customer provides validated assumptions. For example, do not say: "ChatGPT will reduce support costs by 30%." Instead, say: "ChatGPT could help reduce response preparation time for support agents. The customer can validate this by comparing average preparation time, response quality review results, and agent feedback during a workflow test."

A defensible value hypothesis should identify what data the customer would need to confirm value. Depending on the workflow, that data could include: preparation time, review time, response time, revision cycles, output quality scores, manager or reviewer feedback, employee satisfaction, customer satisfaction signals, reduced rework, fewer escalations, evidence that teams reuse the workflow.

Effective value positioning may also include a simple "why now" reason. Common examples include rising support volume, pressure to improve productivity, inconsistent customer experience, an upcoming operating priority, or a need to scale knowledge work without adding more specialist capacity.

Example: "Because support volume is rising and response quality varies by agent, ChatGPT could help agents prepare response drafts from approved knowledge sources. The customer can validate value by comparing response preparation time, review quality, and escalation rates during a focused workflow test."

This stays specific, measurable, and credible.

## Slide 34/48 - Recognize common customer concerns

Trust questions are part of strong ChatGPT positioning. They are not a distraction from the value conversation.

Enterprise customers need to understand not only what ChatGPT can help employees do, but also how the organization can use it responsibly. Your role is to respond at the seller level: acknowledge the concern, connect it to the workflow, and identify what should be validated with the right stakeholders.

Common customer concerns include:

**Data use** — "Will our business data be used to train models?" "Can teams use confidential information?" "What customer data is appropriate for this workflow?"

**Source access and permissions** — "What sources can ChatGPT access?" "Will users only see information they are allowed to see?" "How do we decide which documents, systems, or knowledge sources are in scope?"

**Output accuracy and review** — "How do we know the output is right?" "Who reviews summaries, recommendations, or customer-facing drafts before use?" "What happens if ChatGPT produces an incomplete or incorrect answer?"

**Governance and observability** — "How will the business monitor, control, and improve AI use over time?" "What audit trail or reporting is needed?" "How will policy adherence be checked?"

**Sensitive workflows** — "Is this appropriate for legal, compliance, HR, finance, healthcare, or other sensitive work?" "What approval steps are needed before outputs affect customers, systems, or business decisions?"
