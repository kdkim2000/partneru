## Slide 4/42 - Reframe your thinking from tasks to workflows

Customers often describe ChatGPT opportunities as individual tasks: things like writing an email, summarizing notes, drafting a brief, or reviewing a document.

Those tasks are useful starting points, but they rarely show the full business opportunity. To understand where ChatGPT can create real value, you need to look at the workflow around the task: who does the work, what information they use, what output they need, and where the process slows down or varies in quality.

In this module, you'll learn how to reframe task requests into repeatable workflows that are easier to evaluate, explain, and prioritize in a customer conversation.

## Slide 5/42 - The difference between tasks and workflows

The difference between a task and a workflow matters because customers often start with a task, but value usually comes from improving the workflow around that task.

A one-off task may save a few minutes. A repeatable workflow can improve speed, consistency, review quality, and team alignment over time.

**Task**

A single action. For example, a customer might ask whether ChatGPT can summarize a document, draft an email, analyze notes, or turn a meeting transcript into next steps.

**Workflow**

A workflow is a sequence of work with a clear user, inputs, steps, output, and business purpose. It may happen in a single interaction or as a repeatable, multi-step process. Where available and enabled, that process may use approved apps or tools, run on a schedule or trigger, and pause for action constraints, approvals, or human escalation.

For example, "write a follow-up email" is a task. "Prepare a post-meeting follow-up" is a workflow. It may include reviewing call notes, identifying customer commitments, drafting a response, updating next steps, and preparing the account team for the next conversation.

## Slide 6/42 - What makes a workflow worth exploring?

A workflow is worth exploring when it happens often, takes meaningful time, depends on knowledge work, and produces an output people already need.

Strong ChatGPT workflow signals include repeated drafting, summarizing, research, analysis, preparation, review, or synthesis. They also include work that depends on multiple information sources, creates inconsistent output quality, relies heavily on a small number of experts, or has clear value if it becomes faster or more consistent.

To move from an isolated task to a repeatable workflow, ask six questions:

**1. Who performs the work?**

Identify the user group, not just the task.

For example: Account teams preparing customer follow-ups. Analysts reviewing policy updates. Operations managers preparing weekly leadership updates. Customer success teams summarizing account health.

This matters because ChatGPT workflow design depends on the people who will use it, the way they already work, and the output they are responsible for producing.

**2. What steps repeat?**

Look for the repeated sequence around the task.

For example, a team may not simply "summarize notes." They may repeatedly collect notes, identify key themes, compare them against account priorities, draft a summary, review risks, and share next steps with stakeholders.

The repeatable steps show where ChatGPT could support the workflow.

**3. What context is needed?**

A workflow usually depends on business context. That context may include source materials, customer history, internal policies, prior meeting notes, templates, account plans, project trackers, or approved guidance.

This does not mean you need to design the technical setup. At this stage, you need to understand what information the user would need to do the work well.

Ask:
- What information does the user rely on today?
- Which sources are trusted?
- Which context changes the quality of the output?
- What would ChatGPT need to know to produce something useful?

**4. What output must improve?**

The strongest workflow opportunities have a clear output that the customer already needs.

Examples might include a customer-ready follow-up email, an executive account brief, a weekly leadership update or a policy summary.

Then clarify what "better" means. Does the output need to be faster, more complete, more consistent, more tailored, easier to review, or more aligned to approved guidance?

**5. What governance considerations apply?**

Governance considerations help you understand what must be handled carefully for the workflow to be trusted.

This does not mean designing controls or configuring the workspace. It means identifying early questions that could affect responsible use.

Ask:
- Which source materials are approved for this workflow?
- Does the workflow involve sensitive customer, employee, legal, financial, or regulated information?
- Who should review the output before it is used?
- Are there access boundaries or approval expectations?
- What would make the customer confident that this workflow is being used responsibly?

Governance considerations are especially important when the workflow affects customers, business decisions, regulated work, or sensitive internal information.

**6. How will success be measured?**

A workflow is easier to prioritize when the customer can describe what improvement would look like.

Success measures do not need to be complex at this stage. They should be practical and tied to the workflow.

Examples might be to reduce time spent preparing account briefs, improve the consistency of follow-up summaries, reduce rework before manager review, or increase the completeness of weekly updates.

The goal is to identify a measurable outcome that makes the workflow worth exploring. It is not about building a full ROI model yet.

## Slide 10/42 - Mapping workflows

Once you can spot the difference between a task and a workflow, the next step is to map how the work actually happens today.

A workflow map helps you slow down the conversation and make the opportunity more specific. Instead of jumping straight to what ChatGPT could do, you identify the user, trigger, inputs, steps, decisions, output, and business purpose behind the work.

In this module, you'll learn how to build a simple workflow map and use it to uncover friction points, context, and value signals that can support a stronger customer conversation.

## Slide 11/42 - Use a simple workflow map

Before recommending how ChatGPT could help, map the workflow in plain business language. This keeps the conversation focused on how work happens today, not on a list of features.

A simple workflow map answers eight questions:
- User: Who performs the work?
- Work object: What is moving through the process, such as a brief, case, request, contract, account plan, report, or customer question?
- Trigger: What starts the work?
- Inputs: What information is needed?
- Steps: What happens today?
- Decisions or actions: What does the user decide, create, update, approve, or hand off?
- Output: What needs to be produced, changed, or decided?
- Business purpose: Why does this matter to the customer or team?

Use this simple map to turn a task-level idea into a workflow you can discuss with a customer.

For a multi-step or recurring workflow, also note which apps or tools are involved, whether a schedule or trigger starts or repeats the work, and where action constraints, approvals, or human escalation are required. At this stage, you're identifying requirements—not configuring the workflow or promising feature availability.

In a customer conversation, you do not need to ask these questions as a rigid checklist. Use them to guide the discussion. Start with the work the customer already described, then probe further.

For example, if the task is "summarize support tickets," the workflow map might look like this:
- User: The user is a customer success manager.
- Work object: The work object is a renewal risk brief.
- Trigger: The trigger is an upcoming executive account review.
- Inputs: The inputs are support tickets, account notes, usage trends, renewal history, and customer goals.
- Steps: The steps today include finding information, reading notes, identifying themes, drafting a brief, and checking whether the brief is accurate.
- Decisions or actions: The decisions or actions include identifying which renewal risks and opportunities should be escalated for leadership attention, confirming recommended next steps, and deciding what the account team should raise in the executive review.
- Output: The output is a review-ready account summary with risks, opportunities, and recommended next steps.
- Business purpose: The business purpose is to help the team prepare faster and make renewal conversations more consistent.

This map gives you enough structure to explore ChatGPT's role without jumping too quickly into implementation details.

## Slide 12/42 - Finding the friction points

After mapping the workflow, identify where the work slows down, varies in quality, or depends on manual effort.

Friction often appears when people have to move between disconnected sources, turn messy information into a usable format, or compare materials that were not designed to work together.

It can also show up when teams repeatedly prepare summaries, check work for consistency, or rely on experts to translate complex knowledge into clear outputs.

Friction can also show up as quality variation. Two people may complete the same workflow, but one output is clear and complete while another misses risks, uses the wrong tone, or omits the next action.

That kind of inconsistency is often a strong signal that the customer needs a more repeatable way to produce, review, and improve the output.

When you hear a customer describe friction, listen for the repeated pattern behind it. "It takes too long to prepare these briefs" may really mean: "People have to gather information from multiple places, synthesize it under time pressure, and produce a consistent leadership-ready output."

That is a workflow opportunity.

## Slide 13/42 - Adding workflow context

The same ChatGPT pattern can look very different depending on customer context. "Summarize documents" might mean contract review support in legal, renewal brief preparation in customer success, financial commentary preparation in finance, or policy brief creation in the public sector.

To understand the workflow clearly, scan for these context points:
- Business function: Which team owns the workflow?
- Vertical context: What industry-specific pressures, constraints, or expectations matter?
- User group: Who will actually use ChatGPT in the workflow?
- Business context and sources: What knowledge, documents, systems, or source materials would the workflow depend on?
- Governance need: What data, policy, permission, review, or approval considerations may affect the workflow?

After determining context, you can also scan for these success signals to better communicate how your workflow will benefit the customer:
- Measurable outcome: What would improve if the workflow worked better?
- Monitoring or improvement signal: How would the customer know whether the workflow is being used correctly and producing useful results?

These questions don't turn you into the deployment team. They simply help you have a better seller-level conversation. You are not designing the full solution; you are clarifying whether the workflow is concrete enough to explore further.

Before moving on, check whether the workflow is clear enough to discuss with the customer. There should be a likely workflow owner, identifiable inputs, a user group that feels the pain, and review or approval expectations that are clear enough for a next conversation.

## Slide 14/42 - Adding workflow context: Real-world example

A customer says, "We want ChatGPT to summarize documents."

That statement is too broad. The context changes the recommendation.

**Legal**

In legal, "summarize documents" may mean creating first-pass contract summaries for attorney review. The governance need may include confirming which contract data can be used and what review steps are required before any summary is shared.

**Customer success**

In customer success, the same phrase may mean preparing renewal briefs from account notes, support history, and usage summaries. The governance need may focus on access to account data and human review before leadership meetings.

**Public sector**

In the public sector, the same phrase may mean summarizing policy documents where accuracy, accessibility, and approval are critical. The measurable outcome may not be only speed; it may also include consistency, review quality, and stakeholder confidence.

The pattern is similar, but the workflow, users, risks, and success measures are all different. That is why context matters before you recommend which workflow to explore first.

## Slide 18/42 - Designing prompt-to-output patterns that work for customers

After you map the workflow, the next step is to explain how ChatGPT could practically support the work.

This means connecting the user's goal, context, and instructions to the work ChatGPT supports, the output or action produced, and the point where a person reviews, approves, or escalates. That connection is called a prompt-to-output pattern.

In this module, you'll learn how to identify common prompt-to-output patterns and use them to describe ChatGPT's role in a workflow clearly, without jumping into technical setup or feature detail.

## Slide 19/42 - What is a prompt-to-output pattern?

A prompt-to-output pattern is the seller-friendly way to explain the AI work pattern in a ChatGPT workflow. It connects the user's input and instruction to the business output ChatGPT helps produce.

This matters because customers do not need a feature list at this point. They need to understand how ChatGPT could fit into work they already do.

A prompt-to-output pattern helps you say, "Here is the type of support ChatGPT would provide, and here is the output the user would review or use."

ChatGPT can support both collaborative and delegated workflow patterns. In ChatGPT Chat, the user works with ChatGPT step by step to explore, draft, critique, and refine.

In ChatGPT Work, the user defines an outcome, provides context and boundaries, and reviews completed work. Where eligible Workspace Agent capabilities are available and enabled, a workflow may also use approved apps or tools, run on a schedule or trigger, and pause at action constraints, approvals, or human escalation points.

"ChatGPT can help finance" is too broad a recommendation.

A clearer prompt-to-output pattern instead would be: "A finance manager provides budget data, actuals, and notes. ChatGPT identifies major variances and produces a first-pass commentary for finance review."

This explanation is more practical. It names the input, the work ChatGPT supports, the output, and the review expectation.

Another weak example might be: "ChatGPT can help legal review contracts."

A clearer prompt-to-output pattern instead would be: "A legal operations user provides a vendor contract and the company's review checklist. ChatGPT helps extract key terms and flag missing information. The output is a first-pass contract summary for legal review."

## Slide 20/42 - Common ChatGPT prompt-to-output patterns

Several common prompt-to-output patterns show up in customer workflows.

- Summarize and synthesize: ChatGPT turns multiple sources into a concise summary. This pattern is useful when people spend time reading across documents, notes, tickets, transcripts, or research.
- Draft and refine: ChatGPT creates a first draft, then helps improve tone, structure, clarity, or completeness. This pattern is useful when users repeatedly create similar documents, messages, briefs, or communications.
- Compare and recommend: ChatGPT compares options and produces a recommendation or decision support. This pattern is useful when users must evaluate alternatives, risks, requirements, or tradeoffs.
- Extract and structure: ChatGPT pulls key information into a table, brief, checklist, or template. This pattern is useful when people need to turn messy information into a consistent format.
- Prepare and brief: ChatGPT turns background information into a meeting, account, project, or leadership brief. This pattern is useful when users prepare for recurring customer, internal, or executive conversations.
- Review and improve: ChatGPT checks an artifact against criteria, policy, tone, completeness, or quality standards. This pattern is useful when the customer already has standards but review is slow or inconsistent.
- Guide and coach: ChatGPT helps a user work through a process, prepare for a conversation, or improve an output step by step. This pattern is useful when expert guidance is limited or when users need support applying a process consistently.

These patterns are not mutually exclusive. A single workflow may combine several patterns.

## Slide 21/42 - Map these AI work patterns to your workflow

To identify the right prompt-to-output pattern, ask: What is the user trying to produce, improve, decide, or complete at this step in the workflow?

In a renewal brief workflow: ChatGPT may summarize and synthesize account notes, extract and structure renewal risks, prepare and brief the customer success manager, and review and improve the final brief for completeness.

In a campaign brief workflow: ChatGPT may synthesize audience insights, draft and refine campaign angles, extract key product messages into a template, and prepare channel-specific copy for review.

The point is not to overcomplicate the workflow. Instead, you're aiming to describe ChatGPT's role in terms the customer can recognize: what information goes in, what work happens, what output comes out, and where human review remains important.

A practical pattern statement often follows this structure:

"User provides [inputs]. ChatGPT helps [type of work]. The output is [business artifact] for [user/reviewer] to review, use, or improve."

For a delegated pattern, use a second structure:

"User defines [outcome], provides [context and boundaries], and ChatGPT completes [multi-step work] using [approved apps or tools]. The workflow pauses for [approval or escalation], and the output is [business artifact or action] for [reviewer] to validate."

For example: "The customer success manager provides account notes, support history, renewal risks, and usage summaries. ChatGPT helps summarize and structure the information. The output is a renewal brief for the manager to review before an executive account meeting."

## Slide 22/42 - Prompt-to-output patterns: Real-world example

A finance team spends time preparing monthly variance commentary. The current process is manual: gather budget data, compare actuals, read notes from business owners, identify major drivers, and write commentary for leadership review.

A useful prompt-to-output pattern could be:

"The finance manager provides budget data, actuals, variance thresholds, and business-owner notes. ChatGPT helps compare results, identify major variances, and draft first-pass commentary. The output is a review-ready variance commentary that finance can check before sharing with leadership."

This is stronger than saying, "ChatGPT can analyze finance data," because it explains the workflow in business terms. It shows the inputs, the AI-supported work, the output, and the human review step.

## Slide 26/42 - Prioritizing repeatable and high-adoption workflows

Not every workflow is equally strong as a ChatGPT opportunity. Some may be useful once, but others are repeated often, painful for users, easy to understand, and clear enough to validate.

Before recommending a workflow to a customer, you need to judge whether it is likely to be reused and adopted. That means looking at how often the work happens, whether the output is consistent, who owns the process, and whether users would clearly benefit from a better way of working.

In this module, you'll learn how to assess repeatability and adoption potential so you can identify which ChatGPT workflow is most worth exploring first.

## Slide 27/42 - What makes a workflow repeatable?

A repeatable workflow has a stable user group, a recurring trigger, consistent inputs, and a recognizable output.

Repeatability matters because ChatGPT value grows when teams can use it regularly in work they already do. A one-time task may be helpful, but it rarely creates strong evidence for a broader customer conversation. A repeatable workflow gives the customer more chances to validate improvement, refine the process, and build confidence.

Use four checks to assess repeatability:
- Frequency: Does this happen often enough to matter?
- Consistency: Are the inputs and outputs similar enough to standardize?
- Ownership: Is there a clear team or role that would use it?
- Execution pattern: Are the relevant apps or tools, schedule or trigger, and approval or escalation points clear enough to repeat when needed?

A weekly customer health review, monthly variance commentary, recurring campaign brief, or repeated policy summary request may be more repeatable than a one-off executive presentation. The stronger workflow is usually the one the team recognizes, repeats, and can evaluate over time.

## Slide 28/42 - What makes a workflow likely to be adopted?

A high-adoption workflow is easy for users to understand, useful in daily or weekly work, and connected to a real pain point.

Adoption is more likely when users already recognize the problem and can see how ChatGPT helps them produce a better output faster or more consistently. It's less likely when the workflow requires users to change too much at once, when the output is hard to review, or when ownership is unclear.

Use four checks to assess adoption likelihood:
- User pull: Do users already feel the pain and want a better way?
- Output clarity: Is the desired output easy to recognize and review?
- Low behavior change: Can users try the workflow without radically changing how they work?
- Team fit: Does the workflow support how the team already works, shares information, reviews outputs, or hands work off?

The best early workflow is not always the flashiest one. It is often the workflow that is familiar, repeated, painful, and easy to validate.

## Slide 29/42 - Check workflow context before prioritizing

Before recommending a workflow, confirm whether it is clear enough in context.

Use the context and success-signal questions you've already learned as a quick scan: business function, vertical context, user group, business context and sources, governance or review considerations, measurable outcome, and monitoring or improvement signal.

When two workflows both look promising, choose the one with the clearest repeated trigger, most recognizable output, strongest user pull, and easiest validation path.

A workflow that is moderately valuable but easy to repeat and review may be a better first recommendation than a more ambitious idea with unclear ownership or unclear source materials.

This step prevents vague recommendations. For example, "document support" is not detailed enough. In customer success, document support may mean retrieval and synthesis for renewal briefs.

In legal, it may mean review and evaluation against contract standards. In healthcare, it may require special care around data-use boundaries and review expectations. In the public sector, it may require accessibility, accuracy, and approval considerations.

A workflow should not be prioritized simply because ChatGPT can support it. It becomes a stronger candidate when the work happens repeatedly, users are likely to adopt the new way of working, the customer context is clear, and there is a practical way to check whether the output is improving.

## Slide 30/42 - Repeatable and high-adoption workflows: Real-world example

A marketing team occasionally asks ChatGPT for campaign ideas. That may be useful, but it is not yet a strong workflow. The trigger is vague, the output may vary, and the team may not have a consistent way to review or use the ideas.

A better candidate is campaign brief development.

The team gathers audience insights, summarizes product messaging, drafts campaign angles, creates channel-specific copy, and prepares a review-ready brief.

That workflow is stronger because it is recurring, has a clear user group, produces a recognizable output, and fits work the team already does. It also gives the customer a clearer way to validate improvement: reduced brief preparation time, more consistent campaign inputs, faster review cycles, or better alignment between product messaging and campaign execution.

## Slide 34/42 - Building a workflow recommendation for customers

After you identify a strong workflow, the final step is to explain why it should be explored first.

A good workflow recommendation helps the customer see the connection between the work they do today, the role ChatGPT could play, and the outcome they should validate next. It should be specific enough to guide the next conversation, but not so detailed that it becomes a deployment plan.

In this module, you'll learn how to build a concise workflow recommendation that names the user group, workflow, source materials, AI work pattern, governance or review consideration, measurable outcome, and next step.

## Slide 35/42 - What a workflow recommendation includes

A workflow recommendation is a concise explanation of which ChatGPT workflow should be explored first and why.

It is not a deployment plan, technical design, or detailed adoption strategy. It is a seller recommendation that helps the customer and internal teams understand the workflow opportunity clearly enough to continue the conversation.

Later conversations may go deeper into value positioning, readiness, rollout assumptions, and handoff. At this stage, your role is to make the workflow opportunity clear, evidence-based, and easy for others to build on.

A comprehensive workflow recommendation should state:

- Business function and target user group
- Workflow to improve
- Work object moving through the workflow
- Business context or source materials the workflow depends on
- Prompt-to-output pattern or patterns involved
- ChatGPT interaction and execution pattern — collaborative or delegated, including any relevant apps or tools, schedule or trigger, and approval or escalation point
- Why the workflow is repeatable
- Why users are likely to adopt it
- The relevant governance or review consideration
- The measurable outcome to validate
- How the customer could monitor whether the workflow is working as intended
- What should be confirmed next

This may sound like a lot, but the recommendation itself should be short. You do not need a long report. You need a clear paragraph that connects workflow, user value, governance awareness, and next-step validation.

## Slide 36/42 - Workflow recommendations: Real-world example

A customer success leader says their managers spend too much time preparing for executive account reviews. Each manager gathers everything they need like account notes from different places. The reviews are important, but preparation is inconsistent, and leadership wants clearer renewal briefs before key customer conversations.

A strong workflow recommendation could be:

"Start with renewal brief preparation for customer success managers. This workflow happens before executive account reviews, depends on synthesizing account notes, support history, renewal risks, and product usage summaries, and produces a review-ready brief for manager review."

"ChatGPT could support a summarize-and-brief pattern that helps managers prepare faster and more consistently while keeping human review in the loop. The key governance consideration is confirming which account and support data can be used. The measurable outcome to validate is reduced preparation time and more consistent renewal brief quality."

"The next step is to confirm source inputs, review expectations, monitoring signals, and the initial customer success team to validate with."

This recommendation works because it is specific. It does not say "use ChatGPT for customer success." It identifies the workflow, the users, the repeated trigger, the inputs, the output, the AI work pattern, the governance consideration, the measurable outcome, and the next validation step.

It also keeps the scope at the right level. It does not include workspace setup, admin configuration, integration design, rollout sequencing, or detailed adoption measurement. Those decisions belong in a later deployment conversation.

## Slide 37/42 - Remember to keep the recommendation practical

A practical recommendation stays close to the customer's work.

Avoid saying, "Deploy ChatGPT across the department to improve productivity." That is too broad. It does not identify the workflow, user group, source materials, review expectations, or measurable outcome.

A stronger recommendation says which workflow should be explored first and why. It names the user group, the repeated work, the information required, the output, and the outcome to validate.

The recommendation should also show seller-level responsibility. That means you should acknowledge governance and review considerations without trying to solve every deployment question.

For example, you can say that the customer should confirm which account data, contract data, policy materials, or internal notes can be used before the workflow is piloted or expanded.

A practical workflow recommendation gives the next team enough clarity to continue discovery. It does not pretend all implementation decisions have already been made.
