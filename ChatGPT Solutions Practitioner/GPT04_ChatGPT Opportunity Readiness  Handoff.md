## Slide 5/49 - Assess ChatGPT opportunity readiness

Before a ChatGPT opportunity can move into structured handoff, you need to know whether the evidence is strong enough to support that move.

This module helps you separate a promising idea from a handoff-ready opportunity. You'll learn what readiness means, how to assess readiness signals, and how to identify whether a gap should be resolved before the appropriate next owner continues the conversation.

The goal here is not to slow down good opportunities. You're aiming to reduce ambiguity so the next team can act with confidence.

## Slide 6/49 - What readiness means

Readiness is not the same as fit. A ChatGPT opportunity may be a strong fit and still not be ready for handoff.

For example, a customer success team may have a real workflow pain: account teams spend hours preparing renewal briefs from scattered account notes, support history, product usage summaries, and renewal risks.

ChatGPT may be a good first path because the work involves knowledge synthesis, preparation, drafting, and review inside an employee-facing workflow.

That does not automatically mean the opportunity is ready for structured handoff to the appropriate next owner.

Before handoff, you need enough evidence to answer a few practical questions: what problem is the customer trying to solve? Which workflow would ChatGPT support? Who experiences the pain? Who owns the business outcome? Why does the opportunity matter? What value should the customer validate? What has the customer agreed to do next? What source materials, systems, access boundaries, review expectations, or policy considerations may shape the workflow?

A ready opportunity has a clear story. Someone outside the customer conversation should be able to read your notes and understand the opportunity without guessing what the customer meant.

A ready opportunity should also make the operating context visible enough for handoff. In this course, operating context means the business context ChatGPT would need in order to support the workflow responsibly. This might include company knowledge, policies, documents, workflows, systems, permissions, user groups, and human review expectations.

At this stage, you are not designing the deployment. You are making sure the right questions and assumptions are visible before the next team takes over.

## Slide 7/49 - Use the readiness signal check

A ChatGPT opportunity is ready to progress when the core evidence is clear enough to support a structured next step.

Use these readiness signals to assess the opportunity:

1. **Business problem clarity** — Is the problem specific, workflow-based, and important? A strong problem statement explains what is happening today, who is impacted, and why it matters. It should not sound like a generic AI request.
2. **Stakeholders identified** — Are the operators, outcome owner, decision influencers, and sponsor visible? A ready opportunity should show who experiences the workflow pain, who owns the business outcome, who may influence access or approval, and whether a senior sponsor is identified or still missing.
3. **Initial ChatGPT experience and use case framed** — Is the use case described in business language, and is the intended interaction pattern clear? The use case should explain what work changes, where it lives in the workflow, and whether users will collaborate with ChatGPT Chat or delegate a multi-step body of knowledge work in ChatGPT Work. For delegated work, the handoff should make the intended outcome, context, boundaries, available sources or tools, approval points, expected output, and review standard visible. Avoid leading with a feature list, model names, or technical design.
4. **Customer engagement signal** — Has the customer agreed to a concrete next step? Interest is not enough. A strong engagement signal might be an agreed workflow discovery session, agreement to bring in IT or security, a commitment to share sample process details, or a scheduled validation discussion with the right stakeholders.
5. **Early value hypothesis** — Is there a credible explanation of what improves? The value hypothesis does not need to be a final ROI case. It should logically connect the problem, workflow, and expected outcome.
6. **Operating context and governance assumptions** — Is there an early view of the source materials, intended ChatGPT experience, capability availability, administrator enablement, user access, permission questions, review expectations, or policy considerations that may shape the workflow? For seller-level readiness, this does not mean defining a complete governance model. It means making the right questions visible, such as: Which documents or systems might ChatGPT need to reference? Is the intended ChatGPT experience available and enabled for the target users? What data sensitivity, permission, or review constraints might matter?

Classifying your readiness signals: for each signal, classify the evidence as either **Strong** (clear enough to support handoff or a structured next step), **Developing** (directionally useful, but needs more evidence), or **Missing** (not yet evidenced and should be resolved before handoff).

You shouldn't be aiming to mark every signal "strong" at all costs. The goal is to make a clear, evidence-based recommendation about what should happen next.

## Slide 8/49 - Readiness signals: real-world example

A compliance team says it wants to "use ChatGPT for document review."

That is not enough to progress. The phrase "document review" is too broad. It does not explain the workflow, the users, the value, the source materials, or the customer's next step.

A stronger readiness signal would sound like this: "Compliance analysts spend several hours reviewing policy documents and drafting risk summaries for internal audit preparation. The Head of Compliance owns the turnaround-time outcome. IT and security will need to review data access constraints because the workflow may involve sensitive internal policy documents." "The customer has agreed to a workflow discovery session with compliance, IT, and security, using sample document types to validate the process."

Notice what changed. The stronger version does not just say "ChatGPT for document review." It clarifies: the user group (compliance analysts); the workflow (reviewing policy documents and drafting risk summaries); the business outcome (faster audit preparation and reduced turnaround time); the outcome owner (Head of Compliance); the decision influencers (IT and security); the operating context (internal policy documents and access constraints); the engagement signal (agreed workflow discovery session with sample document types).

This is the difference between a promising idea and a handoff-ready opportunity.

## Slide 12/49 - Introduction

A ChatGPT opportunity can stall when the right people are not involved at the right time.

In this module, you'll learn how to identify the stakeholder roles that usually matter before a ChatGPT opportunity moves forward. You'll also practice spotting alignment gaps that could delay handoff, especially when the workflow depends on business ownership, source materials, permissions, review expectations, or governance input.

You do not need every stakeholder to approve every detail before handoff. But you do need to know who matters, who is already involved, and which stakeholder gaps must be resolved next.

## Slide 13/49 - Identify the stakeholder roles

Stakeholder alignment does not mean every stakeholder has approved the opportunity. At this stage, it means you have identified the people who matter and understand whether any critical roles are missing.

For a credible ChatGPT handoff, look for four common stakeholder roles:

**Operators** — These are the people who experience the workflow pain day to day. They may be customer success managers preparing renewal briefs, support agents drafting responses, analysts writing summaries, legal operations teams reviewing contracts, or finance teams preparing commentary. Operators help validate what work actually happens, where friction appears, and what outputs users need.

**Outcome owner** — This is the person accountable for the business result. The outcome owner may be a VP of Customer Success, Head of Support, Finance Director, Head of Compliance, Legal Operations Lead, or business unit leader. The outcome owner matters because ChatGPT value should connect to a business outcome, not just user interest.

**Decision influencers** — These are the stakeholders who may influence whether the opportunity can move forward. Common decision influencers include IT, security, legal, procurement, compliance, data owners, revenue operations, or business systems teams. Decision influencers often shape questions such as access, permissions, data boundaries, source systems, review requirements, and approval steps.

**Sponsor** — This is a senior leader who can create urgency or unblock progress. Not every early opportunity will have a confirmed sponsor, but sponsor status should be visible. If the sponsor is unknown or only assumed, document that as a gap.

Each opportunity is different. Some opportunities may need additional stakeholder roles depending on the workflow, customer organization, industry, and data sensitivity. The important point is to avoid handing off an opportunity where the stakeholder map is mostly guesswork.

## Slide 14/49 - Spot any alignment gaps

A stakeholder gap can make a strong workflow look weaker than it is.

Common alignment gaps include:

- Only one curious contact is involved.
- Operators are clear, but the outcome owner is unknown.
- The outcome owner is interested, but the actual users have not validated the workflow pain.
- IT or security concerns are likely but have not been surfaced.
- Legal, compliance, or procurement may matter but have not been identified.
- The customer has interest but no named owner for the next step.
- The sponsor is assumed rather than identified.
- The stakeholder present does not match the workflow pattern.

The last point is especially important.

For example, if the opportunity is about preparing customer-facing response drafts, legal, compliance, or quality review may be relevant.

If the opportunity depends on account context from CRM, call notes, or support history, revenue operations, IT, or data owners may need to be involved.

If the opportunity involves sensitive policy or risk material, security and compliance may need to validate boundaries before the next-stage conversation goes deeper.

You do not need to solve these issues at this stage, but you do need to make them visible.

A useful stakeholder note might say: "Operators and outcome owner are identified. IT and security are likely decision influencers because the workflow may use customer account context from multiple systems. Security has not yet been engaged, so stakeholder alignment should happen before structured handoff."

That kind of note helps the next team understand the opportunity without discovering the gap later.

## Slide 15/49 - Stakeholder alignment: real-world example

A customer success team wants ChatGPT to help prepare renewal briefs. The opportunity sounds plausible because the workflow involves repeated knowledge synthesis, drafting, and preparation. But before handoff, the stakeholder roles need to be clear.

A stronger stakeholder map might look like this:

- **Operators** — Customer success managers who prepare renewal briefs.
- **Outcome owner** — VP of Customer Success, who owns renewal readiness and account quality.
- **Decision influencers** — Revenue operations, because source data may come from CRM or account systems; IT and security, because customer data access and permissions may need review.
- **Sponsor** — VP of Customer Success identified, but executive sponsorship beyond the function is not yet confirmed.

If the VP of Customer Success is not engaged and IT/security are unknown, the opportunity may need more alignment before handoff.

This does not mean the opportunity is bad. It means the next step should focus on stakeholder alignment rather than pretending the handoff is fully ready.

## Slide 19/49 - Introduction

A clear handoff should explain what the customer is trying to improve.

In this module, you'll learn how to define seller-level success metrics for a ChatGPT opportunity. These metrics do not need to be a full ROI model or deployment measurement plan; they should simply make the expected value clear enough for the customer and the next team to validate.

Effective success metrics connect ChatGPT to a workflow output, not to generic AI usage. They help everyone understand what better, faster, safer, or more consistent work should look like.

## Slide 20/49 - Why success metrics matter

A handoff should include what the customer is trying to improve.

Without success metrics, the appropriate next owner may inherit a vague use case that is hard to activate, validate, or prioritize. At this stage, the seller and customer should define what needs to be validated next.

**Weak metrics:** A weak metric focuses on generic ChatGPT usage. Some weak examples include: "Increase ChatGPT adoption." "Use ChatGPT more often." "Launch an AI assistant." "Save time across the business."

**Stronger metrics:** Meanwhile, a stronger metric connects the workflow to the business output. Some stronger examples look like this: "Reduce first-draft renewal brief preparation time." "Improve consistency of support response drafts." "Reduce time spent preparing monthly variance commentary." "Increase confidence that policy summaries follow approved source material."

Notice the difference. Useful seller-level success metrics name the workflow output and what should improve. They help the customer and the next team understand what value should be validated.

## Slide 21/49 - Use simple success metric categories

You can define seller-level success metrics using a few practical categories.

1. **Time and Speed** — Does the workflow take less time, and does the workflow move faster from trigger to output? Examples: reduced preparation time, reduced review time, faster decision support, faster briefing preparation.
2. **Quality** — Are outputs more complete, accurate, or useful? Examples: more complete renewal briefs, more consistent support response drafts, better-structured policy summaries, fewer avoidable revision cycles.
3. **Capacity** — Can the team complete more work without relying only on specialists or adding headcount? Examples: more proposals prepared by account teams, more support drafts reviewed by team leads, more internal summaries produced by analysts.
4. **Risk** — Does the workflow support better review discipline, policy alignment, or controlled use? Examples: more consistent use of approved source material, clearer human review before customer-facing use, better visibility into policy-sensitive outputs, fewer unreviewed drafts moving forward.
5. **Experience** — Does the workflow improve the employee or customer experience? Examples: easier preparation for account teams, faster responses for customers, less repetitive work for analysts, more useful internal briefings for leaders.

Choose the metric category that fits the workflow. Do not force every category into the handoff.

## Slide 22/49 - Keep success metrics realistic

At handoff, success metrics should be specific enough to guide the next conversation, but they do not need to be a finalized measurement plan.

Avoid unsupported claims like: "This will save $5 million." "This will reduce work by 80%." "This will transform the entire department." "This will eliminate the need for review."

Unless the customer has provided validated assumptions, those claims create risk and can reduce credibility. Instead, use measured, directional language: "Success could be validated by comparing renewal brief preparation time before and after workflow testing." "The customer wants to validate whether draft quality becomes more consistent across account managers." "The next team should confirm whether reduced revision cycles is the right success measure." "The customer expects faster monthly commentary preparation, but the baseline and target still need validation."

A strong seller-level success metric should answer: what workflow output should improve? What type of improvement matters? Who cares about that improvement? What still needs validation?

This keeps the handoff useful without overstating the impact.

## Slide 23/49 - Success metrics: real-world example

A finance team wants ChatGPT to help prepare monthly variance commentary.

In this context, a weak success metric would be: "Use ChatGPT to make finance more productive."

A stronger seller-level success metric would be: "Validate whether ChatGPT can reduce first-draft preparation time for monthly variance commentary, reduce revision cycles, and improve consistency of commentary across business units."

This metric is stronger because it names: the workflow (monthly variance commentary); the user activity (first-draft preparation); the output (commentary across business units); the expected improvement (reduced preparation time, fewer revision cycles, improved consistency).

The handoff should also state that these metrics still need customer validation. That might sound like: "Success metrics are directional. Finance leadership wants to validate reduced preparation time, fewer revision cycles, and improved commentary consistency. Baselines and target measures have not yet been confirmed."

That level of clarity is enough to guide the next conversation without turning the seller's role into a full measurement plan.

## Slide 27/49 - What rollout assumptions are

Rollout assumptions are early beliefs about how the customer might start using ChatGPT. They are not a final launch plan.

Your rollout assumptions as a seller help the appropriate next owner understand what appears likely, what has been confirmed, and what still needs validation.

Good rollout assumptions answer practical questions like: which workflow is likely to be activated first? Which user group may be involved? Why is this a practical starting point? Which ChatGPT experience—collaborative Chat or delegated Work—is likely to support the workflow? What output do users need? What business outcome does the workflow support? What source materials, systems, or business context may matter? What governance or review considerations may need validation? Which capability, connection, administrator enablement, user access, permission, monitoring, or escalation question may need to be confirmed before broader use?

You are not responsible for designing that operating model in this course. Instead, you're responsible for making sure the handoff captures the assumptions that the next team must validate.

Enterprise value does not come from treating ChatGPT as an isolated productivity tool.

It comes from understanding where intelligence can be embedded into real workflows, with the right context, controls, review expectations, and operating model around it.

## Slide 29/49 - Capture readiness and risk assumptions

After documenting the first workflow and user group, capture assumptions that may affect readiness.

These may include: intended ChatGPT experience and interaction pattern; capability availability, administrator enablement, required connections, and user access; source systems or documents that may be needed; business context ChatGPT may need to produce useful outputs; data sensitivity or policy constraints; access or permission boundaries; human review requirements; stakeholders who still need to validate feasibility; known blockers that could slow handoff; how outputs should be reviewed before affecting customers, systems, or business decisions; how issues, incorrect outputs, or policy concerns should be surfaced; how the customer might monitor whether the workflow is being used appropriately.

Keep these assumptions neutral.

Do not write: "ChatGPT will access the customer's CRM and generate final renewal briefs for customer use." That statement overreaches. It treats assumptions as facts and implies a final workflow design.

Write something like this instead: "The workflow may require account context from CRM and support history. Source access, permission boundaries, and review expectations need validation. The customer expects briefs to remain human-reviewed before use."

The second version is safer and more useful because it separates what appears likely from what still needs to be confirmed.

## Slide 30/49 - Turn assumptions into handoff-ready notes

A handoff-ready assumptions note should make three things clear:

- **Known** — What the customer has clearly described or confirmed.
- **Assumed** — What appears likely but has not yet been validated.
- **Needs validation** — What must be confirmed before the opportunity can progress confidently.

Use neutral wording. Avoid presenting assumptions as facts, or prescribing the deployment plan. You should also avoid hiding blockers or open questions, naming detailed configuration choices, or making legal, security, or compliance claims that have not been validated.

A simple structure looks like this:

- **Known** — Legal analysts spend time preparing first-pass vendor agreement summaries. The legal operations manager wants faster preparation and more consistent summary structure.
- **Assumed** — Vendor agreement summaries may be a practical first workflow. Legal analysts may be the first user group. Human review is expected before outputs are used.
- **Needs validation** — Contract source access, review requirements, clause-level manual review expectations, and legal/security approval process.

This structure helps the next team clearly see where to focus the next conversation.

## Slide 36/49 - Match readiness to the right next step

Not every ChatGPT opportunity should be handed off immediately. Your recommendation should match the evidence present in your specific opportunity.

Use these practical next-step options:

**Ready for structured handoff** — Use this when the problem, workflow, stakeholders, value, rollout assumptions, and next step are clear enough for the appropriate next owner to continue the conversation. Example signal: The customer has confirmed the workflow pain, identified the operators and outcome owner, involved the right decision influencers, agreed to a structured handoff discussion, and clarified what value should be validated.

**Needs focused discovery** — Use this when one or more readiness signals are developing but fixable. Example signal: The customer has a promising workflow, but the exact process, input sources, or output expectations are still unclear.

**Needs stakeholder alignment** — Use this when the workflow is promising, but the right owner, sponsor, or decision influencer is missing. Example signal: Operators are enthusiastic, but the outcome owner is not engaged, or IT/security needs to be involved before handoff.

**Needs value clarification** — Use this when the use case is plausible, but the measurable outcome is unclear. Example signal: The customer wants ChatGPT to "help teams work faster," but has not clarified which workflow output should improve or how they will validate success.

**Not ready yet** — Use this when the opportunity is still generic, feature-led, or exploratory. Example signal: The customer says they want "an AI assistant," but cannot name the workflow, user group, business problem, value, or next step.

A simple evidence-to-next-step pattern can help: if the evidence is clear and validated enough, recommend handoff. If the evidence shows a specific gap, recommend the next step that resolves that gap. If the evidence is too weak or generic, recommend further discovery before progression.

## Slide 42/49 - What a handoff summary includes

The handoff summary brings together readiness evidence, stakeholder alignment, success metrics, rollout assumptions, open questions, and the recommended next step.

A strong ChatGPT opportunity handoff summary should include:

- **Customer problem** — What is happening today, who is impacted, and why it matters.
- **Priority workflow or workflows** — Which ChatGPT-supported workflow should be explored first, whether the intended interaction is collaborative Chat or delegated Work, where it lives in the current process, and what output is expected.
- **Target users and stakeholders** — Who will use the workflow, who owns the business outcome, which decision influencers may matter, and whether sponsor status is clear or still developing.
- **Value hypothesis** — What improvement the customer expects and why that improvement is credible based on the workflow pain.
- **Readiness signals** — Which evidence is strong, developing, or missing across problem clarity, stakeholder alignment, use case framing, customer engagement, value, and operating context.
- **Business context, product, and access assumptions** — What knowledge, documents, systems, or data sources may matter for the workflow; which capabilities or connections must be available and enabled; and whether intended users have access.
- **Governance and review assumptions** — What access, permission, policy, review, approval, or monitoring questions may need validation before broader use.
- **Success metrics** — What value the customer should validate, such as reduced preparation time, improved consistency, faster review, better knowledge access, or clearer human review discipline.
- **Initial rollout assumptions** — The likely first workflow, first user group, and practical starting point, clearly separated from anything that still needs validation.
- **Open questions or readiness risks** — What is still unknown or could slow the next-stage conversation if not addressed.
- **Recommended next step for the appropriate next owner** — What should happen next, who should be involved, and what evidence or decisions should be confirmed.

This may look like a lot, but your final summary should still be concise. Remember you are not writing a full, comprehensive deployment plan; you are writing a clear handoff note so other teams can quickly understand the opportunity.

## Slide 43/49 - Keep the handoff concise

The handoff summary should be practical, not exhaustive. It should help someone who was not in the customer conversation understand the opportunity and continue the right conversation quickly.

Avoid including things like a detailed workspace configuration or a final deployment design.

Don't make any unsupported ROI claims, or provide unvalidated technical assumptions. Don't create long product feature lists, as this isn't an effective way of being specific about the opportunity.

Avoid any generic claims about AI productivity, as these aren't useful for the customer. Finally, don't include any internal notes that do not help the next team act.

A strong handoff summary usually fits into a few short paragraphs or a structured set of concise bullets.

Before sending it, ask: could someone outside the conversation understand the problem? Is the workflow specific? Are the key stakeholders named or gaps clearly stated? Is the value directional but realistic? Are assumptions separated from confirmed facts? Are governance and review questions visible? Is the recommended next step clear?

If the answer is yes, the summary is likely useful.
