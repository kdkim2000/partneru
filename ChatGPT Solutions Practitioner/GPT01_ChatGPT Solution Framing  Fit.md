## Slide 6/51 - Recognize ChatGPT-fit opportunity patterns

Early ChatGPT opportunities often sound simple. A potential customer might say "We want an AI assistant," "Can ChatGPT help our sales team?" or "We need a copilot for operations." Those are useful starting signals, but they aren't enough to confirm fit.

In this module, you'll learn how to recognize ChatGPT-fit opportunity patterns in a seller-friendly way. You'll focus on the workflow pain behind the request, the business users involved, the output the customer needs, and the operating context that would shape safe use.

The key question you need to think about is:
What does the AI need to return, change, or complete for the business user in this workflow?

## Slide 7/51 - How to recognize ChatGPT-fit opportunity patterns

ChatGPT-fit opportunities usually involve people or teams who need a governed way to think, create, access knowledge, or delegate knowledge work using relevant context, tools, and human review.

Remember that a strong ChatGPT-fit opportunity is grounded in workflow pain—not a feature request or generic interest in AI.

**Strong ChatGPT-fit signals**

A customer problem may be a strong ChatGPT fit when you hear signals like:

- "Our team spends too much time on the same manual prep work each week."
- "The information we need is spread across documents, meeting notes, systems, and teams."
- "Researching, drafting, summarizing, and preparing materials takes longer than it should."
- "Different people create the same type of output in different ways, so quality varies."
- "We have trusted internal knowledge, but teams struggle to find and reuse it."
- "Business users need a safe, shared place to use AI for everyday work."
- "We know which team does this work today, and they feel the pain directly."
- "The workflow depends on specific sources, such as CRM notes, project documents, policies, support tickets, or internal knowledge bases."
- "We need guardrails around access, approved sources, and output review."

These signals matter because ChatGPT is strongest in the selling conversation when it connects to a repeatable way of working—whether users collaborate with ChatGPT or delegate a bounded knowledge-work outcome—not just a one-off productivity task.

**Start with the primary-output test**

Before treating a request as ChatGPT-fit, ask:

What does the AI need to return, change, or complete for the business user in this workflow?

This is the primary-output test, where you're asking what the AI should produce. It helps you move from vague labels to a concrete business output.

Not enough: "They want a chatbot."

Stronger: "Customer support managers need a trusted summary of common escalation themes from ticket notes so they can prepare weekly improvement actions."

Not enough: "They want a sales copilot."

Stronger: "Account teams need a synthesized account briefing before executive reviews, using call notes, account plans, and CRM exports."

The stronger version identifies the user, workflow, information context, and output. This information gives you a much clearer basis for deciding whether ChatGPT is likely to fit.

## Slide 8/51 - Common ChatGPT-fit patterns

ChatGPT-fit opportunities often show up through recurring patterns of work. These patterns are not product features. They are practical ways people may collaborate with ChatGPT or delegate work to it: producing, improving, retrieving, reviewing, synthesizing, or completing outputs in a governed environment.

**Knowledge access**

The AI helps produce a trusted answer, summary, synthesis, comparison, or briefing.

This pattern is common when teams spend too much time finding, interpreting, and reusing information across approved sources.

Examples:
- Preparing an account briefing from call notes, account plans, and customer history.
- Synthesizing internal policy guidance into a plain-language summary.
- Comparing updates across multiple documents before a leadership meeting.

**Work acceleration**

The AI helps produce or complete a draft, summary, analysis, plan, message, prepared material, or other review-ready deliverable.

This pattern is common when business users know the outcome they need, but producing it requires repeated research, synthesis, drafting, tool use, or review across several steps.

Examples:
- Drafting a customer follow-up email from meeting notes.
- Preparing a first-pass project update from status inputs.
- Summarizing a set of research notes into a planning memo.

**Team consistency**

The AI helps produce a reviewed, standardized, or policy-aligned output.

This pattern is common when different people produce similar outputs, but quality, structure, tone, or completeness varies.

Examples:
- Standardizing customer follow-up summaries.
- Helping teams apply approved guidance when drafting business documents.
- Producing more consistent first-pass summaries for review.

**Decision support**

The AI helps produce a comparison, recommendation, briefing, or next-step guidance.

This pattern is common when teams need to reason across information before making a decision or recommendation.

Examples:
- Comparing vendor options against a decision rubric.
- Preparing next-step guidance from support trends and account history.
- Synthesizing risks, tradeoffs, and open questions before stakeholder review.

Across these patterns, connected applications, tools, schedules, triggers, actions, approvals, or automation may be required. Treat them as capability and operating-model requirements to validate—not as standalone product routes.

## Slide 9/51 - ChatGPT opportunity patterns: real-world example

A customer's sales operations team spends hours preparing for executive account reviews.

Currently, the team searches across call notes, account plans, and CRM exports. Each account manager prepares the review differently, so leadership receives inconsistent information. Some account managers surface risks clearly. Others focus on activity updates without explaining what needs attention.

At first, the customer says: "We need an AI assistant for sales."

That is a weak signal on its own. It names an experience label, but not the workflow. A stronger framing you can use is:

"Sales operations needs a faster and more consistent way to synthesize call notes, account plans, and CRM exports into executive-ready account review briefs."

This suggests a ChatGPT-fit because the problem centers on knowledge synthesis, preparation, and repeatable team output. It also identifies the likely user group, the source materials, and the business output.

Before positioning ChatGPT in this situation, you would still validate:
- Which users prepare the reviews?
- Which source materials are approved and reliable?
- What does a good executive-ready brief include?
- Who reviews the output before it is used?
- What value would the customer expect—faster preparation, more consistent quality, better risk visibility, or another outcome?

## Slide 10/51 - Weak or incomplete ChatGPT-fit signals

Not every AI request is ready to be framed as a ChatGPT opportunity.

A request is weak if the customer says they need a "chatbot," "AI assistant," or "copilot," but cannot explain the workflow behind it. These labels describe an experience. They don't prove that a situation is a good fit for ChatGPT. When a customer uses a broad label, ask what sits underneath it.

A request needs more discovery if the customer can't explain:
- The affected user group.
- The workflow pain.
- The business impact.
- The likely source or data constraints.
- The next validation step.

**Weak signal example**

A customer says: "We want a chatbot for HR."

That is not yet enough to position ChatGPT.

You still need to know:
- Is the problem employee knowledge access, HR case triage, policy drafting, onboarding support, or something else?
- Who will use it—employees, HR business partners, managers, or another group?
- What information would it need to use?
- What output should it return?
- What review, governance, or permission expectations apply?
- What would make the customer confident enough to continue?

**Stronger signal example**

A stronger early signal would then be:

"HR business partners spend too much time answering repeated manager questions about internal policies. They want a governed way to help managers find and synthesize approved policy information, while keeping HR review expectations clear for sensitive cases."

This still needs validation, but it is much closer to a ChatGPT-fit opportunity because the workflow, users, source context, and governance considerations are visible.

## Slide 13/51 - Choosing the right solution for customers: ChatGPT vs other OpenAI solutions

Once you identify a possible AI opportunity, the next question is whether ChatGPT is the right first path.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes another OpenAI solution path may be more appropriate. And sometimes the right answer isn't clear yet because the customer problem, user experience, workflow ownership, or validation path needs more discovery.

In this module, you'll practice seller-level routing across ChatGPT Chat, ChatGPT Work, Codex, API-enabled experiences, and combined paths. You'll separate three decisions: the primary user or product surface, the capabilities the workflow requires, and the operating model needed to connect, govern, and run it.

## Slide 14/51 - Start with the work: ChatGPT or API?

A customer's first request does not always reveal the right solution path. They may say they want: "ChatGPT," "an AI assistant," "an agent," "automation," or "AI in our product."

Those labels are useful starting points, but they are not enough to decide where to start.

Before recommending a path, ask: What does the customer need AI to help people, products, systems, or teams do differently?

That question helps you place ChatGPT correctly within the broader OpenAI portfolio.

ChatGPT is an environment in which people can work with AI directly. ChatGPT Chat supports collaborative thinking and creation; ChatGPT Work supports delegated, multi-step knowledge work that uses context, tools, boundaries, and a review standard.

That makes ChatGPT a strong first product path when people need to collaborate with AI or delegate a knowledge-work outcome. The API may be more appropriate when intelligence must be embedded into a product, application, service, portal, or operational process.

**When ChatGPT may be the right first path**

ChatGPT may be the right first product path when business users need a governed way to work with AI directly—either collaboratively in Chat or by delegating bounded knowledge work in Work.

This usually means:
- Business users are the primary users.
- The work involves knowledge, reasoning, synthesis, drafting, analysis, preparation, review, or decision support.
- The customer needs a ChatGPT experience where people can collaborate with AI, delegate work, or steer and review a result.
- The workflow may use approved files, connected applications, tools, or actions, with permissions and human review appropriate to the customer context.
- The workflow can be explored with users before deeper technical design is required.
- The main value comes from helping people produce, improve, retrieve, review, synthesize, or complete business outputs more effectively.

Example: A customer success team wants a governed way to prepare account health summaries from internal notes, renewal history, support trends, and customer feedback before customer calls.

This points toward ChatGPT because the immediate need is for people to work with AI on a repeatable knowledge workflow, not to embed intelligence into a separate product or process. The next routing question is whether the team should collaborate in Chat or delegate more of the workflow in Work.

**When API may be more appropriate**

API may be more appropriate when the customer needs AI embedded into a product, application, service, portal, or business system.

This usually means:
- The AI experience must live inside an existing product, portal, application, or internal system.
- End users may never open ChatGPT directly.
- Product, application, or platform teams likely own the experience.
- The customer needs technical design around application behavior, data flow, user experience, and integration.
- The goal is embedded product or process intelligence, not primarily a direct ChatGPT experience for business users.

Example: A bank wants to embed an AI-powered research experience directly into an advisor portal so advisors can retrieve approved insights without leaving the application they already use.

This may point toward the API because the customer is asking for intelligence inside an existing product or system experience, not primarily for people to use ChatGPT directly.

You've just seen when API may be a better fit than ChatGPT. Next, you will look at two other cases where ChatGPT may not be the best first path: Codex for software delivery work and broader solution needs that may require another OpenAI path.

## Slide 15/51 - When Codex or another path may be a better fit

Some opportunities are not ChatGPT-first, even when ChatGPT could help with part of the work. Before routing, look at the primary users, where the work happens, who owns the workflow, and what kind of output the customer needs.

**When Codex may be more appropriate**

Codex may be more appropriate when the customer's main need is to transform software work. This usually means:
- Developers or engineering teams are the primary users.
- The work is part of the software development lifecycle.
- The customer needs support for writing, reviewing, testing, debugging, refactoring, or maintaining code.
- The business goal depends on improving engineering throughput, code quality, or delivery velocity.
- The opportunity is about software delivery transformation rather than general business-user productivity.

Example: An engineering team wants help reviewing pull requests, debugging production issues, improving test coverage, and refactoring legacy services.

This points toward Codex because the primary workflow is software delivery, not everyday business knowledge work.

**When capability or operating-model requirements change the route**

Do not treat agents, connected applications, open-weight models, migration, or infrastructure choices as peer product surfaces. First choose where the user or system will work, then identify the capabilities and operating model the solution requires.

This may change the recommendation when:
- People need ChatGPT to plan and carry out multi-step knowledge work across tools, with clear boundaries, approvals, and human review.
- The workflow needs connected applications, retrieval, actions, schedules, triggers, or automation that the selected experience must support.
- The customer is modernizing or migrating an existing AI system, application, or workflow.
- The customer has requirements for model choice, infrastructure, deployment environment, permissions, retention, residency, or operational control that need technical validation.
- The full problem spans multiple user surfaces, teams, systems, or operating-model decisions.

At this stage, you do not need to design the full solution. Your role is to identify the likely primary surface, the required capabilities, and the operating questions that must be validated next.

Next, you will look at situations where the answer may not be a single path at all.

## Slide 16/51 - When the answer may be hybrid

Some customer needs span more than one user group, workflow surface, or ownership model. In those situations, the seller-level recommendation may point to a hybrid path rather than one product path.

**When hybrid paths may become relevant**

A hybrid path may be relevant when one product path is not enough to support the full customer workflow.

For example, the customer may start by validating a business-user workflow in ChatGPT, then later decide to embed a proven experience into an internal application through API. Or a customer may need ChatGPT for business users, Codex for engineering teams, and an API path for embedded system experiences.

This usually means:
- The customer problem includes more than one user group or workflow surface.
- Business teams, product teams, engineering teams, or IT teams each own part of the problem.
- ChatGPT Chat or Work may help validate user value first, while API embedding, Codex engineering work, connected tools, migration, or a different operating model may become relevant later.
- The customer's governance, system, scale, or operating requirements cannot be met by one surface or team alone.

Example: A sales organization first validates a ChatGPT workflow that helps account teams prepare executive-ready account briefs. After proving value with sellers, the customer later explores embedding the same briefing experience into its CRM or account-planning system.

This may become a combined path because the team can validate the workflow in ChatGPT first, while the API may later support an embedded experience.

**A simple seller-level rule**

Use this rule to keep the comparison clear:

- Use ChatGPT Chat when people need to think, create, analyze, or refine work collaboratively.
- Use ChatGPT Work when people need to delegate bounded, multi-step knowledge work and review a finished or review-ready output.
- Use Codex when the primary need is software engineering, including planning, implementing, testing, reviewing, debugging, or maintaining code.
- Use the API when intelligence must be embedded into a product, application, system-to-system workflow, custom user experience, or operational process.
- Consider a combined path when the workflow spans user surfaces, tools, systems, teams, or control requirements. Then identify the required capabilities and operating model.

The goal is not to force every opportunity into one product label. The goal is to recommend the surface, capabilities, and operating model that best match the customer's workflow, users, desired experience, and next validation step.

This simple rule gives you a starting point. Next, you will compare solution paths more carefully using decision criteria.

## Slide 17/51 - Compare solution paths using decision criteria

At this stage, the goal is not to choose the product the customer mentioned first. It's to understand what the customer is trying to improve, who needs to use the solution, where the work happens, and what kind of output or experience is required. Once you have that information, you can determine if ChatGPT is the right approach or not.

Use decision criteria to compare solution paths before making a recommendation.

Use the criteria in three layers:
1. User or product surface, where people or systems will interact.
2. Required capabilities, what the workflow must do.
3. Operating model, how it must be connected, governed, and run.

The validation path tests whether the proposed combination can be tried credibly.

These criteria help you slow down and test the fit before recommending a solution. They keep the conversation anchored in the customer's workflow, desired output, user experience, ownership, and validation path.

**1. Primary business output/workflow**

Start by identifying what the customer needs the AI to help with. This is the primary business output.

Do not focus only on the interface the customer asks for. A customer may say they want a chatbot, assistant, copilot, embedded tool, or agent, but those labels don't explain what the business actually needs from the AI. Look for the business output behind the request.

For example:
- A customer asks for "a chatbot," but the real output may be a trusted answer from approved internal policy documents.
- A customer asks for "a sales assistant," but the real output may be a renewal briefing, customer follow-up email, or account-risk summary.
- A customer asks for "automation," but the real output may be a completed report, routed ticket, updated record, or structured recommendation.
- A customer asks for "developer productivity," but the real output may be code changes, pull request review, test coverage, or debugging support.

**2. User experience**

Next, clarify where and how the user needs to interact with the AI.

This helps distinguish among ChatGPT Chat, ChatGPT Work, Codex, an API-enabled experience, or a combined path. The same business output may require a different route depending on how the user or system needs to work.

For example, "summarize customer issues" could mean:
- A support manager collaborates in ChatGPT Chat to prepare a weekly leadership summary.
- A support manager delegates the multi-step preparation of a review-ready weekly summary in ChatGPT Work.
- A customer sees an AI-generated answer inside a support portal.
- An engineering team uses Codex to improve the support platform's issue-routing logic.
- A team starts in ChatGPT, then later embeds a validated workflow into another system.

This helps distinguish whether the customer needs a collaborative Chat experience, delegated Work experience, Codex, an API-enabled experience, or a combined path.

**3. Workflow ownership**

Then identify who owns the workflow or system the customer wants to improve.

Ownership matters because the route often depends on who is responsible for the work.

A business team may validate Chat or Work directly. A product or application team may need an API-enabled experience. An engineering team may need Codex. A cross-functional workflow may require a combined path or more discovery.

**4. Operating context**

Then, clarify what the AI would need to know or access to support the workflow both usefully and responsibly.

This is not detailed deployment planning. At this stage, you are identifying the business and control context that may shape the route.

Documents, systems, permissions, review steps, and governance requirements may affect whether Chat, Work, Codex, an API-enabled experience, or a combined path is appropriate and what must be validated next.

Remember that operating context also includes anything the AI needs to know about the business to support effectively.

**5. Integration surface**

Then clarify where the AI capability needs to live.

A workflow may fit ChatGPT Chat or Work even when it uses approved applications, tools, actions, schedules, or approvals.

If the experience must be embedded inside a customer-facing product, internal application, portal, or operational process, the API may be more appropriate.

If the primary work is software engineering, Codex may be the better surface.

**6. Validation path**

Finally, identify what the customer can reasonably test next.

The right first route is often the one that lets the customer validate useful work with the least confusion.

If people can test a collaborative workflow in Chat or a delegated workflow in Work, ChatGPT may be the right product path. If value cannot be tested without embedding intelligence into a system, an API-enabled or combined path may be needed. If the primary work is software engineering, Codex may be the better validation path.

If the answer is unclear, the opportunity needs more discovery before a solution path is recommended.

Use these criteria to compare ChatGPT Chat, ChatGPT Work, Codex, API-enabled experiences, and combined paths while keeping capability and operating-model requirements separate.

## Slide 18/51 - Avoid routing too early

Early framing should validate the customer problem first, before designing the final solution.

A feature request does not automatically determine the solution path. For example:
- "We need a chatbot" does not automatically mean ChatGPT.
- "We need automation" does not automatically mean API.
- "We need developer productivity" does not automatically mean Codex.
- "We need agents" does not automatically mean a standalone agentic workflow.

Each of those requests may be a starting point, but you still need to understand the workflow. Avoid routing to a specific solution before you've fully understood what the customer needs AI to solve.

**What to validate before routing**

Before recommending a route, test the three layers and the validation path:
- Workflow: What work is happening today?
- Output: What should the AI return, change, complete, or deliver?
- Primary surface: Where will people or systems interact with the AI?
- Required capabilities: Does the workflow need collaboration, delegation, connected applications, retrieval, actions, schedules, approvals, automation, or engineering tools?
- Ownership: Which team owns the workflow, product, or system?
- Operating model: What source boundaries, permissions, human review, infrastructure, retention, or migration requirements matter?
- Validation path: What can be tested next, and what evidence would support the route?

**Keep the recommendation at the right level**

Remember that your role at this stage is not to solve every technical detail. Your role is to create a clear, defensible recommendation about the likely path and what needs validation next.

A strong early recommendation might sound like:

"Based on what we know, ChatGPT is the right product path because the team needs to work with approved account information directly. ChatGPT Work may be the better starting experience if they want to delegate the multi-step preparation of review-ready briefs; ChatGPT Chat may fit if they want to synthesize and refine the brief interactively. Before we go deeper, we should validate approved sources, permissions, the review owner, and which work pattern the users prefer."

## Slide 19/51 - Choosing a solution: real-world example

A bank wants advisors to retrieve trusted internal research faster. At first, this sounds like a ChatGPT opportunity. The route depends on the primary surface, whether the work is collaborative or delegated, the required capabilities, and the operating model.

**Possible ChatGPT path**

ChatGPT Chat may fit if advisors need to ask questions, synthesize approved research, and refine briefing notes interactively. ChatGPT Work may fit if they need to delegate a multi-step research-and-briefing workflow and then review the completed output.

Either ChatGPT experience may make sense if the customer can validate the workflow with advisors before deciding whether to embed it elsewhere.

**Possible API path**

API may fit if the bank wants the experience embedded directly inside an advisor portal.

This path may make sense if the desired user experience must live in an existing application, with the AI output integrated into that system.

**Possible Codex path**

Codex is unlikely to be the first path if the problem is advisor research access rather than software delivery, code, testing, debugging, review, or engineering workflow transformation.

**Possible hybrid path**

A combined path may emerge if advisors validate the workflow in ChatGPT, while the bank later embeds a proven experience through the API or adds approved tools and actions.

**Seller-level recommendation**

A clear recommendation might be:

"ChatGPT appears to be the right product path if advisors should work with trusted research directly. Use ChatGPT Chat for interactive analysis and refinement, or consider ChatGPT Work if advisors should delegate the multi-step preparation of a review-ready briefing."

"If the experience must be embedded directly into the advisor portal from the start, an API-enabled path may be more appropriate."

The next step is to validate the preferred work pattern, source materials, permissions, review expectations, required tools, and whether ChatGPT is a practical first test.

## Slide 22/51 - Frame the customer problem before the solution

After comparing ChatGPT with other OpenAI solution paths, you may have a working view that ChatGPT is the right first path for the customer's problem.

But before you position ChatGPT to the customer, you need a clear problem statement.

A clear problem statement helps everyone understand the workflow pain, who is affected, why the problem matters, and what output the AI should help with.

This is important because vague problem statements lead to vague recommendations. Strong problem framing gives you the evidence needed to test ChatGPT fit and recommend the next validation step.

## Slide 23/51 - Start with the customer's specific problem

Strong ChatGPT framing starts with a specific customer problem, not a product feature.

Instead of asking: "How can we use ChatGPT here?"

Ask: "What workflow is painful enough to explore, and what would improve if that workflow changed?"

**Weak problem statement**

"The customer wants to use ChatGPT for productivity."

This is too broad. It does not explain the user group, workflow, friction, business impact, or output.

**Stronger problem statement**

"Regional account managers spend several hours preparing weekly account updates by searching across call notes, CRM exports, and internal planning documents. The process creates inconsistent updates and limits visibility into blockers before leadership review. The customer wants a faster, more consistent way to synthesize account information into review-ready summaries."

This version is stronger because it identifies the affected users, current workflow, source materials, friction, desired improvement, and output.

## Slide 24/51 - Use a problem frame to make the customer problem specific

Use the problem frame to turn a broad customer complaint into a clear, specific problem statement. At this stage, you are turning your view of a working solution path into a stronger customer-facing problem framing.

The goal is to make the customer problem specific enough to support or challenge your ChatGPT-fit view and identify what should happen next.

Use these seven prompts to clarify the problem.

## Slide 25/51 - Customer Problem Framing 1: What triggers the problem?

Start by identifying when the problem shows up. Many customer problems are easier to understand when you connect them to a specific trigger: for example, a deadline, meeting, customer request, reporting cycle, review step, escalation, or handoff.

This helps you move from a general pain point to a real moment in the customer's work.

Ask:
- When does this problem happen?
- What starts the work?
- Is it tied to a weekly, monthly, or quarterly process?
- Does it happen after a customer meeting, support escalation, policy update, renewal cycle, or leadership request?

For example:
- "Every Friday, support managers prepare a leadership update from ticket notes and escalation summaries."
- "Before each executive account review, account managers pull information from call notes, CRM exports, and account plans."
- "Whenever a new policy update is released, analysts need to interpret it and summarize the operational impact."

## Slide 26/51 - Customer Problem Framing 2: What is the specific pain?

Next, identify what is actually hard, slow, inconsistent, risky, or inefficient.

Avoid stopping at broad language such as "the process is manual" or "the team is too busy." Those may be true, but they don't explain the specific friction.

Ask:
- What takes too long?
- What is duplicated?
- What varies too much from person to person?
- Where do people get stuck?
- What is harder than it should be?
- What creates rework, delays, or risk?

For example:
- "Managers spend hours copying information from multiple systems into a weekly update."
- "Different account managers prepare review briefs in different formats, so leadership cannot compare risks consistently."
- "Analysts have to interpret long policy documents manually, which slows down response time and increases the chance that important details are missed."

The specific pain should explain why the current way of working is not good enough.

## Slide 27/51 - Customer Problem Framing 3: Who is affected?

Then clarify who experiences the problem directly and who depends on the output.

This is more specific than naming a department. "Sales," "legal," or "operations" may be too broad. Instead, try to identify the role, team, or stakeholder group connected to the problem.

Ask:
- Who performs the work today?
- Who feels the pain most directly?
- Who receives or depends on the output?
- Who cares if the output is late, inconsistent, or incomplete?

For example:
- "Customer support managers prepare the update, and the VP of Support uses it to decide where to focus improvement efforts."
- "Account managers prepare the review brief, and sales leadership uses it to identify renewal risks."

This helps show both the operator and the business stakeholder who cares about the outcome.

## Slide 28/51 - Customer Problem Framing 4: What is the current workaround?

Next, identify how the team handles the problem today.

Workarounds are useful because they show the gap between what the customer needs and what their current process can support. They also reveal where the process depends on manual effort, individual judgment, or scattered information.

Ask:
- How do teams handle this today?
- What tools, documents, or systems do they use?
- What manual steps are involved?
- Where does the workaround break down?
- What do people do when the information is missing, outdated, or unclear?

For example:
- "Managers copy notes from ticketing systems, Slack threads, and spreadsheets into a slide template."
- "Account teams search through call notes and CRM exports, then manually rewrite the key points for leadership."
- "Analysts compare policy documents side by side and write summaries from scratch."

A current workaround often shows why the problem is a good candidate for further exploration.

## Slide 29/51 - Customer Problem Framing 5: What business consequence does the problem create?

Then connect the pain to a business consequence.

This is where the problem becomes more than an inconvenience. A strong problem statement should explain what happens when the workflow stays slow, inconsistent, or hard to manage.

Ask:
- What happens when this problem is not solved?
- What decision gets delayed?
- What risk becomes harder to see?
- What customer or employee experience is affected?
- What cost, capacity, quality, or speed issue continues?
- Why does the business care?

For example:
- "Leadership gets a slower and less consistent view of customer support risks."
- "Account risks are harder to spot before executive reviews."
- "Senior legal reviewers spend time correcting inconsistent first-pass summaries instead of focusing on higher-value judgment."

The business consequence helps explain why the problem is worth exploring.

## Slide 30/51 - Customer Problem Framing 6: What decision or deliverable is at stake?

Next, identify the output, decision, approval, or handoff that the problem affects.

This keeps the problem statement practical. The goal is not just to say the work is painful. The goal is to show what the team is trying to produce or decide.

Ask:
- What needs to be created, updated, reviewed, approved, or handed off?
- What does a good output look like?
- Who uses the output next?
- What decision does the output support?

For example:
- "A weekly leadership summary that highlights support trends, escalations, and recommended actions."
- "An executive account review brief that shows account status, renewal risk, open blockers, and next steps."
- "A first-pass contract summary that highlights key terms, missing clauses, and areas for legal review."

This gives the problem a concrete shape.

## Slide 31/51 - Customer Problem Framing 7: What would "better" look like?

Finally, describe the improved state in business language.

Do not jump straight to the solution. Instead, explain what the customer wants to be different if the problem is addressed.

Ask:
- What would improve?
- What should become faster, easier, more consistent, or more visible?
- What should the team be able to do that they struggle to do today?
- How would the customer know the problem is improving?

For example:
- "Support managers could prepare leadership updates faster and with a more consistent view of themes and escalations."
- "Account teams could produce review briefs that make risks and next steps easier for leadership to compare."
- "Legal analysts could create more consistent first-pass summaries for senior review."

This gives you a clearer basis for deciding whether the opportunity is worth pursuing.

## Slide 36/51 - Reading buyer context and early validation signals

A ChatGPT opportunity may look promising, but it still needs early validation.

At this stage, you are not deciding whether the opportunity is ready for handoff. You are deciding whether it is specific enough to keep pursuing and what evidence is still missing.

This module connects ChatGPT fit to buyer context, stakeholder evidence, and early validation signals. You'll learn how to identify who cares about the opportunity, which signals are strong enough to continue, and which gaps need to be resolved next.

## Slide 37/51 - Understanding buyer context

The same ChatGPT opportunity may need to be framed differently depending on the buyer.

A strong seller does not change the facts of the opportunity, but they do connect the workflow to the buyer's priorities.

**Business leaders**

Business leaders care about value, consistency, speed, quality, and business impact.

They may ask:
- What business impact would this create?
- How does this help teams work faster or better?
- What outcome can we validate?
- Why should this matter now?

Example framing: "This workflow could help account teams prepare more consistent executive review briefs, which may improve leadership visibility into account risks and next steps."

**IT and security stakeholders**

IT and security stakeholders care about governance, access, data boundaries, feasibility, and risk.

They may ask:
- What information does the workflow depend on?
- Which users need access?
- What data boundaries matter?
- Where should human review happen?
- What needs to be validated before moving forward?

Example framing: "The immediate question is not only whether ChatGPT can support the workflow, but which approved sources, access expectations, and review steps would shape safe use."

**Operators**

Operators are the people doing the work day to day. They care about whether the workflow becomes easier, faster, or more useful.

They may ask:
- Will this reduce manual effort?
- Will the output be useful enough to review and use?
- Will it fit the way we already work?
- Will it help with the repeated tasks that slow us down?

Example framing: "The goal is to reduce the time account managers spend stitching together information before reviews, while helping them produce a more consistent starting point."

## Slide 38/51 - Map who is involved in the opportunity

You've just looked at buyer context: how different buyers may care about the same ChatGPT workflow for different reasons.

Now shift from how to frame the message to who must be visible in the opportunity.

At this stage, you do not need every person fully aligned. But you do need enough clarity to understand who feels the workflow pain, who cares about the outcome, who may need to review the path forward, and who can help the opportunity keep moving.

This helps you avoid a common early-stage problem: a customer sounds interested, but the opportunity stalls because the right people were never identified.

**1. Who feels the pain?**

Start with the people closest to the workflow.

These are the users or teams who deal with the problem in their day-to-day work. They can explain what happens today, where the work slows down, what is inconsistent, and what a useful output would look like.

Examples: Sales representatives preparing customer follow-ups. Legal analysts preparing first-pass contract summaries. HR business partners answering repeated manager questions. Support managers reviewing ticket themes before leadership updates.

Ask:
- Who performs this work today?
- What part of the work is hardest for them?
- What do they need to produce, decide, or hand off?
- What would make the output useful enough for them to review and use?

**2. Who owns the outcome?**

Next, identify the person or team accountable for the business result.

This is not always the person doing the work. Instead, their concern is the business result. They care whether the workflow improves in a way the organization can recognize and value.

Examples: Head of Sales Operations, Legal Operations Lead, Customer Support Director, Finance Operations Lead, HR Operations Lead.

Ask:
- Who is accountable for the result this workflow supports?
- What metric, risk, delay, or operating issue matters to them?
- What would make this problem worth improving now?
- What would they need to see to believe the opportunity is worth pursuing?

**3. Who could shape or slow the path forward?**

Then identify the teams that may need to review the opportunity before it can progress.

These people may not own the business outcome, but they can influence whether the opportunity moves forward responsibly. They help clarify what information can be used. They also help determine who should have access. If review or approval is required, they help define that path.

Examples: IT, Security, Legal, Procurement, Compliance, Data or platform teams.

Ask:
- Who needs to review data access, source materials, or governance expectations?
- Which policies, permissions, or approval steps may matter?
- Who could slow progression if they are not engaged early?
- What questions should be surfaced now so they do not become blockers later?

**Who can create priority?**

Finally, identify whether there is someone senior enough to create urgency and help align the right people.

This person may not be deeply involved in the workflow details, but they can help make the opportunity a priority if it connects to a meaningful business goal.

Ask:
- Who cares enough about this problem to prioritize it?
- Is the problem tied to a business goal that leadership already cares about?
- Who can help bring the right stakeholders into the next conversation?
- Who would support a focused validation step if the opportunity looks promising?

**What this gives you**

By the end of this step, you should be able to describe the opportunity in terms of people, not just the workflow. For example:

"Customer support managers feel the pain because they prepare weekly leadership updates manually. The Customer Support Director owns the outcome because the update informs escalation priorities and improvement actions. IT and security may need to review source access and permissions. The VP of Customer Experience could create priority if the workflow connects to faster issue resolution or improved customer satisfaction."

That level of clarity is enough for early framing. You are not trying to complete stakeholder alignment yet. You are identifying the people you need to understand first, the people you need to involve next, and the open questions you need to validate before the opportunity can move forward.

## Slide 39/51 - Identifying early validation signals

At this stage, you are deciding whether the opportunity is specific enough to pursue and what evidence is still missing.

Early validation signals help you avoid relying on enthusiasm alone.

Look for:
- Clear business problem.
- Identified stakeholders.
- Initial ChatGPT use case framed in business-output language.
- Customer engagement signal.
- Early value hypothesis.
- Required business context or source materials understood at a high level.
- Governance, permission, or review needs identified.
- A likely success signal or value measure, named.

Then classify each signal as one of the following:
- Enough to pursue: The signal is clear enough to justify continued exploration.
- Needs validation: The signal is directionally useful, but more evidence is needed.
- Missing: The signal is not yet evidenced.

**Example classification**

"The customer wants ChatGPT to support customer success managers before renewal meetings. They mentioned that managers spend a lot of time preparing, but they have not confirmed which source materials are approved or who owns the renewal-readiness outcome."

Possible classification:
- Clear business problem: Needs validation.
- Identified stakeholders: Needs validation.
- Initial ChatGPT use case framed in business-output language: Enough to pursue.
- Customer engagement signal: Missing unless a next step has been agreed.
- Early value hypothesis: Needs validation.
- Required business context or source materials: Needs validation.
- Governance, permission, or review needs: Missing.
- Likely success signal or value measure: Missing.

This opportunity may be promising, but the next step should focus on validation, not handoff.

## Slide 43/51 - Using the ChatGPT fit checks

This module brings everything you have just learned together. You have learned how to recognize ChatGPT-fit patterns, compare solution paths, frame the customer problem, and read buyer context and early validation signals.

Now you'll use those pieces to create a short ChatGPT fit-check judgment.

A fit-check judgment is not a formal handoff. It is a seller-level statement that explains whether the opportunity appears worth pursuing, why ChatGPT may be the right first path, and what happens next.

## Slide 44/51 - Introducing the ChatGPT fit checks

Fit checks are simple questions you use before positioning ChatGPT.

They help you confirm three things (all three checked ✓):
1. The opportunity is grounded in a real workflow problem.
2. ChatGPT is likely the right product path, and you have a reasoned view of whether the workflow should be collaborative in Chat or delegated in Work.
3. You know what must be validated next before deeper progression.

Fit checks also help prevent two common mistakes:
- Positioning ChatGPT too early because the customer used a broad AI label.
- Over-engineering a solution before you understand the workflow, users, output, and validation path.

A strong fit-check judgment is short, specific, and evidence-based.

It should not sound like: "This is a great ChatGPT opportunity because the customer wants productivity."

It should sound more like: "This appears worth pursuing as a ChatGPT opportunity because account managers need to synthesize approved account information into review-ready summaries. ChatGPT is likely the right product path; ChatGPT Work may be the better starting experience if the team wants to delegate the multi-step preparation, while ChatGPT Chat may fit if users want to refine the summary interactively. The next validation gap is confirming approved sources, review expectations, and the business owner for account-review quality."

## Slide 45/51 - The 3 ChatGPT fit checks

Apply three checks before making your recommendation.

**1. Workflow and output fit**

Start by checking whether the opportunity is grounded in a real workflow and a clear business output.

This is the foundation of the fit judgment. A customer may say they want "AI," "ChatGPT," a "copilot," or a "chatbot," but those labels are not enough. You need to know what work is happening, who is doing it, and what the AI would help produce, improve, retrieve, review, or synthesize.

This check helps you avoid recommending ChatGPT for a vague productivity request.

Ask: Is there a clear knowledge-work problem, and can we name the business output ChatGPT should help with?

Look for:
- A clear user group.
- A repeated workflow.
- A business output.
- A knowledge, reasoning, synthesis, drafting, analysis, review, or preparation need.
- A clear reason the workflow matters.

For example: "Customer success managers need to prepare renewal briefing notes from call notes, support tickets, and renewal history."

This is stronger than saying: "The customer wants ChatGPT for customer success."

The stronger version names the users, the workflow, the source context, and the output. That gives you something specific to test.

**2. Solution-path fit**

Next, check whether ChatGPT is the right product path and which experience best matches the work. Separate the primary surface from the capabilities and operating model so that agentic, connected, or multi-step requirements do not get treated as separate product routes.

Ask: Should users collaborate in ChatGPT Chat, delegate knowledge work in ChatGPT Work, use Codex for engineering, or rely on an API-enabled or combined experience?

Look for:
- Business users who need to work with AI directly in a governed environment.
- A clear collaborative or delegated work pattern and a defined output or deliverable.
- A validation path that can start in ChatGPT Chat or ChatGPT Work.
- No immediate requirement to embed the experience into a product, application, or operational process before value can be tested.
- No primary requirement for software engineering that would make Codex the better surface.
- Required applications, tools, actions, schedules, approvals, permissions, and review boundaries are understood well enough to validate the route.

For example: "ChatGPT appears to be the right product path because business users need to work with approved source material directly. ChatGPT Work may be the better starting experience if the team wants to delegate preparation of review-ready materials; ChatGPT Chat may fit if users want to explore and refine the work interactively. An API-enabled path is not required at this stage because the experience does not need to be embedded in another system."

This is stronger than saying: "ChatGPT is the right path because the customer asked for it."

The stronger version explains the primary surface, work pattern, and why the required capabilities and operating model support ChatGPT first.

**3. Validation fit**

Finally, check whether the next validation step is clear.

A promising ChatGPT opportunity does not need to be fully qualified or ready for handoff yet. But it should have a clear next step. You should be able to name what still needs to be confirmed before the opportunity moves deeper into solution development, positioning, or handoff.

This check also helps you avoid treating early interest as readiness.

Ask: Do we know enough to keep pursuing this opportunity, and what is the most important validation gap to resolve next?

Look for:
- A clear business problem.
- Identified people connected to the workflow.
- An initial ChatGPT use case framed in business-output language.
- A customer engagement signal or agreed next step.
- An early value hypothesis.
- Required business context or source materials understood at a high level.
- Governance, permission, or review needs identified.
- A likely success signal or value measure.

For example: "The opportunity appears worth pursuing, but the next step is to validate source access, review expectations, and the outcome owner's success criteria."

This is stronger than saying: "The opportunity looks good, so we should move forward."

The stronger version explains what still needs to be confirmed before the recommendation becomes more confident.

**Fit-check judgment format**

To create a fit-check judgment, use this structure:

"[Opportunity] appears / does not yet appear worth pursuing as a ChatGPT opportunity because [workflow and output fit]. [ChatGPT Chat / ChatGPT Work / another path] appears / does not appear to be the right first experience because [primary surface, capability, and operating-model fit]. The next validation gap is [validation fit]."

Example fit-check judgment: "This appears worth pursuing as a ChatGPT opportunity because customer success managers need to synthesize call notes, support tickets, and renewal history into renewal briefing notes. ChatGPT Work may be the right starting experience if the team wants to delegate the multi-step preparation of a review-ready brief using approved sources and human review; ChatGPT Chat may fit if managers want to build and refine the brief interactively. The next validation gap is confirming the preferred work pattern, approved source materials, review expectations, and the success criteria owned by the customer success leader."
