## Slide 5/32 - What vertical solution application and vertical context mean

Vertical solution application is the process of adapting your recommendation to the customer's real operating environment.

Many workflow patterns appear across multiple industries. Teams review documents, prepare summaries, answer questions, investigate issues, make decisions, and coordinate work.

However, the conditions surrounding those workflows can vary significantly.

The same workflow may involve different stakeholders, different governance requirements, different data considerations, and different success measures depending on the industry and organization.

Do not worry about becoming an industry expert. You just need to understand enough context to make a credible recommendation and identify what still needs to be validated.

Vertical context is the set of industry-specific realities that shape an AI opportunity.

It includes the customer's workflows, stakeholders, risks, governance expectations, and success metrics.

These factors influence what you should ask during discovery, how you judge readiness, what value story will matter, and what solution direction may be credible.

A simple way to read vertical context is to look for five evidence areas:

| Evidence area | What it means | Why you should care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workflow | The process, handoffs, systems, and outputs the customer relies on | Keeps the recommendation grounded in real work |
| Stakeholders | The people who sponsor, use, approve, govern, or operate the solution | Shows who cares, who decides, and who must validate |
| Risk | Business, operational, legal, regulatory, data, or reputational concerns | Helps identify what cannot be assumed |
| Governance | Approval steps, oversight, permissions, auditability, and operating controls | Helps determine what requires validation before handoff |
| Success metrics | The outcomes the customer will use to judge whether the work improved | Connects the opportunity to business value |

This context matters because industry labels often hide important differences.

"AI for document review" may sound like a single use case, but the details can vary significantly.

You should understand:

- what documents are being reviewed
- who performs the review
- what decisions the documents support
- what data and systems are involved
- what controls or governance requirements apply
- what outcome the customer is trying to improve

The aim is to ask questions that uncover the context behind the request and capture the evidence that could influence the recommendation, validation approach, or next step.

## Slide 6/32 - Your role in the conversation, and what sellers should listen for and document

As a seller, your role is to connect workflow evidence with customer context.

Strong vertical solution application helps you move beyond broad statements such as "AI for healthcare" or "AI for finance" and understand what is actually happening inside the customer's environment.

Effective conversations help answer questions such as:

- What workflow is the customer trying to improve?
- Who is involved in the process?
- What data, systems, or information are required?
- What governance, review, or approval requirements may apply?
- What business outcome is the customer trying to improve?
- What still needs validation before a recommendation can be confirmed?

This course is about building that contextual judgment before the conversation moves into deeper solution design, technical validation, deployment planning, or implementation. Now let's focus on what you, as a seller, should be listening for and documenting.

When you are working in a vertical customer context, listen for the specific evidence that makes the opportunity clearer.

**Start with the work object**

This is the thing moving through the workflow. It might be a claim, case, contract, ticket, loan file, patient-admin request, research report, code issue, or customer conversation.

**Then listen for the workflow friction**

Where do people search, summarize, classify, draft, review, approve, route, monitor, or coordinate handoffs?

From there, document the vertical-specific details that could affect qualification and routing:

**Data boundary**

Does the workflow involve regulated, sensitive, confidential, customer, patient, financial, research, source-code, or operational data?

**Stakeholder and owner**

Who owns the business outcome? Who uses the workflow? Who approves risk? Who owns the system? Who operates the solution after launch?

**Review and audit needs**

Do outputs require human review, source traceability, audit trails, evidence packages, policy checks, governance reporting, or approval records?

**Success metric**

Is the customer trying to improve turnaround time, quality, consistency, throughput, risk reduction, customer experience, revenue impact, cost reduction, or adoption?

These details help you avoid an unsupported recommendation.

For example:

"The customer wants AI to summarize records" is not enough.

A stronger seller note would say:

"The customer's administrative intake team reviews incoming requests, summarizes relevant information, and routes work to the right operational owner. The workflow may involve sensitive data, so the customer must confirm approved data boundaries, review points, system ownership, and the success metric before moving to handoff."

That is a more credible vertical read because it names the workflow, the work object, the risk area, and the validation need.

## Slide 7/32 - Real-world example

The same workflow pattern can appear in very different industries.

Imagine three customers. One works in financial services, one in healthcare, and one in life sciences.

All three describe a similar challenge: employees spend significant time reviewing information from multiple sources, creating summaries, and preparing materials that help others make decisions.

At first glance, these opportunities look identical. A sales person might describe all three as "AI for document review."

However, once you look more closely, important differences begin to emerge.

The shared workflow pattern is:

Review, synthesize, and prepare decision-ready information from multiple sources.

The workflow might be similar, but the operating environment is not. Each customer has different stakeholders, risks, governance requirements, and success criteria. Those differences can change what needs to be validated and how the opportunity should progress.

| Vertical context | What the workflow may involve | What changes in the recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financial services risk review | Analysts prepare risk-review packets from customer documents, internal policies, prior notes, market context, and committee requirements. | You should document sensitive financial data boundaries, auditability needs, policy consistency, systems of record, committee review steps, risk-owner approval, and success metrics. |
| Healthcare administrative intake | Administrative teams review requests, summarize information, prepare documentation, and route work to the appropriate operational owner. | You should confirm approved data boundaries, whether sensitive health information is involved, whether the workflow is administrative, operational, or clinical, where human review is required, and who approves use. |
| Life sciences knowledge synthesis | Research, medical, or regulatory teams synthesize scientific, regulatory, research, or internal documentation into briefing materials or review-ready outputs. | You should document evidence standards, source traceability, review cycles, domain-owner validation, data sensitivity, controlled access, and any explicit deployment or infrastructure requirements. Treat open-weight or self-managed options as validation items, not default conclusions. |

Notice what changes across these examples:

**Financial services**

The conversation might focus on auditability, policy consistency, systems of record, and risk-owner approval.

**Healthcare**

The discussion might focus on data boundaries, privacy expectations, human review requirements, and whether the workflow is administrative, operational, or clinical.

**Life sciences**

The emphasis might be on evidence quality, source traceability, scientific review, potential regulatory requirements to validate with qualified specialists, and domain-owner validation.

The workflow pattern is similar, but the evidence needed to support the recommendation is different.

What you should notice:

This example demonstrates a core principle of vertical solution application: workflow patterns often repeat across industries, but the surrounding context changes how opportunities should be evaluated.

You should identify:

- The shared workflow pattern — Reviewing, synthesizing, and preparing information for decision-making.
- The industry-specific context — Data, governance, risk, compliance, review processes, and stakeholder expectations.
- The validation requirements — What evidence is needed before the opportunity can progress.
- The decision owners — Who must review, approve, or adopt the solution.
- The factors that could change the recommendation — Such as auditability, privacy requirements, evidence standards, or deployment constraints.

The lesson is simple: start with the workflow, but do not stop there. Strong recommendations come from understanding how the customer's operating environment shapes what is possible, what must be validated, and what success looks like.

## Slide 10/32 - Introduction (Module: Adapt solution patterns to a vertical workflow)

After you understand the vertical context, look at what the workflow needs to produce.

In this module, you'll connect the workflow output to a solution pattern: the repeatable way AI could help the work produce a better result. Then you'll adapt that pattern using the customer's data, stakeholders, review needs, value outcome, and validation requirements.

## Slide 11/32 - Start with the workflow output

A solution pattern is a repeatable way AI helps a workflow produce an outcome. The pattern answers one question:

What job does AI need to do in this workflow?

That question matters because customers often describe opportunities with vague labels:

- "We need AI for finance"
- "We want AI for healthcare"
- "We need a chatbot"
- "We want a copilot"
- "We need automation"

Those labels can be useful starting points, but they do not explain the work AI is being asked to support.

Instead, focus on the workflow output. Is the AI helping someone retrieve knowledge, extract fields, draft content, classify work, review an artifact, recommend an action, coordinate steps, or support software delivery?

Common seller-level patterns include:

- Retrieve and synthesize knowledge
- Extract and structure information
- Draft, summarize, or transform content
- Classify, route, or prioritize work
- Review, evaluate, or check outputs
- Recommend next actions
- Coordinate multi-step work across tools and approvals
- Support software delivery work across planning, building, testing, review, documentation, and maintenance

This is not a technical taxonomy or a product list. It is a practical way to keep the conversation focused on business output.

A customer might say, "We want an AI assistant for intake." A better question you might ask is, "What output should the workflow produce at the intake step?"

The answer might be a structured summary, a priority label, a routed request, a drafted response, or an exception flag. Each output leads to a different recommendation and a different validation need.

## Slide 12/32 - Adapt the pattern with vertical evidence

Once the workflow output is clear, adapt the pattern to the customer's vertical context.

Ask:

- What output does the workflow need to produce?
- Which data, systems, or knowledge sources are needed?
- Who reviews or approves the output?
- What could go wrong if the output is incomplete, inaccurate, or unauditable?
- What value would improve if the workflow worked better?
- Which user or product surface appears most plausible based on the evidence?
- Which capabilities and deployment or operating requirements need validation before the recommendation can move forward?
- What next step is supported by the evidence?

The same workflow pattern can point to different choices across three independent axes:

**User or product surface**

ChatGPT Chat for collaborative thinking and creation; ChatGPT Work for delegated knowledge work and finished deliverables; Codex for software engineering; or an application or process built with the API when intelligence must be embedded.

**Required capability or architecture**

Retrieval, structured outputs, apps and tools, code execution, automation, or approval-gated multi-step execution.

**Deployment and operations**

Where and how the workflow will run and be controlled, including identity, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, human review, and operating ownership.

**How the axes combine**

The axes can combine—agentic or multi-step execution is a capability, not a peer product route.

**Open-weight or self-managed options**

Treat these as deployment and operations choices only when explicit requirements remain after current hosted controls are evaluated. Do not infer the route from data sensitivity or residency alone.

Your role is not to design the implementation. Your role is to document enough evidence to make a credible seller-level recommendation.

The customer says:

The likely workflow steps are:

2. Review submitted information.
3. Summarize the need.
4. Identify the right owner.
5. Route the work.
6. Prepare a response or next action.

The vertical-specific considerations matter immediately. The workflow may involve sensitive health information, approved data boundaries, privacy policy, human review, and clear operational ownership. ## Slide 17/32 - Value changes by stakeholder

Vertical value is not only about what the AI can do. It is about what different stakeholders need the workflow to improve.

The same use case matters for different reasons depending on who is evaluating it.

| Stakeholder group | What they may care about | Seller implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive sponsors | Growth, efficiency, risk reduction, speed, differentiation, or strategic transformation | Connect the workflow to business priorities, not only task-level benefits. |
| Business owners | Throughput, backlog reduction, quality, decision speed, customer experience, or operational resilience | Show how the use case improves a workflow they own. |
| Risk, legal, compliance, or governance stakeholders | Auditability, approved use, human review, access control, policy adherence, and output traceability | Document what must be reviewed, approved, governed, or validated. |
| Technical stakeholders | Integration, systems of record, data access, security review, operational ownership, and validation requirements | Identify where technical or deployment handoff may be needed. |
| End users | Usability, workflow fit, time saved, reduced rework, and confidence in AI-supported outputs | Explain how the solution direction supports real work, not just executive ambition. |

A weak value statement is generic:

"This could save time."

A stronger vertical value statement connects stakeholder priority to workflow reality:

"For the risk operations owner, faster preparation of risk-review packets could reduce review backlog and improve consistency. For governance stakeholders, the opportunity depends on source traceability, auditability, review ownership, and approval steps."

The stronger version does not overstate ROI. It identifies which value matters to whom and what must be validated.

## Slide 18/32 - Make trust and readiness requirements specific

Trust and readiness are often discussed too broadly. In vertical selling, that can create problems. Instead of saying, "There are governance considerations," be specific about what you have heard and what still needs validation.

You should document:

- Whether regulated, sensitive, confidential, customer, patient, financial, research, or source-code data is involved.
- Whether outputs need human review before action.
- Whether the customer needs source traceability, audit trails, reporting, or governance visibility.
- Which systems of record, knowledge sources, tools, applications, or repositories are involved.
- Which permissions, approval steps, and operating owners are required.
- What success metric will prove value.

Use the three routing axes to keep this practical:

**User or product surface**

Where will the person or workflow primarily operate—ChatGPT, Codex, or an application or process built with the API?

**Required capability or architecture**

What must the system do—such as use apps or tools, retrieve information, produce structured outputs, execute code, or complete approval-gated multi-step work?

**Deployment and operations**

Where and how will the workflow run and be controlled, including identity, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, human review, and operating ownership?

These axes help you capture requirements without pretending to design the architecture.

You should not decide:

- Legal or regulatory interpretation.
- Security architecture or compliance approval.
- API design, SDK usage, model evaluation, deployment configuration, or production architecture.
- Open-weight model infrastructure, self-managed operations, data-residency implementation, or air-gapped deployment design.
- Migration planning details or production rollout mechanics.

You should request validation before advancing when:

- The data boundary is unclear.
- The customer requires technical architecture decisions.
- The customer has a covered high-stakes, regulated, or highly sensitive workflow.
- The customer is replacing an existing AI solution or vendor-dependent workflow.
- The recommendation depends on open-weight models, migration, advanced agentic capabilities, or a combination of surfaces, capabilities, and operating models.

This boundary is important. Strong sellers do not avoid complexity. They document it clearly and bring in the right validation.

Policy boundary: For covered high-stakes decisions in sensitive areas, current OpenAI Usage Policies require human review. Tailored legal or medical advice that requires a license also requires appropriate licensed-professional involvement. Treat policy, legal, regulatory, and compliance questions as validation items for qualified specialists, not seller conclusions. See OpenAI Usage Policies.

## Slide 22/32 - Introduction (Module: Recommend and pitch the vertical recommendation)

A strong vertical recommendation should help the customer and partner team decide what happens next.

In this module, you'll practice turning vertical evidence into a concise seller-level recommendation. The goal is to name the likely direction, identify what still needs validation, and recommend the next step without presenting the recommendation as a final architecture or implementation plan.

## Slide 23/32 - Use three seller decisions in order

A strong vertical recommendation separates three decisions.

**1. Form the routing read across three axes** ✓ (Click to complete)
Name the likely user or product surface, the capabilities the workflow needs, and the deployment or operating requirements that must be validated. These are related decisions, but they are not interchangeable.

**2. Identify what needs validation** ✓ (Click to complete)
Name the requirement, constraint, or uncertainty that could change or extend the recommendation. This could include data boundaries, governance needs, multi-step workflow requirements, migration signals, technical feasibility, or explicit open-weight or self-managed infrastructure requirements.

**3. Choose the recommended next step** ✓ (Click to complete)
Decide what should happen next. The next step could be further qualification, validation before handoff, technical or deployment handoff, or migration discovery.

This order matters because sellers often jump from customer need to solution pitch too quickly.

A strong recommendation does not sound like this:

"You need ChatGPT"

Or

"This should be built with the API"

A stronger recommendation sounds like this:

"Based on the workflow, the likely surface is ChatGPT Work for delegated analyst preparation. The required capabilities include source retrieval, synthesis, and review-ready output. Before confirming the route, we need to validate data classes, systems of record, permissions, audit expectations, and review ownership. The next step should be validation before handoff with the risk owner and technical stakeholder."

That is a seller-level recommendation. It is specific, evidence-based, and careful about what still needs validation.

## Slide 24/32 - Seller-level recommendation signals

Use these signals to keep the three routing axes separate.

**User or product surface**
ChatGPT Chat is strongest when people need to think, explore, draft, critique, and refine collaboratively. ChatGPT Work is relevant when teams can delegate complex knowledge work by providing an outcome, context, boundaries, and a review standard. Codex is the dedicated surface for software engineering. An application or process built with the API is the route when intelligence must be embedded into a product, system, or operational workflow.

**Required capability or architecture**
Identify what the workflow must do, such as retrieve information, produce structured outputs, use apps or tools, execute code, automate steps, or complete approval-gated multi-step work. Agentic behavior belongs here—it is a capability that can be delivered through an appropriate surface, not a peer product route.

**Deployment and operations**
Identify where and how the workflow will run and be controlled, including identity, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, human review, and operating ownership. Confirm current plan, workspace, app, and permission availability before treating a capability as available.

**Open-weight or self-managed requirements**
Treat these as deployment and operations constraints only when the customer has explicit infrastructure or control requirements. Evaluate current hosted controls before concluding that an open-weight or self-managed option is required.

**Combination of surfaces and capabilities**
A customer may need more than one surface or capability—for example, ChatGPT for employee work and an API-based process for embedded execution. State each axis separately instead of labeling the whole solution "hybrid."

This separation helps you avoid false precision while preserving a clear seller-level recommendation and validation path.

Some requirements could change or extend the recommendation.

**Self-managed or open-model requirements**
May matter when the customer has explicit requirements for runtime control, self-managed infrastructure, private deployment, data residency, on-premises operation, or controlled environments. These requirements should not become a seller-level technical design. They should become a validation need.

**Governance requirements**
May matter when the workflow involves sensitive data, human review, auditability, permissions, approval steps, monitoring, or policy adherence. Confirm who owns review, approval, operating controls, and ongoing oversight.

**Technical feasibility**
May need validation when the recommendation depends on systems integration, data access, structured outputs, repositories, tool access, or workflow automation across systems.

**Multi-step workflow requirements**
May matter when the customer expects work to move across tools, context, approvals, and deliverables rather than stop at content generation, synthesis, or analysis.

These signals help you avoid false precision. You can recommend a likely direction, but you should also be clear about what must be validated before anyone treats that direction as ready for execution.

## Slide 25/32 - Choose the right next step

The best next step depends on both opportunity maturity and customer readiness.

Use this next-step logic:

**1. Further qualification** ✓ (Click to complete)
is the right next step when you cannot yet explain the workflow, owner, data boundary, value outcome, readiness, or routing read with confidence.

**2. Validation before handoff** ✓ (Click to complete)
is the right next step when the routing read is credible, but technical, security, legal, compliance, migration, open-weight, self-managed, or other deployment and operations questions must be confirmed.

**3. Technical or deployment handoff** ✓ (Click to complete)
is the right next step when the workflow, owner, value, data boundary, readiness evidence, and routing read are clear enough to move forward.

**4. Migration discovery** ✓ (Click to complete)
might be needed when the customer is replacing an existing AI solution, model path, automation, coding assistant, vendor-specific implementation, or workflow tool.

Do not treat handoff as a way to escape ambiguity. Handoff should happen when you can clearly explain what has been validated, what still needs confirmation, and which customer or partner owner should be involved next.

- If the evidence is weak, strengthen the evidence first.
- If the evidence is strong but constraints are unresolved, validate the constraints.
- If the evidence, value, routing, and readiness are clear, prepare the handoff.

Required capabilities may include retrieval, source traceability, structured outputs, recurring monitoring, and review checkpoints. Before moving forward, validate source access, review expectations, evidence standards, data sensitivity, permissions, human review, success metrics, and any explicit infrastructure requirements."

The pitch should avoid unsupported claims. You can recommend a routing read based on workflow and evidence. Do not claim that a policy, legal, regulatory, technical, migration, open-weight, or deployment requirement is solved until the appropriate qualified owner validates it.

A strong vertical recommendation separates the routing read into three axes: the likely user or product surface, the required capability or architecture, and the deployment or operating requirements that need validation. It then names the next step.
