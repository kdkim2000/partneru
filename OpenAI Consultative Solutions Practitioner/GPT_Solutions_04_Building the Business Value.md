## Slide 2/46 - Introduction

As a seller, you will often hear a customer describe an AI idea before they can clearly explain the business value behind it.

Your role is to help turn that idea into an opportunity the customer can evaluate, sponsor, and progress.

This course helps you build that value story. You'll learn how to connect an AI-enabled workflow improvement to a clear business outcome, supporting evidence, and a practical next step.

The goal is not to build a final ROI model. It is to create a credible value story that helps the customer decide what is worth validating next.

- Explain where business value fits in OpenAI opportunity conversations.
- Connect workflow problems to business outcomes and evidence needs.
- Build evidence-based value hypotheses.
- Identify measurable indicators and directional benefit estimates.
- Develop customer-specific value narratives.

## Slide 6/46 - From use case to value story

A use case describes what AI might help the customer do. A value story explains why that use case is worth pursuing.

For example, a customer may say:

"We want AI to help with support knowledge."

That is a useful use case direction, but it does not yet explain value.

You need to help clarify:

- What is difficult in the support workflow today?
- What would improve if agents could retrieve and synthesize answers more easily?
- Which business outcome would that support?
- How would the customer know the improvement is working?
- What should the partner and customer validate next?

This is how business value moves the conversation from product interest to opportunity development.

Without business value, the conversation can stay at the level of capabilities:

- "Can it summarize content?"
- "Can it answer questions?"
- "Can it use our knowledge base?"
- "Can it save employees time?"

Those questions matter, but they are not enough to progress a sales opportunity.

A stronger conversation connects the capability to a workflow, the workflow to a business outcome, and the business outcome to evidence.

That is what you will practice throughout this course.

## Slide 8/46 - Keep portfolio awareness light

OpenAI technologies should be understood as building blocks for customer outcomes.

In this course, you do not need to compare every product path or design the solution. That comes later. For this course, your job is to describe the value logic clearly enough that the opportunity can move toward validation.

Use portfolio language only when it helps clarify how value may be realized.

For example:

**ChatGPT Chat** — Can help employees think, research, draft, analyze, and refine work collaboratively.

**ChatGPT Work** — Can help people delegate longer, multi-step knowledge work, use approved Apps and files as context, and return finished or review-ready outputs while people steer the work and approve important actions.

**The API** — Can help companies embed intelligence into products, systems, workflows, applications, and customer experiences.

**Codex** — Can help engineering teams delegate bounded software work such as planning, implementation, testing, review, debugging, and documentation.

**Agentic capabilities** — Can support multi-step work across tools and approval points, but the product experience, permissions, and operating model still need validation.

**Open-weight models, migration, and hybrid paths** — May become relevant when validated requirements point to self-managed infrastructure, customization, migration, or combined solution needs.

Use these descriptions lightly. They help you connect the value story to OpenAI's portfolio, but they do not replace deeper solution mapping.

A useful seller phrase is:

"The solution path still needs validation, but the value hypothesis is that this workflow improvement could help the customer achieve [specific outcome]."

That keeps the value conversation credible without turning it into solution routing too early.

## Slide 11/46 - Introduction (Module: Connecting workflow change to business outcomes)

Once the workflow is clear, the next seller decision is focus.

## Slide 12/46 - Choose the outcome that matters most

The next seller move is choosing the business outcome the workflow improvement is most likely to support. This is where sellers often need to be selective.

One AI-enabled workflow improvement can support several outcomes. For example, faster support answer retrieval could improve response time, reduce escalations, increase agent capacity, improve answer consistency, and support customer experience.

All of those may be true, but they should not all be presented with equal weight.

Your job is to identify the outcome that matters most to the customer right now.

Start by asking:

- What priority did the customer actually mention?
- Which stakeholder cares about this problem?
- What metric or business pressure is affected?
- What would make the customer decide this is worth validating?
- What evidence would support that outcome?

This keeps the value conversation focused.

A weak value story tries to claim every possible benefit.

A stronger value story leads with the outcome that best matches the customer's priority and evidence.

## Slide 13/46 - Start with the work, not the technology

Strong value conversations begin with the customer's workflow reality.

A label such as "AI assistant," "chatbot," "agent," "automation," or "document AI" does not explain value by itself. Those labels may describe a preferred interface or solution direction, but they do not tell you what business result the customer needs.

Before you talk about value, clarify the work:

- Who performs the work today?
- What output does the workflow produce?
- Where is the workflow slow, costly, inconsistent, risky, or difficult to scale?
- What happens when the workflow does not perform well?
- Which business outcome is affected?

For example, a customer request for "AI for contract review" could point to several different value stories:

- Faster review cycle time
- Lower outside counsel spend
- Better clause consistency
- Reduced commercial risk
- More self-service for business users
- More capacity for legal teams to focus on complex work

The same technology label can hide very different business priorities.

As a seller, your role is to uncover the business reason behind the workflow request. That allows you to build a value story the customer can validate instead of relying on generic AI benefit language.

## Slide 14/46 - Use outcome areas as prompts, not a checklist

The outcome areas below are not a separate framework to present to the customer. Think of them as a short menu of options that helps you choose the strongest value angle.

Use them when you need to ask, "What kind of business result could this workflow change support?"

| Outcome area | What it means in a workflow conversation | Example evidence to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grow revenue or increase revenue capacity | Teams can pursue, serve, or convert more valuable work | Conversion rate, qualified volume, response rate, pipeline progression, seller capacity |
| Reduce cost or avoid unnecessary spend | The workflow uses fewer paid resources or avoids external spend | Cost per case, outside spend, rework cost, manual hours tied to cost |
| Increase productive capacity | People spend less effort on repetitive work and more effort on higher-value work | Review hours, manual touches, cases handled, queue size |
| Speed up work or shorten cycle time | Work moves through the process faster | Cycle time, turnaround time, wait time, backlog age |
| Improve quality and consistency | Outputs are more complete, accurate, consistent, or policy-aligned | Error rate, rework rate, consistency score, quality review findings |
| Reduce risk or improve control | The organization identifies, prevents, or escalates issues more consistently | Audit findings, escalation rate, policy exceptions, risk flags |
| Improve customer experience | Customers get faster, clearer, more consistent, or more personalized service | Response time, resolution rate, satisfaction, complaint rate |
| Improve employee experience | Employees spend less time on frustrating or low-value work | Workload feedback, repetitive-task time, satisfaction, burnout indicators |

The key is to choose the outcome that best fits the customer's current priority.

If the customer is worried about backlog, lead with throughput, cycle time, and capacity.

If the customer is under audit pressure, lead with risk, consistency, escalation quality, and evidence trails.

If the customer is focused on cost discipline, lead with cost per unit of work, avoided spend, or capacity assumptions that still need validation.

If the customer is trying to improve customer experience, lead with response time, resolution rate, consistency, and escalation reduction.

This helps you avoid generic value claims and build a value story that a stakeholder can recognize.

## Slide 16/46 - Real-world example

A financial services firm has a slow internal risk-review workflow. Analysts spend hours reviewing documents and drafting summary memos.

A weak value conversation would start with a broad statement like:

"AI can help with document review and improve productivity."

That statement is too general. It does not explain which workflow is affected, what outcome matters, or what evidence is needed.

A stronger value conversation starts with the workflow

Risk analysts:

- Review case documents
- Identify relevant issues
- Draft summary memos
- Escalate cases that need additional review

The workflow is creating backlog, and leaders are concerned about review consistency and audit readiness.

Several business outcomes may be plausible:

**Speed** — Shorter case review cycles

**Capacity** — More analyst time for higher-value review

**Quality** — More consistent summaries and decision support

**Risk** — Lower backlog and stronger audit readiness

You should not treat all outcomes as equally important. The next question is: which priority matters most to the customer right now?

If audit pressure is the executive priority, the value story should lead with risk, consistency, evidence quality, and review controls.

If operational backlog is the priority, the value story should lead with cycle time, throughput, and analyst capacity.

If the customer is preparing for a pilot, the next step is to gather evidence such as:

- Monthly case volume
- Current review effort per case
- Backlog size
- Review cycle time
- Quality review findings
- Current escalation rate
- Human oversight requirements

The lesson is simple: a workflow-specific problem creates a clearer value conversation than a generic "document AI" pitch.

## Slide 19/46 - Introduction (Module: Building an evidence-based value hypothesis)

A value hypothesis turns a promising outcome into something the customer can test.

It gives you evidence-based language for what may improve, why it matters, and what must be validated before stronger claims are made.

## Slide 20/46 - Turn the outcome into a value hypothesis

A value hypothesis turns the selected workflow outcome into a testable statement.

It answers:

"Based on what we know, how could this workflow improvement create business value, and what should we validate next?"

A value hypothesis is not a final claim.

It is not a guarantee.

It is not a full ROI model.

It is a seller tool that helps the customer and partner align on what may be true if the opportunity is worth developing further.

This is a simple value hypothesis builder showing the sentence structure:

Because [current workflow problem] affects [stakeholder or business outcome], improving [workflow step or output] with AI could help [expected measurable improvement], which matters because [customer priority]

Example:

"Because risk analysts spend several hours per case reviewing documents and drafting memos, AI-assisted summarization and memo drafting could help reduce review effort and backlog while preserving analyst oversight. This matters because the customer is under pressure to shorten audit cycles and improve consistency."

The hypothesis works because it is specific enough to validate.

It names the workflow problem, the possible improvement, the expected outcome, and the customer priority.

It also leaves room for the next step: checking the evidence.

## Slide 21/46 - Separate evidence from assumptions

Before a value hypothesis becomes credible, you need to separate what you know from what you still assume.

This is not another framework. It is a credibility check.

Evidence is what the customer has already told you, shown you, or can reasonably validate.

Assumptions are the parts of the value story that may be true but still need proof.

Evidence may include:

- Workflow volume
- Current effort or cost
- Cycle time
- Error, rework, or escalation rates
- Stakeholder priorities
- Existing operational metrics
- Customer-provided examples
- Sample work or representative documents
- Current ownership of the workflow

Assumptions may include:

- Adoption level
- Baseline performance
- Data availability
- Human review needs
- Deployment complexity
- Change-management effort
- Whether saved time becomes cost reduction, added capacity, improved service quality, or risk reduction

This distinction helps you sell more credibly.

If you know analysts spend 2–3 hours per case, you can use that as evidence.

If you do not know whether saved time would become reduced cost, increased throughput, or improved review quality, that is an assumption to validate.

Use this sentence pattern:

"The evidence we have is [known evidence]. The current assumption is [assumption]. The next validation step is [specific evidence to gather]."

Example:

"The evidence we have is that support agents are escalating policy questions because answers are hard to confirm across multiple sources. The current assumption is that better retrieval and synthesis would reduce avoidable escalations. The next validation step is to confirm baseline escalation rate, average search time, and answer-quality expectations with the workflow owner."

This keeps the opportunity moving without overstating what has been proven.

## Slide 22/46 - Why unsupported value claims weaken sales conversations

Unsupported value claims are broad statements that sound persuasive but are not yet grounded in customer evidence.

"AI will save the team time."

"This will reduce cost."

"This will lower risk."

"This will create ROI quickly."

"This use case will transform the business."

For sellers, these claims create avoidable problems.

First, they reduce trust.

Senior stakeholders are used to hearing broad technology promises.

If you cannot explain the workflow, the metric, and the validation path, the claim may sound like hype rather than business analysis.

Second, they are hard for customer sponsors to repeat.

A sponsor may need to explain the opportunity to finance, operations, technology, legal, compliance, or executive stakeholders. "AI will improve productivity" is difficult to defend without workflow evidence.

Third, they weaken qualification.

If the team progresses an opportunity based on a broad value claim, the opportunity may stall later when the customer asks for evidence that was never gathered.

Fourth, they make OpenAI sound like a generic efficiency tool.

If the value story is only "time saved," the customer may compare the opportunity against any other productivity tool or keep it inside a limited experimentation budget.

A stronger story ties the use case to growth capacity, operating leverage, customer experience, risk control, or workflow transformation.

The goal is not to be cautious for the sake of caution, but about being precise.

Use language that is confident and evidence-based.

| Instead of saying | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "This will reduce cost" | "The value hypothesis is that reducing manual review effort could lower cost per case or create saved capacity. We should validate current effort, volume, and how the customer would use the capacity." |
| "AI will improve productivity" | "This workflow improvement could reduce time spent on repetitive preparation work, giving account teams more capacity for customer-facing activities." |
| "This will reduce risk" | "AI-assisted review could help surface policy exceptions earlier, subject to validation with representative documents and human oversight requirements." |
| "The ROI should be strong" | "The directional benefit may be meaningful, but ROI requires solution cost, adoption rate, time horizon, and value-conversion assumptions." |

Precise value language helps you sell more effectively because it gives the customer a claim they can validate, repeat, and sponsor.

## Slide 28/46 - Measure only what supports the value story

Once the value hypothesis is clear, choose indicators that support the specific value claim you are making.

The goal is not to measure everything.

The goal is to choose evidence that supports the specific value claim you are making.

Ask:

- What business outcome are we trying to support?
- What indicator would show whether that outcome is improving?
- What baseline do we need?
- What assumption are we testing?
- What evidence would help the customer decide whether to keep investing in the opportunity?

Example

Example

If the value story is about increasing seller capacity, you may need preparation time, follow-up quality, account coverage, and how the customer defines productive seller time.

Example

Use indicators that match the outcome.

| Business outcome | Indicators to consider |
| --- | --- |
| Speed | Cycle time, turnaround time, wait time, backlog age |
| Productivity or capacity | Manual effort, cases handled, review hours, queue size |
| Cost | Outside spend, rework cost, cost per case, avoided hiring |
| Quality | Error rate, rework rate, consistency score, quality review findings |

## Slide 29/46 - Use a directional benefit estimate as an early sizing check

A directional benefit estimate is an evidence-based estimate of the potential impact of improving a workflow.

It helps you and the customer answer:

"If this workflow improves, could the benefit be meaningful enough to keep pursuing?"

For a seller, this matters because not every useful AI idea is automatically a strong business opportunity.

A workflow may be frustrating, but the value may still be too small, too unclear, or too hard to validate.

A directional benefit estimate helps you test whether the opportunity is worth deeper discovery, a pilot, a technical validation step, or a stronger business case.

It is not a final business case.

It is not a promise.

It is not ROI.

Here is a simple support operations example.

A support team handles 10,000 policy-related cases per month. Agents spend an average of 4 minutes searching for policy guidance per case.

**Current search effort**

10,000 cases × 4 minutes = 40,000 minutes, or about 667 hours per month.

**Directional estimate if the customer believes AI-enabled retrieval could reduce search time by 25%**

667 hours × 25% = about 167 hours of saved search capacity per month.

That estimate is useful because it gives the customer a more concrete sense of potential value.

But it is still not ROI.

It does not include solution cost, implementation cost, adoption rate, time horizon, workflow change, or how the customer will use the saved capacity.

A strong seller would frame it like this:

"Based on the current assumptions, this could represent meaningful saved capacity. The next step is to validate the baseline, adoption assumptions, and how the customer would convert that capacity into business value."

If the estimate is small or built on weak inputs, the next move may be to refine the use case, gather better evidence, or focus on a higher-value workflow.

## Slide 30/46 - Know when not to estimate yet

Not every early opportunity needs a calculation immediately.

A directional benefit estimate is useful only when the available inputs help you and the customer understand the opportunity more clearly. If the inputs are mostly guesses, the estimate can make the opportunity look more certain than it really is.

A specific number can sound convincing, even when the assumptions behind it have not been validated. In that situation, the stronger seller move is to identify which inputs are missing and make those inputs the next validation step.

Do not force an estimate when:

- The workflow volume is unknown.
- Current effort or cost is unknown.
- The expected improvement is a guess with no customer input.
- The business outcome is unclear.
- The customer has not agreed which workflow problem matters most.
- The estimate would distract from a more important discovery.

In those cases, the next step is to identify the missing evidence.

For example:

"The next step is to gather the baseline evidence needed to estimate the potential impact."

That is still a strong value conversation.

It shows the customer that you know what evidence is needed and that you are not overstating the opportunity.

## Slide 31/46 - Keep directional benefit separate from ROI

A directional benefit estimate helps you size potential impact. ROI requires more.

Before calling something ROI, you need assumptions such as:

- Solution cost
- Implementation and integration cost
- Enablement, adoption, and change-management cost
- Time horizon
- Expected adoption rate
- Baseline performance
- Whether saved time becomes cost reduction, revenue capacity, service improvement, or risk reduction.
- Ongoing operating and support costs
- Risk adjustment and confidence level

This distinction matters because time saved does not automatically become cost saved.

A customer might use saved time to:

- Handle more cases
- Improve service quality
- Reduce backlog
- Increase revenue capacity
- Avoid hiring
- Reduce external spend
- Improve control or consistency
- Free experts for higher-value work

Each path can be valuable, but each path needs different evidence.

For example, saved review time may become cost reduction only if it reduces contractor spend, outside counsel spend, overtime, or planned hiring.

If employees use the time for higher-value work, the benefit may be capacity, throughput, quality, or growth enablement instead.

A strong seller does not avoid financial value. A strong seller keeps the financial language accurate.

Use this distinction:

| If you have... | You can usually say... | Do not say yet... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workflow volume and current time spent | "This may represent saved capacity." | "This is cost savings." |
| Current cost and a validated improvement assumption | "This may represent potential gross benefit." | "This is final ROI." |
| Cost, adoption, time horizon, implementation effort, and value-conversion assumptions | "This can support an ROI model." | "ROI is proven" unless the model has been validated. |

Your role in this course is to help the customer move toward the evidence needed for a stronger business case.

After creating a directional estimate, ask:

- Which input is strongest?
- Which input is weakest?
- What assumption could change the estimate most?
- Who owns the metric or baseline?
- What should the partner and customer confirm next?

The estimate should lead to a practical sales action.

| What the estimate shows | Seller action |
| --- | --- |
| Potential benefit looks meaningful and evidence is reasonably strong | Confirm baselines with the workflow owner and prepare for deeper validation. |
| Potential benefit looks meaningful but evidence is weak | Gather missing volume, effort, cost, adoption, or quality assumptions before positioning the value more strongly. |
| Potential benefit looks small | Revisit the workflow scope, business outcome, or use-case priority before investing more time. |
| Estimate depends on a major assumption | Name the assumption clearly and make it the next validation step. |

This keeps you from treating the estimate as the end of the conversation. A directional estimate should help you decide what to do next.

## Slide 33/46 - Real-world example

A support operations team wants to reduce policy escalations. Discovery shows that customer support agents search across multiple policy sources and escalate when answers are inconsistent or hard to confirm.

Useful indicators may include:

- Monthly policy-related support case volume
- Average search time per case
- Escalation rate
- Percentage of escalations that may be avoidable
- Average effort required to resolve an escalation
- Answer consistency
- Customer response time
- Agent satisfaction

A directional benefit estimate could use customer-validated inputs to estimate saved capacity

Monthly cases × Current search time per case × Expected search-time reduction = Potential saved search capacity

A second estimate could look at escalation effort

Monthly cases × Current escalation rate × Estimated avoidable escalation share × Effort per escalation = Potential avoided escalation effort

These estimates help you describe directional benefit more concretely, but they are still not ROI.

They show possible saved capacity or gross benefit before the team has validated solution cost, implementation effort, adoption rate, time horizon, and how saved capacity converts into business value.

The next validation step is to confirm:

- Baseline search time
- Escalation definitions
- Answer-quality expectations
- Adoption assumptions
- Data access
- Workflow-owner priorities
- Human review or approval needs

This gives the customer a practical path forward: validate the evidence before building a full business case.

## Slide 34/46 - Knowledge check

**Question:** A customer estimates monthly cases × current search time per case × expected search-time reduction. They do not yet know the solution cost, adoption rate, or time horizon. How should this estimate be framed?

- As a risk-adjusted return estimate, because it depends on future assumptions.
- As ROI, because it uses a calculation tied to customer workflow data.
- **As a saved capacity estimate, not ROI.** ✓ Correct
- As a confirmed cost reduction, because search time is manual effort.

This estimate uses time-based inputs, so it can show potential saved capacity. It should not be called ROI until cost, adoption, time horizon, and value-conversion assumptions are validated.

## Slide 35/46 - Summary

A directional estimate is a decision aid, not the decision itself.

Use it to test whether the opportunity is worth deeper validation, identify weak assumptions, and define the next sales action.

## Slide 36/46 - Introduction (Module: Developing customer-specific value narratives)

The value narrative is the short version a customer stakeholder can repeat.

It should preserve the evidence you have gathered while making the opportunity clear enough to discuss, validate, and progress.

## Slide 37/46 - Assemble the value narrative

A value narrative brings the main parts of the opportunity into a short, customer-ready summary.

This matters because most sales opportunities involve more than one stakeholder. The person you speak with first may need to explain the opportunity to a finance leader, operations leader, technology leader, executive sponsor, or workflow owner.

If the value story is too broad, that sponsor may struggle to repeat it. If it is too technical, the business audience may miss why it matters. If it is too financial too early, the customer may challenge assumptions that have not been validated.

A strong value narrative helps avoid those problems.

It gives the customer a clear way to understand:

- What business priority the opportunity supports
- What workflow problem is being addressed
- What AI-enabled improvement may be possible
- What business outcome could improve
- What evidence supports the value story
- What assumptions still need validation
- What next step would make the opportunity more credible

Think of the value narrative as the bridge between your discovery work and the next sales action.

It is not a pitch script. It is not a final business case. It is not a product recommendation.

It is a concise explanation of why the opportunity may be worth validating.

A weak value narrative sounds like:

"OpenAI can help the support team become more productive and improve customer experience."

That statement may be directionally true, but it is difficult for a stakeholder to evaluate. It does not explain the workflow problem, the business outcome, the evidence, or the next step.

A stronger value narrative sounds like:

"The support team is losing time because agents search across multiple policy sources before responding. An OpenAI-enabled retrieval and answer-synthesis workflow could reduce search effort, improve answer consistency, and lower avoidable escalations. The next step is to validate baseline search time, escalation rate, answer-quality expectations, and adoption assumptions with the workflow owner."

The stronger version works because it gives the customer something specific to discuss, challenge, validate, and share with others.

In the rest of this module, you'll learn how to shape that summary for different stakeholders while keeping the evidence and assumptions consistent.

## Slide 38/46 - Tailor the narrative by stakeholder

Different stakeholders evaluate value differently.

Tailoring does not mean inventing new claims. It means emphasizing the parts of the evidence that matter most to each audience.

| Stakeholder | Value narrative emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| CFO | Financial exposure, cost, productivity, investment assumptions, and confidence in the benefit estimate |
| COO | Throughput, cycle time, operating burden, and service quality |
| CIO/CTO | Scalability, integration fit, governance, data access, and operational control |
| Workflow owner | Day-to-day usability, quality, adoption, handoffs, and effort reduction |
| Executive sponsor | Strategic priority, urgency, risk of inaction, business impact, and why the opportunity deserves attention across the organization |

The same evidence can support different conversations.

For a support policy escalation use case:

A COO may care about fewer escalations, faster response, and support capacity.

A CIO may care about data access, governance, and whether the workflow can be operated safely.

A workflow owner may care about whether support agents trust the answers and can easily review them.

A CFO may care about whether saved capacity could reduce cost per case, avoid hiring, or improve operating leverage.

An executive sponsor may care about whether the use case supports a broader customer experience or transformation priority.

A strong value narrative adapts emphasis without changing the facts.

## Slide 39/46 - Keep the narrative portfolio-aware

A customer-specific value narrative should remain connected to how value may be realized, but it should not become solution routing.

If the solution path is not yet validated, use neutral language such as:

"An OpenAI-enabled workflow improvement…"

If a solution path is already plausible, name it only to clarify the value story.

For example:

"A ChatGPT Work workflow could use approved Apps and files to retrieve and synthesize policy guidance within ChatGPT, while employees review the output before use."

"An API-based workflow could embed intelligence directly into the customer's support application."

"A Codex workflow could help an engineering team delegate bounded software-engineering tasks, such as implementation, testing, or code review, while preserving human verification and approval."

"Agentic capabilities could support multi-step work across tools and approval points within the chosen product experience, subject to validated permissions, review, and operating boundaries."

Use this language carefully.

Do not compare product paths, explain implementation choices, or make architecture claims in this course. The purpose is to show how the value may connect to OpenAI capabilities while leaving detailed product selection, architecture, and deployment planning for later validation.

A good seller narrative keeps three things separate:

| Seller statement | Keep it focused on... |
| --- | --- |
| Value hypothesis | What outcome may improve and why it matters |
| Solution direction | The likely way value could be realized, if already known |
| Validation need | What evidence, constraints, or handoffs are needed next |

This helps you stay credible and avoid over-positioning before the opportunity is ready.

## Slide 40/46 - Create the connected value story

When working with potential customers, use the value story to turn everything you have learned into a short, customer-ready explanation.

The purpose is not to show the customer every step of your analysis. It is to make the opportunity easy to understand, discuss, validate, and progress.

At this point in a customer opportunity, you should have the main pieces of the value story:

- The customer priority
- Workflow problem
- AI-enabled improvement
- Likely business outcome
- Supporting evidence
- Assumptions
- The next step

Do not try to include every concept from the course in the final narrative. The customer does not need to hear the internal thinking step by step.

The final narrative should be concise, specific, and useful for decision-making.

The diagram below shows the elements of a concise value story that a customer stakeholder can understand, validate, and share.

A concise value story might sound like this:

"The customer wants to improve support consistency while controlling operating burden. Today, support agents search across several policy sources and escalate when answers are unclear. An OpenAI-enabled retrieval and answer-synthesis workflow could help reduce search effort, improve answer consistency, and lower avoidable escalations. Based on early assumptions, the opportunity may create meaningful saved support capacity, but the baseline still needs validation. The recommended next step is to confirm search time, escalation rate, answer-quality expectations, and representative support tickets with the workflow owner."

This kind of narrative helps another stakeholder understand the opportunity quickly. It also makes the next action specific: validate the evidence, not just continue discussing AI in broad terms.

A strong value story should pass three tests:

1. Could the customer repeat it to another stakeholder?

2. Could the customer identify what evidence needs to be validated?

3. Could the partner identify the next action needed to progress the opportunity?

If the answer is yes, the narrative is specific enough for early opportunity progression.

## Slide 41/46 - Real-world example

The customer priority is to improve support consistency while controlling operating burden. The workflow problem is that customer support agents search across several knowledge sources and escalate when answers are unclear.

AI-enabled use case: Knowledge retrieval and answer synthesis inside the support workflow.

Likely business outcomes:

- Faster response
- Better answer consistency
- Saved support capacity
- Improved customer experience

Directional impact: Reduced search time and fewer avoidable escalations may create saved capacity and improve response consistency.

Evidence already known: Agents report search friction, escalation rate is visible, and sample support tickets can be reviewed.

Assumptions to validate:

- Baseline search time
- Answer-quality threshold
- Adoption rate
- Data access
- Approval rules
- Whether saved time improves throughput or lowers cost.

Recommended next validation step: Confirm baseline metrics and review representative support-ticket samples with the workflow owner.

A stakeholder-specific version for an operations leader might sound like:

"Support agents are spending time searching across policy sources and escalating when guidance is unclear. An OpenAI-enabled retrieval and synthesis workflow could help reduce avoidable escalations, improve response consistency, and create saved support capacity. To validate the opportunity, we should confirm baseline search time, escalation rate, and response-quality expectations."

A version for a technology leader might sound like:

"The value hypothesis depends on whether support knowledge can be accessed, governed, and reviewed safely in the workflow. The next step is to validate data access, approval rules, and answer-quality expectations using representative support tickets."

Both versions are grounded in the same facts. They simply emphasize different stakeholder concerns.
