## Slide 5/49 - Introduction (Module: Use a simple routing map before naming a solution)

Customers often describe AI needs with broad labels like "agent," "chatbot," "copilot," or "automation."

Those labels are not enough to recommend a solution. The right direction depends on the workflow, users, systems, constraints, and business outcome. In this module, you'll use a simple seller-level routing map to separate three decisions:

- User or product surface: Where will the person or workflow primarily operate?
- Capability or architecture: What must the system do?
- Deployment and operating model: Where and how will it run and be controlled?
- Progression motion: What should happen next?

## Slide 6/49 - Why routing needs structure

Customers often describe what they want using broad labels: "agent," "chatbot," "copilot," "automation," "API," or "migration." These labels are useful clues, but they are not enough to route an opportunity.

Routing is the process of determining the most appropriate OpenAI solution path to explore by separating where the work happens, what capabilities are required, how the solution will run and be controlled, and what should happen next.

A customer who says "we need an agent" may mean several different things.

They might need employees to find information faster.

They might need intelligence embedded into a support workflow.

They might need AI to work across tools and ask for approval.

They might be replacing an existing automation tool.

This is why you need routing structure before naming a surface or solution. The goal is to slow down just enough to understand which decision is being made.

In this course, vertical context means the industry-specific conditions that shape whether an AI opportunity is credible, valuable, feasible, and ready to progress.

It includes the customer's workflows, stakeholders, data environment, governance considerations, risk tolerance, adoption needs, success metrics, and operating model.

You do not need to become deeply certified in financial services, healthcare, life sciences, or any other vertical to use this judgment.

But you do need to understand how context changes the questions you ask, the risks you notice, the validation you recommend, and the handoff you prepare.

Use the solution map as a seller guide. It is not something you need to present to the customer by name. It helps you organize your thinking so the recommendation is based on workflow evidence rather than product labels.

## Slide 7/49 - The sales-facing routing map

The solution map separates three solution decisions from the progression decision.

| Decision layer | Seller question | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| User or product surface | Where will the person or workflow primarily operate? | ChatGPT Chat; ChatGPT Work; Codex; an application or process built with the API |
| Capability or architecture | What must the system do? | Apps and tools; Workspace Agents; retrieval; structured outputs; code execution; automation |
| Deployment and operating model | Where and how will it run and be controlled? | Hosted or embedded operation; local or cloud execution; identity; permissions; approvals; retention; logging; human review; self-managed open-weight deployment when validated |
| Progression motion | What should happen next? | Further discovery; validation; migration discovery; technical handoff; deployment handoff |

User or product surface answers:

"Where will the person or workflow primarily operate?"

**ChatGPT**

ChatGPT Chat is the collaborative surface for exploring, asking questions, drafting, critiquing, and refining work with the user closely involved.

ChatGPT Work is the delegative surface for longer, multi-step knowledge work that uses context and tools to produce completed or review-ready outputs while the user steers and approves.

**API**

An application or process built with the API is the builder and integration surface when intelligence must be embedded into products, systems, applications, or operational workflows.

**Codex**

Codex is the specialized surface for software engineering work across planning, implementation, testing, review, debugging, documentation, and maintenance.

Capability or architecture answers:

"What must the system do?"

**Agents**

Examples include Apps and tools, Workspace Agents, retrieval, structured outputs, code execution, and automation. An agentic requirement describes what the system must do—such as planning, using tools, taking multi-step action, or requesting approval—not a separate product surface.

**Open models**

Deployment and operating model answers: "Where and how will the solution run and be controlled?"

**Hybrid approaches**

This layer covers hosted, embedded, local, cloud, or self-managed operation, together with identity, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, monitoring, and human review. A customer-managed open-weight model is a deployment consideration only when the requirement is validated.

Primary product direction answers:

"What should happen next?"

**Migration discovery**

Relevant when the customer is moving from an existing AI tool, stack, workflow, or vendor environment.

**Technical or deployment handoff**

Relevant when deeper validation is required before the opportunity can progress.

**Further discovery or validation**

Relevant when the customer problem, workflow, ownership, value, or readiness is still unclear.

Use this visual as a quick reminder that the surface, capability or architecture, deployment and operating model, and next action are separate seller decisions.

## Slide 8/49 - Real-world example

A customer support leader says:

"We need an AI agent to reduce ticket resolution time."

Do not immediately route to "agents" as though that were a product surface like ChatGPT or an API-based application.

The better response is to clarify the workflow.

If support employees need to explore policies, draft responses, and refine answers interactively, ChatGPT Chat may be the likely surface.

If the team needs to delegate a longer, multi-step process that gathers context and produces a review-ready response package, ChatGPT Work may be the likely surface.

If intelligence needs to be embedded inside the ticketing system, an application or process built with the API may be the likely surface.

If the workflow needs to classify tickets, retrieve account context, draft responses, escalate exceptions, and request approval, those are capability and architecture requirements to validate. Depending on the workflow, they may be supported through ChatGPT Work, a Workspace Agent, an API-based solution, or a combination.

If the customer is replacing an existing support automation tool, migration discovery may be the right progression motion.

The value conversation should stay anchored in the workflow outcome: faster resolution, more consistent responses, lower escalation burden, or improved customer experience.

## Slide 12/49 - Introduction (Module: Map the workflow to a user or product surface)

Once you can separate routing decisions, the next step is to connect the customer's workflow to a likely user or product surface.

## Slide 13/49 - Start with workflow evidence

A use case is easier to route when you can explain the work clearly.

Before recommending a surface, make sure you understand:

- Who does the work today?
- What workflow is slow, costly, risky, inconsistent, or difficult to scale?
- What output should improve?
- Where AI would interact with people, systems, tools, or data?
- What value the customer expects if the workflow improves?

Vague labels such as "copilot," "AI assistant," or "automation" are not enough.

You should be able to describe what the AI would actually do in the workflow.

For example, "AI for legal" is too broad.

"Help legal operations teams summarize routine contract changes, flag missing clauses for review, and draft business-friendly summaries" is easier to route because it describes the work, user group, and output.

Vertical context matters here because the same workflow label may carry different implications. A document-review workflow in a bank, a healthcare organization, or a manufacturing company may involve different stakeholders, data boundaries, approval steps, and success measures.

Do not overclaim those requirements. Identify what may matter and validate it with the customer.

## Slide 14/49 - When ChatGPT Chat or ChatGPT Work is the likely surface

ChatGPT is often the first product family to explore when the customer need is centered on employee-facing knowledge work. The next distinction is whether the work is collaborative or delegative.

Strong fit signals for ChatGPT Chat include:

- Employees need to explore, ask questions, draft, critique, and refine work interactively.
- Human judgment, review, and iteration are central to each step.
- The customer wants a familiar employee-facing workspace without building a custom application first.

Strong fit signals for ChatGPT Work include delegated, multi-step knowledge work; organizational context and tools; a clear outcome or artifact; and user steering or approval during the work.

Examples include preparing a review-ready operating report, assembling a presentation from multiple sources, or running a repeatable workflow that produces finished materials.

ChatGPT may be a weaker fit if the primary need is an embedded custom experience or high-volume system-to-system execution; an API-based application or process may be the better surface.

If the problem is software engineering, Codex may be the better surface.

A more credible statement is:

"ChatGPT Chat may fit if users need to think and create together. ChatGPT Work may fit if they can define an outcome, provide context and constraints, and review completed or review-ready work. We should validate the user groups, data access, available tools, approval boundaries, and adoption path before confirming either surface."

## Slide 15/49 - When an API-based application or process is the likely surface

An application or process built with the API is usually the surface to explore when the customer needs to embed intelligence into a product, system, operation, or customer-facing experience.

Strong fit signals include:

- The experience must live inside an existing product, application, system, or workflow.
- The solution needs custom logic, orchestration, structured outputs, retrieval, tool use, or application-level control.
- The workflow must serve customers, internal systems, or high-volume operational processes.

The API path often fits when intelligence needs to become part of how a product, process, or system operates. For example, a customer may want to embed AI into a support portal, onboarding flow, underwriting system, reporting workflow, or internal operations tool.

Do not treat the API as simply "more technical ChatGPT." The stronger judgment is about where the experience must live. If users need an employee-facing workspace, ChatGPT Chat or Work may be the better surface. If intelligence must be embedded in software, systems, or customer experiences, an API-based application or process may be the better surface.

An API-based surface may be premature if the customer is not ready to support integration, evaluation, governance, or product ownership. That does not mean the opportunity is weak. It means the architecture and operating model need validation before the opportunity progresses.

## Slide 16/49 - When Codex is the likely surface

Codex is usually the first direction to explore when the customer problem is tied to software delivery or engineering work.

Strong fit signals include:

- The customer wants to improve planning, coding, testing, review, documentation, maintenance, or modernization.
- Engineering teams need help moving work through the software development lifecycle, not only generating code.
- The business value depends on faster, safer, or more scalable software delivery.

Codex should be framed around software delivery work rather than generic productivity. A customer may be dealing with old code, slow review cycles, fragile tests, documentation gaps, modernization needs, or engineering bottlenecks that prevent product or operational progress.

You do not need to design the engineering workflow. Your job is to recognize when the business problem is rooted in SDLC work and capture the evidence that a Codex-focused discussion should be explored.

Codex may be a weaker fit when the workflow is not connected to software delivery, engineering operations, or technical delivery. If the buyer is primarily looking for collaborative knowledge work, delegated knowledge work, or an embedded business workflow, ChatGPT Chat, ChatGPT Work, or an API-based application may be more plausible surfaces.

## Slide 17/49 - Real-world examples

**Finance reporting**

A finance team spends days gathering source material and preparing weekly performance updates. ChatGPT Chat may fit if analysts need to explore and refine the story interactively. ChatGPT Work may fit if they can delegate the multi-step gathering, analysis, and production of a review-ready update while staying in control.

An application or process built with the API may become relevant if the reporting workflow needs embedded data extraction, structured outputs, or integration into an internal reporting system.

You should validate where the work happens today, what data is required, who reviews the output, and what evidence would show the reporting process improved.

**Customer onboarding**

A SaaS company wants to personalize onboarding journeys inside its product. An application or process built with the API may be the likely surface because intelligence needs to be embedded into the customer-facing experience.

ChatGPT Chat or Work may still support internal teams with research, enablement, account preparation, or customer-success planning. That does not make ChatGPT the primary surface if the main business problem is the customer-facing product experience.

**Legacy modernization**

An engineering organization is slowed down by old code, fragile tests, and review bottlenecks. Codex may be the likely surface because the problem is centered on software delivery and SDLC throughput.

You should capture what part of the lifecycle is creating the constraint: planning, coding, testing, review, documentation, maintenance, or modernization. That evidence helps the right technical or Codex-focused owner validate the path.

## Slide 18/49 - Summary (Module: Map the workflow to a user or product surface)

User or product surface is about the most credible place where the person or workflow will operate.

ChatGPT Chat is strongest for collaborative thinking and creation. ChatGPT Work is strongest for delegated, multi-step knowledge work that returns completed or review-ready outputs. An application or process built with the API is strongest when intelligence must be embedded into a product, system, or operational workflow. Codex is strongest when the problem is rooted in software delivery or engineering work.

A credible recommendation explains the workflow evidence behind the surface. Avoid saying only, "This is ChatGPT," "This is API," or "This is Codex." Explain whether the work is collaborative, delegative, embedded, or engineering-focused, and state what still needs validation.

## Slide 21/49 - When the workflow needs apps, tools, or multi-step action

Sometimes the customer does not just need an answer, draft, or summary. They need AI to help move a workflow forward.

Listen for statements like:

- "We need it to take action, not just answer questions."
- "It needs to work across several systems or tools."
- "It needs to check status, update records, create tickets, or trigger follow-ups."
- "It needs to ask for approval before completing certain steps."
- "It needs to handle exceptions or escalate to a person."

These are capability and architecture signals. The workflow may require apps or tools, retrieval, multi-step planning, action, scheduling, approvals, exception handling, or a Workspace Agent.

Do not route to "agents" as if that were a product surface. Clarify what the system must do, then determine whether ChatGPT Work, a Workspace Agent, an API-based solution, or a combination can support it. Availability and permissions must be validated for the customer's plan and workspace.

It might sound like this:

"This workflow has an agentic capability requirement because the system must move work across tools and request approval before certain actions. We should validate the exact steps, apps, permissions, exception paths, and review requirements before choosing the architecture."

## Slide 22/49 - When deployment control or self-managed infrastructure may matter

Some customers have deployment or operating requirements that may make a hosted or embedded path insufficient. Treat these as requirements to validate, not automatic product conclusions.

Listen for things like:

- "We need to run this in our own environment."
- "Data cannot leave our controlled infrastructure."
- "We have strict data residency or sovereignty requirements."
- "We need offline, private, or air-gapped operation."
- "We need deeper control over where and how the solution executes, customization, or infrastructure costs."
- "Our team is prepared to manage model operations ourselves."

These are deployment and operating model signals. First validate whether available OpenAI-hosted products and enterprise controls can satisfy the requirement.

A customer-managed open-weight model may be relevant only when validated requirements call for self-managed execution, offline operation, or infrastructure ownership.

This kind of requirement usually needs technical validation because self-managed deployment introduces operating, governance, monitoring, and support responsibilities.

Capture why the customer needs self-managed deployment and whether the customer is prepared to own those responsibilities.

Do not conclude that sensitive data or data residency alone requires open-weight deployment. Evaluate available hosted controls and involve technical, security, and compliance specialists before recommending a self-managed path.

## Slide 23/49 - When one surface or capability is not enough

Some workflows span multiple user groups, systems, surfaces, or operating needs. In those situations, one surface or capability may not be enough.

This could sound like:

- "Employees need a workspace, but the workflow also needs system integration."
- "The AI needs to support internal teams and customer-facing users."
- "Some work should happen in ChatGPT Chat or Work, but some needs to be embedded in our product."
- "Engineering work is part of the opportunity, but business users also need adoption support."
- "The workflow crosses systems, approvals, data sources, or handoffs."

These signals may indicate a combined or hybrid solution. A combined solution may use ChatGPT Chat or Work for employee-facing work, an API-based application for embedded workflows, Codex for software engineering, and apps, tools, or agentic capabilities where needed.

Do not recommend "hybrid" as a way to avoid making a decision. A combined solution should have a clear business reason. Name which surface, capability, and operating model each part of the workflow requires.

For example:

"ChatGPT Work may support the employee-facing review process, while an application built with the API may be needed if approved outputs must be embedded into the customer's workflow system. We should validate whether both surfaces are required for the first phase or whether one should be explored first."

## Slide 24/49 - When the customer is replacing or moving from an existing AI environment

Migration is relevant when the customer already has AI tools, models, workflows, vendors, pilots, or production systems in place.

Listen for statements such as:

- "We already have an AI tool, but it is not scaling."
- "We want to consolidate AI spend or vendors."
- "We are replacing an existing chatbot, copilot, model, or automation platform."
- "We have pilots in multiple teams and need a more governed approach."
- "We need to move users, workflows, data, or integrations from the current setup."
- "We are worried about disruption, continuity, evaluation, or change management."

Migration is not a product surface or capability. It is a progression or implementation motion. The opportunity may require current-state discovery before the target surface, capabilities, and operating model can be validated.

Capture what the customer is moving from, why they want to change, what must continue working, and what risks need validation. If current-state and target-state planning are needed, route to the appropriate migration, technical, or deployment owner.

## Slide 27/49 - Summary (Module: Identify capability, architecture, and operating requirements)

Capability, architecture, and operating requirements help you avoid treating every AI opportunity as a simple product choice.

## Slide 28/49 - Introduction (Module: Test whether the route is credible)

A route can sound plausible and still be too early to recommend with confidence. The seller's job is to test whether the direction fits the workflow and whether the customer is ready to take the next step.

In this module, you'll look at the readiness, constraints, and tradeoffs that affect routing decisions.

## Slide 29/49 - Separate solution fit from readiness

A solution direction can sound plausible and still be too early to recommend.

Solution fit asks "Does this direction match the workflow, use case, and value hypothesis?"

Readiness asks "Is the customer prepared to move this direction forward?"

A route can fit the use case but still be premature if the customer has not clarified the workflow, named an owner, confirmed data access, aligned stakeholders, or defined success.

For example:

API may appear to fit because the customer wants intelligence inside an existing operations system.

But if the customer has not identified a product owner, data source, integration path, evaluation approach, or governance review process, the opportunity might not be ready for a technical handoff yet. The next step may be a validation session.

Avoid presenting a direction as ready just because it sounds like a good match. A stronger recommendation explains what is known, what is assumed, and what still needs validation.

## Slide 30/49 - Check whether the customer is ready to move forward

Use readiness checks to decide whether the opportunity can advance or needs more discovery.

| Readiness check | What to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Problem and priority workflow | Can the customer clearly explain the business problem, priority workflow, team, and user group? |
| Stakeholder ownership | Is there a business owner who cares about the outcome and can help drive the next step? |
| AI maturity and next step | Has the customer shown enough experience, urgency, or operating capacity to move beyond exploration? Have they agreed to a concrete next action? |
| Governance and approvals | Do they know who needs to review or approve AI usage? |
| Data and integration readiness | Do they know what data, knowledge, or systems the AI would need to access? Does the workflow need to connect into existing systems? |
| Adoption and success measures | Do they know who will use the solution, what behavior needs to change, and what improvement would prove value? |
| Whether the value hypothesis is explicit | If the customer can only say "AI should improve the business somehow," the route may still be too early. A credible next step needs a testable reason to act. |
| Whether the customer can support validation | If no owner can join a validation session, share workflow detail, confirm requirements, or involve the right reviewers, the opportunity may need more discovery before it can progress. |

If several answers are unclear, the next step is usually more discovery.

If the use case and value are clear but operating details are uncertain, the next step is usually validation.

If technical feasibility, data, governance, or integration questions are material, the next step is usually technical or deployment handoff.

## Slide 31/49 - Ask three routing questions when the workflow is complex

Use these questions only when they help clarify the opportunity. They are not a separate framework to memorize, but a practical way to test whether the proposed solution path fits the customer's workflow and operating requirements.

**Question 1:**

Where will the person or workflow primarily operate?

This is the user or product surface question: ChatGPT Chat, ChatGPT Work, Codex, or an application or process built with the API.

**Question 2:**

What capabilities or architecture are required?

This covers business context, apps and tools, retrieval, structured outputs, code execution, multi-step action, scheduling, approvals, and custom orchestration.

**Question 3:**

Where and how will it run and be controlled?

This is the deployment and operating model question. Runtime means where and how the solution executes. Monitoring and tracing are observability; permissions, approvals, retention, logging, and human review are governance and operating controls.

Use plain language first: "Where will the work happen, what must the system do, and how will it run and be controlled?"

Use the formal terms only where they help connect the sales conversation to technical or deployment teams. Your job is to document the answers and open questions, not design the operating model.

The path below shows how each decision builds on the previous one. Start with workflow evidence, then identify the likely surface, capability or architecture, deployment and operating model, readiness and tradeoffs, and next step.

## Slide 32/49 - Compare the tradeoffs that could change the route

Before recommending a direction, check whether any tradeoff makes the route stronger, weaker, premature, or dependent on handoff.

| Tradeoff | Seller question | What it may affect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Will this be expensive to build, run, govern, or scale? | Whether the value case is strong enough to justify the route. |
| Latency | Does the workflow need real-time or near-real-time responses? | Whether technical validation is needed before routing. |
| Integration | Does the solution need apps, tools, system integration, or an embedded experience? | Whether an API-based application, Workspace Agent, app action, combined solution, or technical handoff needs validation. |
| Data | What data is required, where does it live, and how sensitive is it? | Whether access, governance, security, available hosted controls, or self-managed open-weight requirements need validation. |
| Governance | What approvals, policies, audit needs, or oversight requirements apply? | Whether a validation session or deployment handoff is needed. |
| Adoption | Who needs to use the solution, and what behavior must change? | Whether the solution is ready to create value in practice. |
| Complexity | Is the proposed route proportional to maturity, urgency, and expected value? | Whether the route should be simplified or phased. |
| Human role and reversibility | Does a person need to review, approve, override, or supervise the output? Can mistakes be corrected easily? | Whether approval, audit, escalation, or review controls need validation. |

Tradeoffs are not reasons to stall the opportunity. They are reasons to route it responsibly.

If the tradeoff is business-related, continue value and stakeholder alignment.

If the tradeoff is technical, data, security, governance, or integration-related, capture it for validation or handoff.

## Slide 33/49 - Real-world example

**Operations team**

A manufacturing operations team wants to reduce equipment downtime by helping maintenance teams identify likely failure causes and prepare repair guidance.

You should first test the likely route:

ChatGPT Chat could fit if technicians and managers need interactive troubleshooting guidance, knowledge access, shift handoff notes, and repair summaries. ChatGPT Work could fit if the team can delegate multi-step gathering, synthesis, and preparation of review-ready guidance.

An API-based application may fit if intelligence needs to be embedded into maintenance systems, sensor workflows, work-order tools, or technician applications.

Capability or architecture requirements may include gathering equipment context, retrieving maintenance history, creating a work order, notifying the right owner, or requesting approval before action.

Technical validation might be needed if the workflow depends on live equipment data, low-latency response, safety constraints, system integration, or strict governance.

Do not simply recommend "AI for predictive maintenance."

A credible recommendation should explain the workflow, likely surface, required capabilities or architecture, deployment and operating assumptions, readiness gaps, tradeoffs, and next step.

## Slide 35/49 - Summary (Module: Test whether the route is credible)

A credible solution map needs both fit and readiness. Fit means the proposed surface, required capabilities, and operating model match the customer's workflow, use case, user group, and value hypothesis. Readiness means the customer has enough clarity around ownership, data, governance, adoption, success measures, and next steps.

Use tradeoffs and constraints to decide what still needs validation. A strong recommendation is clear about what is known, what is assumed, and what should happen next.

## Slide 36/49 - Introduction (Module: Recommend the next step and prepare the handoff)

After mapping a likely route, the next task is to make the recommendation usable. A customer, partner team, or technical owner should be able to understand why the direction fits and what needs to happen next.

In this module, you'll practice turning routing judgment into a clear next step and concise handoff.

## Slide 37/49 - What a strong recommendation includes

A strong recommendation answers six questions:

- What customer problem and workflow are we trying to improve?
- What value hypothesis makes this worth pursuing?
- Which user or product surface appears most credible?
- Which capability or architecture requirements need validation?
- Which deployment and operating assumptions, risks, or controls need validation?
- What next step should happen, and who should own it?

The recommendation should explain why the selected surface and architecture appear credible and why alternatives are less likely, premature, or dependent on further validation.

This does not mean presenting a final architecture. It means giving the customer, partner team, and OpenAI team enough clarity to decide what should happen next.

A strong recommendation might sound like:

"Based on the workflow evidence, ChatGPT Work appears to be the most credible first surface because the immediate need is to gather context, synthesize it, and produce a review-ready deliverable while employees steer and approve the work. ChatGPT Chat may be sufficient if the task remains highly interactive. An API-based application may become relevant if the workflow must be embedded into an internal system. Before progressing, validate data access, apps and tools, approval boundaries, operating controls, and success measures."

## Slide 38/49 - Use next-step logic

Use the next step that matches the opportunity evidence.

| Condition | Guidance |
| --- | --- |
| If the customer problem or stakeholder evidence is weak, continue discovery. | Do not recommend a route until the workflow, owner, and value are clearer. |
| If the use case and value are clear but requirements are uncertain, run a validation session. | Use the session to confirm workflow details, success measures, data needs, approvals, capability or architecture requirements, and deployment and operating requirements. |
| If technical feasibility, integration, governance, data, or security questions are material, involve the appropriate technical or deployment owner. | Sales should not try to resolve these questions alone. |
| If migration signals are present, initiate current-state and target-state discovery. | Clarify what the customer is moving from, why they want to change, what must continue working, and what risks need validation. |
| If evidence, value, readiness, and routing are clear, prepare a structured handoff. | A handoff should help the next owner validate the opportunity without restarting discovery. |

## Slide 39/49 - Use a concise handoff note

A concise handoff note gives the next owner consistent opportunity context.

Use this structure:

| Handoff | Capture |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow and priority use case | What workflow is being improved, and which use case appears most important? |
| Customer owner and partner owner | Who owns the business outcome on the customer side, and who owns the opportunity on the partner side? |
| Evidence gathered | What has the customer shared about the problem, workflow, stakeholders, value, and next step? |
| Value hypothesis | What improvement may matter to the customer, and why is it worth pursuing? |
| Likely surface and alternatives considered | What user or product surface appears most credible? What alternatives were considered, and why are they less likely, premature, or dependent on validation? |
| Capability, architecture, and operating requirements | Are there any requirements related to apps, tools, Workspace Agents, retrieval, structured outputs, automation, open-weight models, governance, data, integration, adoption, or operating models that may shape the solution? |
| Risks, assumptions, and open questions | What is known? What is assumed? What still needs validation? |
| Recommended next action and next owner | What should happen next, and who should own it? |

The handoff should be specific enough that someone outside the original conversation can understand the opportunity without restarting discovery.

## Slide 40/49 - What the technical or deployment owner needs from sales

The next owner needs the evidence behind the recommendation, not just the recommended route.

When preparing a handoff, clearly separate:

- What is known?
- What is assumed?
- What needs validation?
- Who owns the next step?
- What decision the next step should enable?

For example, do not write only:

"Customer needs API."

Write:

"An API-based application appears plausible because the customer wants intelligence embedded in an existing claims review workflow. Known: the business owner wants faster review preparation and more consistent summary outputs. Assumed: the required source data can be accessed by the workflow. Needs validation: system access, review requirements, data sensitivity, integration effort, operating controls, and whether apps, tools, or agentic capabilities are required. Recommended next step: technical validation with the customer's operations and IT owners."

This helps reduce rework, avoid premature design conversations, and move the opportunity forward with clearer ownership.

That gives the next owner something useful to validate.
