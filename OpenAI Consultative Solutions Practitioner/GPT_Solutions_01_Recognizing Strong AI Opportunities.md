# Recognizing Strong AI Opportunities

## Slide 1/37 - Title

Recognizing Strong AI Opportunities

Created July 2026

## Slide 2/37 - Introduction

Customers often express interest in AI. Some are exploring possibilities. Some are responding to executive pressure. Some have a real workflow problem they want to solve.

At the start of a conversation, these signals can sound similar. A customer saying "we need AI," "we want a chatbot," or "our leadership wants us to explore AI" may be opening the door to a valuable opportunity. It could also be early curiosity without enough evidence to justify deeper qualification.

This course helps you recognize the difference. You will learn how to look past the AI label and assess whether the customer signal is grounded in a real business problem, a workflow that needs improvement, visible ownership, and a credible next step.

## Slide 3/37 - What you'll learn

By the end of this course, you should be able to:
- Distinguish AI interest from a qualified opportunity.
- Explain the difference between Lead, Stage 0, and Stage 1 opportunity states.
- Recognize the core evidence categories used to assess early AI opportunities.
- Identify evidence, missing evidence, and red flags in customer conversations.
- Judge whether an opportunity is strong, developing, or not yet ready.
- Decide what still needs clarification before progressing an opportunity.

Let's get started!

## Slide 4/37 - Why this matters

Partner sellers and pre-sales teams make better decisions when they can separate general AI interest from opportunity evidence.

If you treat every AI conversation as equally strong, you might spend time on opportunities that are not ready, jump too quickly into product discussion, or miss the discovery needed to understand the customer's real problem.

If you recognize the evidence early, you can focus your effort where it matters most. You can decide whether to keep exploring, ask better qualification questions, involve the right stakeholders, or document what still needs to be clarified before the opportunity progresses.

## Slide 5/37 - How strong opportunities become visible

Strong opportunities become visible through evidence. Instead of asking "Does this customer want AI?" effective partners ask:
- What business problem is the customer trying to solve?
- Where does the problem appear in the workflow?
- Who experiences or owns the problem?
- What would improve if the problem were addressed?
- Has the customer agreed to a meaningful next step?

These questions help you move from enthusiasm to judgment. The goal in this course is not to design the final solution. The goal is to recognize whether the opportunity is strong, still developing, or not yet ready for deeper qualification.

## Slide 6/37 - Introduction (Module: What makes an AI opportunity worth pursuing?)

Many AI conversations begin with a request, idea, or product label. Before you decide whether it is worth pursuing, step back and ask what is really driving the conversation.

The first filter is simple: problem before product.

## Slide 7/37 - AI interest is a starting point, not an opportunity

Customers often begin with broad AI requests. These requests can sound promising, but they usually need translation before they can be assessed.

You might hear statements like:
- "We want to explore AI across the business."
- "We need a chatbot."
- "Our CEO asked us to look into AI."
- "We want to use AI for customer service."

These are useful starting points. They tell you there may be a conversation worth exploring. But they do not yet tell you what problem the customer is trying to solve.

A strong opportunity needs more than interest. It needs enough evidence to show that there is a real problem, that the problem appears in a real workflow, that people care about solving it, and that there is a credible reason to keep moving.

A customer saying "we need a chatbot" may point to many different needs. They might need faster customer support, better internal knowledge access, more consistent HR responses, lower call-center volume, or something else entirely.

Until the workflow problem is clearer and supported by evidence it will be difficult to make a strong recommendation about whether a chatbot is the right solution, what type of chatbot is needed, or how success should be measured.

The conversation should focus first on understanding the underlying business problem, the current process, and the outcomes the customer wants to achieve.

## Slide 8/37 - Start with the customer problem

Strong AI opportunities begin with a customer problem, workflow challenge, or operational constraint.

A customer problem is not just a theme like "efficiency," "automation," or "innovation." It should describe what is happening in the business today and why it matters.

Look for evidence such as:
- A specific workflow that is creating friction.
- A measurable problem or pain point.
- People who experience or own the problem.
- A reason the problem matters to the business.

For example, "we want to improve efficiency" is too broad.

A stronger problem statement would be: "Our claims team spends several hours per case reading documents, extracting key details, and preparing summary notes before a reviewer can make a decision."

That stronger version tells you where the work happens, who is affected, what the team is doing today, and why AI may be worth exploring.

Remember: The goal at this stage is not to solve the problem yet. The goal is to understand whether the problem is real enough to justify deeper qualification.

## Slide 9/37 - Move from AI request to workflow understanding

Customers often describe a possible solution before they have clearly described the problem. That is normal. Your role is to slow the conversation down enough to understand the work behind the request.

Use this simple qualification lens:
- What problem is the customer trying to solve?
- Where does the problem appear in the workflow?
- Who owns or experiences it?
- What improves if the problem is addressed?

This lens is not a formal scoring framework. It is a practical way to keep the conversation problem-led rather than product-led. It helps you avoid treating words like "chatbot," "copilot," "agent," or "automation" as if they are already complete opportunities.

At this stage, you are not deciding where the person or workflow will primarily operate, what the system must do, or where and how it will run and be controlled.

Your job is to gather enough opportunity evidence so those later solution decisions can be made with confidence.

The lens matters because partners sell more effectively when they can explain the customer's work, not just repeat the customer's AI label.

A stronger opportunity story says, "Here is the workflow friction, here is who owns it, here is what could be improved, and here is why the customer wants to keep exploring."

That gives the customer and OpenAI team something concrete to validate.

## Slide 10/37 - Real-world example

A legal operations leader tells a seller: "We want AI for contract review."

At first glance, that sounds like a clear requirement. But "contract review" can refer to many different activities.

It might mean finding key clauses, comparing terms against a playbook, identifying risk, summarizing obligations, routing approvals, drafting fallback language, or reporting on contract status.

A useful seller response is not to jump straight to a product discussion. It is to slow down and uncover the work behind the request.

You might ask:
- "What part of contract review is creating the most friction today?"
- "Who is doing that work, and what are they spending time on?"
- "Where do delays, inconsistencies, or escalations happen?"
- "What would improve if that part of the workflow worked better?"
- "Who owns the process and would need to validate a better approach?"

As the discussion continues, the underlying challenge becomes clearer.

The legal team explains that contract volumes have increased significantly over the past year. Reviewers spend hours identifying key clauses across different contract types. Similar contracts are sometimes assessed differently by different team members. As a result, turnaround times have increased, and business stakeholders are frustrated by delays.

The customer is not simply asking for "AI for contract review." They are describing a workflow problem:

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| Workflow | Reviewers identify and assess clauses across contract types. |
| Pain | Reviews take too long, and similar contracts are assessed inconsistently. |
| Owner | The Head of Legal Operations owns the process. |
| Reason to act | Delays are affecting business stakeholders, and the team wants more consistent review. |
| Boundary | Human oversight still matters because legal decisions require review and accountability. |

This is stronger than the original AI request because there is something concrete to investigate.

However, the opportunity is not fully qualified yet. The seller still needs to clarify success measures, approval stakeholders, sample contract availability, review criteria, and what validation step the customer will commit to next.

The lesson is simple: when a customer names a solution area, do not stop at the label. Translate the request into opportunity evidence by uncovering the workflow, pain, owner, business reason, oversight boundary, and next validation step.

## Slide 13/37 - Introduction (Module: Understanding opportunity readiness)

Once a problem starts to take shape, the next question is how ready the opportunity is. Lead, Stage 0, and Stage 1 help you describe that readiness without pretending every detail is already known.

## Slide 14/37 - Lead, Stage 0, and Stage 1

Early opportunities become stronger as evidence becomes clearer. A simple way to understand this progression is: Lead ✓, Stage 0 ✓, Stage 1 ✓ (all three "click to complete" items clicked).

The diagram below shows how early AI conversations progress as signals turn into evidence and evidence builds confidence.

Use these stages to understand how much confidence you have in the opportunity.

| Opportunity state | What it means | What is usually known |
|---|---|---|
| Lead | The earliest indication that a potential AI opportunity may exist. | There is customer interest or a possible problem area, but the opportunity is not yet clear. |
| Stage 0 | Structured validation of whether the opportunity is real and ready to progress. | The problem is becoming more specific, a plausible use case is emerging, and stakeholders or next steps are becoming visible. |
| Stage 1 | The opportunity has enough evidence to justify deeper joint work. | The customer problem, relevant stakeholders, reason AI may be worth exploring, and justified next steps are clear enough to support structured opportunity development. |

A Lead can come from a customer conversation, event, referral, outreach, or inbound interest. The table gives you the basic meaning of each stage. In practice, the important question is not "How excited is the customer?" It is "How much evidence do we have?"

A Lead may be worth exploring, but it usually needs more discovery before you can judge whether there is a real opportunity.

Stage 0 means enough evidence is emerging to justify structured validation, but the opportunity still needs clarification.

Stage 1 means there is enough evidence to justify deeper qualification and joint work, even though the solution, ROI, architecture, and commercial path are not finalized.

Use the stages as a confidence check. When you hear a broad AI request, ask: What do we know? What is still missing? What next step would build confidence? Progression is based on evidence, not enthusiasm.

**What you should practice:** When you hear a broad request, translate it into evidence categories:
- What workflow is affected?
- What friction or pain is visible?
- Who owns or experiences the problem?
- What use-case direction is emerging?
- Why would improvement matter?
- What customer commitment or validation step is still needed?

In these examples, the stronger signals are not the AI labels. They are the workflow details behind the labels. Your job is to capture those details clearly enough that the next qualification action is obvious.

## Slide 15/37 - What evidence builds confidence?

As opportunities progress, you are looking for evidence that answers a few practical questions:
- Is the customer problem clear?
- Is the workflow understood?
- Are relevant stakeholders visible?
- Is there a plausible direction to explore?
- Has the customer committed to a meaningful next step?

No opportunity begins with every answer. Stronger opportunities simply make these questions easier to answer.

For example, a Lead might only tell you that a customer is interested in AI for customer support.

Stage 0 evidence might show that support agents spend too much time searching across knowledge sources, that a support operations manager owns the workflow, and that the customer is willing to share sample tickets.

Stage 1 readiness may be reached when the customer has agreed to a structured discovery session with the right stakeholders and there is enough evidence to explain why the problem matters.

## Slide 16/37 - What Stage 1 really means

Stage 1 is sometimes misunderstood. It does not mean the opportunity is complete, and that:
- The solution is finalized.
- The architecture is known.
- ROI has been proven.
- Commercial terms are agreed.
- The deal is committed.

Stage 1 means there is enough confidence to explain:
- The customer problem.
- The workflow involved.
- The stakeholders involved.
- Why AI may be worth exploring.
- What step should happen next.

Some opportunities may be conditionally ready. Conditionally ready means the opportunity has enough evidence to justify deeper qualification, but one or more areas still need validation. For example, the workflow problem and owner may be visible, while success measures, the approval path, stakeholder alignment, or validation scope still need clarification.

This is common. Opportunities progress when there is enough evidence to support the next step, not when every detail is fully understood.

## Slide 19/37 - Introduction (Module: The evidence that makes an opportunity stronger)

Opportunity judgment becomes easier when you know what to listen for. This module breaks "good evidence" into practical signals you can recognize in customer conversations.

## Slide 20/37 - Business problem clarity

Business problem clarity exists when a customer can describe a specific operational challenge that is important enough to investigate.

Many conversations start with broad statements such as: "We need to be more efficient."

While that may be true, it does not tell us what is actually happening, who is affected, or where improvement is needed.

A stronger description sounds more like: "Our analysts spend several hours per case reviewing documents and drafting summary memos. The work is creating a backlog and slowing down audit preparation."

This version is more useful because it describes a real workflow. We can see who is involved, what they are doing, and why it matters to the business.

When exploring an opportunity, try to understand:
- What is happening today?
- Who is affected by the problem?
- What is the impact on time, cost, risk, speed, quality, or customer experience?
- What would a better outcome look like?

The goal is not to force customers to have all the answers immediately. It is to move from a general desire for improvement to a shared understanding of the problem that needs solving.

This matters because opportunities are easier to evaluate when everyone is discussing the same business challenge. Without problem clarity, conversations often drift into product demonstrations, feature discussions, or broad AI brainstorming before there is a clear reason for change.

## Slide 21/37 - Stakeholder visibility

Stakeholder visibility means understanding who is connected to the problem and who may influence whether the opportunity can move forward.

At an early stage, you do not need a complete org chart or a full list of decision makers. But you do need enough clarity to show that the problem is real, relevant, and possible to validate.

Start by looking for the people closest to the work. These are the operators who experience the pain day to day. They can explain what is happening now, where the friction appears, and how it affects the workflow.

Then look for the person who owns the outcome. This might be a team lead, department head, process owner, or business sponsor. They are usually the person who cares about the result, such as reducing turnaround time, improving quality, managing risk, or lowering cost.

It is also important to understand who else may influence the opportunity. Teams such as IT, security, procurement, legal, and compliance may not own the problem directly, but they can affect whether a solution can be validated, approved, or adopted.

Interest from one curious contact is useful, but it is not always enough. If that contact does not experience the pain, own the business outcome, or have a path to the people who do, the opportunity may struggle to progress.

A helpful question to keep in mind is: "Who cares enough about this problem to help validate it?"

## Slide 22/37 - Use-case direction

Use-case direction means the customer has described what work could improve, not just what tool they want to use.

At this stage, you do not need to design the final use case. You only need a plausible direction that connects AI to a workflow improvement.

Look for:
- The workflow step AI may improve
- The output that changes
- Why AI is appropriate
- How the use case connects to a business need

Use business language. For example, "AI-assisted account preparation" is clearer than "a sales copilot" if you can explain that account teams spend time gathering CRM notes, call summaries, account plans, and relevant context before renewal conversations.

The label may still be useful later, but the opportunity becomes stronger when the work itself is clear.

## Slide 23/37 - Customer commitment

Customer commitment means the customer has shown credible willingness to keep moving.

Positive sentiment is not enough. A customer saying "this sounds interesting" is helpful, but it does not show whether they will invest time, involve stakeholders, or provide the context needed to validate the opportunity.

Look for stronger signals such as:
- A scheduled follow-up with a clear purpose
- Agreement to bring in additional stakeholders
- Agreement to share workflow, data, or process detail
- A defined workshop, validation session, or pilot discussion

A credible next step is often one of the strongest signs that the opportunity is real. It shows the customer is willing to move beyond curiosity.

A weak next step sounds like: "Let's reconnect sometime."

A stronger next step sounds like: "The support operations manager will bring two team leads to a workflow review next Thursday and share sample ticket categories beforehand."

## Slide 24/37 - A reason the improvement matters

A strong opportunity includes a clear reason why solving the problem would matter to the business.

At this stage, you do not need a detailed business case, ROI calculation, or executive approval. What you do need is a credible explanation of how improving the workflow could lead to a better outcome.

A useful way to think about this is as a simple chain (all three "click to complete" items clicked):
1. The workflow problem ✓
2. The expected improvement ✓
3. The business outcome ✓

**Example:** Imagine a customer explaining that support agents spend a significant amount of time searching across multiple systems to find policy information.

The opportunity is not simply that AI could answer questions. The opportunity is that faster access to information could help agents resolve customer issues more efficiently.

If that happens, the business may see fewer avoidable escalations, more consistent responses, and a better customer experience.

Notice that this is not a final value claim. Nobody is promising a specific result yet. Instead, it provides a logical reason to continue validating the opportunity and testing whether the expected improvement is realistic.

A weak value story sounds like: "AI would be cool."

A stronger value story sounds like: "This could reduce a known source of effort, improve a measurable outcome, or lower a recognized business risk."

Don't focus on proving the value immediately. The goal is to establish a believable connection between the problem being experienced today and the outcome the organization hopes to achieve.

## Slide 25/37 - Real-world example

Imagine you are speaking with two different customers about AI opportunities.

The first customer says: "We want to explore AI across the business."

This is a common starting point. It tells you the organization is interested in AI, but it does not tell you what problem they are trying to solve. There is no clear workflow, no obvious source of friction, and no indication of who owns the challenge.

At this stage, there is interest, but very little evidence of a specific opportunity.

A useful seller response is not to jump straight to a product discussion. It is to slow down and uncover the work behind the request.

Now consider a different conversation. A customer explains that their support team spends a significant amount of time searching across multiple knowledge bases before responding to customer queries. Agents often need to check several systems to find the right answer, which slows response times and sometimes leads to inconsistent guidance being provided to customers. The team wants to explore whether AI could help retrieve and synthesize information more effectively, improving response consistency and reducing avoidable escalations.

The same pattern appears in many organizations.

For example, an HR leader might initially say: "We need an AI chatbot." After further discussion, it becomes clear that the HR team spends a large amount of time answering the same onboarding questions from new employees. The team wants to understand whether AI could provide consistent answers to common questions, allowing HR staff to focus on more complex employee needs.

For example, a sales leader might initially say: "We want AI for sales." As the conversation develops, they explain that account teams spend hours gathering information from CRM records, call notes, and account plans before renewal meetings. They are interested in whether AI could help bring relevant information together more efficiently, improving preparation quality while reducing manual effort.

A compliance team may also start with a broad request: "We need AI for compliance." Further discussion reveals that reviewers manually compare documents against policy requirements and spend considerable time identifying exceptions. They want to explore whether parts of the review process could be supported while maintaining appropriate human oversight.

These examples show how broad AI requests become more useful when the underlying work is clearer. They are not fully qualified yet, but they give the seller something specific to investigate next.

## Slide 28/37 - Introduction (Module: Applying opportunity judgment)

Now it is time to apply the evidence. You will compare opportunities that are strong, still developing, or not ready yet, and decide what action each one supports.

## Slide 29/37 - Strong opportunities

A strong opportunity has enough clarity to support a confident next step.

They often show several of the following indicators:
- A clear workflow problem.
- Visible ownership.
- A plausible use-case direction.
- Customer commitment to a next step.
- A credible reason the improvement matters.

A strong opportunity does not need to be perfect. It does not need final solution design, complete financial modeling, or full deployment planning. It needs enough credible evidence to justify the next qualification step.

A strong opportunity story might sound like this: "The risk operations team has a document-review backlog. Analysts spend several hours per case reviewing documents and preparing summary memos. The Head of Risk Operations owns the process and has agreed to a workflow discovery session with sample cases. The likely value is reduced review effort and improved consistency, but baseline review time and quality expectations still need validation."

This is strong because someone who was not in the meeting can understand the problem, the workflow, the owner, the reason to act, and what should happen next.

## Slide 30/37 - Opportunities that need more evidence

Many good opportunities begin as developing opportunities.

A developing opportunity shows promise, but one or more important pieces of evidence still need to be clarified before the opportunity can move forward confidently.

Common gaps include:

- A broad or vague problem statement.
- Incomplete stakeholder visibility
- An unclear use-case direction.
- A weak next-step commitment.
- An undeveloped value story.

For example:

"We think AI could help improve customer service."

This may be a real opportunity, but the statement is still broad. You would need to clarify which customer service workflow is affected, who owns it, what friction exists today, what outcome matters, and whether the customer is willing to take a concrete next step.

Some developing opportunities may be conditionally ready for a structured next step.

This usually happens when the core problem, likely owner, and next validation action are clear, even though some details still need work.

The practical question is: "Do we know enough to justify deeper qualification, and do we know exactly what still needs to be validated?"

## Slide 31/37 - Opportunities that are not ready

Some opportunities require significantly more validation before they should progress.

"Not ready" does not mean "not valuable." It means the current evidence is too weak to justify deeper qualification or partner and OpenAI investment.

Watch for these red flags:

| Red flag area | What it may sound like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear problem definition | "We want to do something with AI." | The problem is vague, and the partner may end up defining the customer's problem for them. |
| Workflow uncertainty | "We need an AI assistant." | The label does not explain which workflow changes, who uses it, or what output improves. |
| Unclear ownership | "One contact is interested, but they do not own the process." | The opportunity may stall if no operator, outcome owner, or sponsor path is visible. |
| Sensitive workflow without boundaries | "We want AI for compliance decisions." | The workflow may need clear ownership, oversight, approval paths, and validation boundaries before it can progress. |
| No customer commitment | "Let's check back later." | Without a credible next step, there may not be enough engagement to move forward. |
| Value is implied but not stated | "AI should improve the business somehow." | The opportunity lacks a testable reason to act. |
| First use case is too broad or risky | "We want AI to transform the whole compliance process." | Early validation needs a bounded workflow, owner, and approval path. |
| Stakeholders cannot support validation | "No one can join a discovery session or share workflow details." | Without participation, the opportunity cannot build credible evidence. |

The right action is to gather better evidence; clarify the workflow, identify the owner, define the next step, and document what is still unknown.

## Slide 32/37 - Real-world example

Imagine three different customer conversations. All three customers express interest in AI, but the amount of evidence behind the opportunity is very different.

In the first conversation, a customer says:

"We want to explore AI across the enterprise."

This shows interest, but little else is known. No specific workflow has been identified, no business problem has been described, and no stakeholder has emerged as the owner of a particular outcome. There is also no agreed next step.

At this stage, it is difficult to know where to focus or how to evaluate the opportunity.

The most useful next action would be discovery conversations to identify a specific problem area worth exploring further.

In the second conversation, a customer says:

"We think AI could help improve customer service."

As the discussion continues, more detail emerges. The customer explains that support agents spend a long time searching for information and that customers sometimes receive inconsistent responses.

A Support Operations Manager is involved in the discussion, and there is a general understanding of the outcomes the team hopes to achieve.

This opportunity is beginning to take shape. There is a visible workflow, a recognized source of friction, and a stakeholder connected to the problem. However, important questions remain unanswered.

How will success be measured? Who else needs to be involved? What is the next validation step?

The opportunity is moving in the right direction, but additional evidence is still needed.

In the third conversation, a customer explains:

"Our risk analysts spend several hours reviewing documents and preparing summary memos. We'd like to explore ways to reduce review effort while maintaining quality and oversight."

This conversation provides much stronger evidence. The workflow is clearly defined, the source of effort is understood, and a relevant process owner is involved.

The desired outcome is clear:

Reduce manual review effort without compromising quality. The customer has also agreed to a workflow discovery session to explore the opportunity in more detail.

At this point, there is enough information to support deeper qualification and opportunity development.

The same level of customer interest can point to very different readiness decisions. What matters is whether the evidence supports exploration, further validation, or deeper qualification.

## Slide 36/37 - Recap

General AI interest is a starting point, not the opportunity. A stronger opportunity appears when that interest connects to a real workflow problem, visible ownership, a plausible use-case direction, customer commitment, and a credible reason to act.

Use Lead, Stage 0, and Stage 1 as an evidence check. Readiness is not based on having every detail finalized. It is based on whether there is enough evidence to decide if the signal should stay in early exploration, move into structured validation, or support deeper qualification.
