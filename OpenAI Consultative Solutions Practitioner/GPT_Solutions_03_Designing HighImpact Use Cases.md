# Designing High-Impact Use Cases

## Slide 1/36 - Title

Designing High-Impact Use Cases

Created July 2026

## Slide 2/36 - Introduction

Discovery helps you understand the customer's situation. Use-case design helps you decide where AI could support a meaningful workflow improvement.

Customers often describe several challenges during discovery. They could be manual work, delays, repetitive tasks, inconsistent outputs, or difficulty finding information. Each of these may point to more than one possible use case.

This course helps you turn discovery findings into practical use-case opportunities. You will learn how to look across a workflow, identify where AI could support a specific improvement, and decide which candidate use case is worth validating first.

## Slide 3/36 - What you'll learn

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

- Explain the role of use-case design in opportunity development.
- Translate workflow problems into clear use-case opportunities.
- Identify where AI can create value within a workflow.
- Evaluate candidate use cases based on impact, practicality, and readiness for validation.
- Prioritize candidate use cases and recommend a strong starting point.
- Define success signals that can support future validation and value discussions.

Let's get started!

## Slide 4/36 - Turning problems into opportunities

A strong use case starts with a real workflow problem. It describes the part of the work that could improve, the people involved, what AI would help produce or change, what humans still review or decide, and the outcome the customer wants to achieve.

This matters because vague ideas are hard to evaluate. "AI for onboarding" or "AI for support" might sound promising, but those labels do not tell you what work changes, who benefits, or how the customer would validate improvement.

Clear use cases make later conversations stronger because they keep value, validation, and solution discussions grounded in real work rather than broad AI ambition.

## Slide 5/36 - Introduction (Module: Translating discovery into use cases)

A customer problem can point in more than one direction. The next step is to make the opportunity specific enough to discuss, test, and improve.

Use-case design starts by staying close to the work: where it happens, who is involved, what AI could help produce, and what people still need to review or decide.

## Slide 6/36 - From workflow problems to use cases

Discovery findings often reveal what is difficult, slow, inconsistent, risky, or expensive in a customer's current workflow.

Use-case design takes the next step. It asks: "Where could AI support a specific improvement in that workflow?"

A problem statement describes what is happening today.

A use-case statement describes the workflow improvement AI may support.

For example:

| Discovery finding | Stronger problem statement | Stronger use-case statement |
|---|---|---|
| "Onboarding is too manual." | "HR coordinators manually review submitted documents, chase missing information, and track onboarding tasks across teams." | "AI helps HR coordinators extract required fields from onboarding documents and flag missing information for human review, reducing manual review effort and improving readiness tracking." |

The use-case statement is stronger because it explains the work, the user, the AI-supported output, the human role, and the intended improvement.

This matters because vague use cases are hard to evaluate. "AI for onboarding" could mean document extraction, policy retrieval, learning-plan generation, task monitoring, manager reminders, or employee guidance.

Each is a different opportunity with different evidence needs, controls, and success signals.

A useful use case stays connected to the workflow problem. It should not jump straight to a product label, interface, or feature.

## Slide 7/36 - Using workflow understanding to identify opportunities

Customers often describe the pain they feel most directly. A deeper look at the workflow can reveal additional opportunities they have not yet named.

When you examine a workflow, look for places where people:

- Search across multiple sources before they can act.
- Copy information from documents, emails, transcripts, forms, or tickets.
- Sort, classify, prioritize, or route work.
- Draft messages, summaries, plans, reports, or other artifacts.
- Review work for quality, completeness, risk, or readiness.
- Make decisions with incomplete or scattered context.
- Coordinate handoffs or monitor status across teams.

These are not product requirements. They are work patterns. They help you see where AI could support a process step.

For example, a customer might say, "Our employee onboarding process is slow." A workflow discussion may reveal several possible improvement points: document review, policy lookup, task coordination, manager notifications, and status monitoring.

That does not mean every point should become a use case. It means you now have a more precise view of where AI might create value.

Strong use-case design starts with the work itself. The better you understand the workflow, the easier it becomes to identify both obvious and hidden opportunities for improvement.

## Slide 8/36 - Clarify what the AI would do

Many workflow problems can generate several candidate use cases. To separate them, ask one simple question:

"What would the AI actually do for the user at this point in the workflow?"

This question keeps the use case specific. It prevents broad statements like "AI will assist the team" or "AI will improve the process."

The answer often reveals the type of support the workflow needs:

| AI support type | What the AI helps produce or change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Information retrieval | A grounded answer, summary, comparison, or synthesis | Find relevant policy guidance across approved sources |
| Extraction | Structured fields or facts from unstructured inputs | Extract required fields from submitted forms |
| Classification or routing | A label, category, queue, priority, or routing suggestion | Classify support tickets by issue type |
| Generation | A draft, message, summary, plan, or report | Draft a first-pass customer response |
| Review | Findings, flags, quality checks, or completeness checks | Review a draft summary for missing required information |
| Recommendation | A suggested next action or ranked option | Recommend which follow-up action a manager should review |
| Coordination or monitoring | Status updates, exceptions, or alerts | Flag onboarding tasks that are delayed or incomplete |

Use this as a clarification lens, not as a technical taxonomy.

You are not selecting the final product or implementation path yet. You are clarifying the job AI would perform inside the business process.

That distinction helps you avoid collapsing different use cases into one vague idea.

A customer-support workflow might include information retrieval, response drafting, quality review, escalation recommendations, and workflow monitoring.

Those are related, but they are not the same use case.

## Slide 9/36 - Framing a use case

A strong use case explains the workflow improvement in business terms.

Use this simple framing approach:

"At [workflow step], [user or stakeholder] uses AI to [AI-supported output] for [work object], while [human role] [reviews, approves, decides, or acts], so [desired outcome]."

Each part has a purpose:

| Use-case element | What to capture | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Workflow step | Where the use case appears in the process | Keeps the use case grounded in real work |
| User or stakeholder | Who uses, reviews, or benefits from the output | Clarifies ownership and adoption needs |
| Work object | The item moving through the workflow | Makes the opportunity concrete |
| AI-supported output | What the AI returns, changes, or prepares | Clarifies what the use case actually does |
| Human role | What people still review, approve, decide, or act on | Supports responsible workflow design |
| Desired outcome | What should improve | Connects the use case to business value |

Here is an example:

"At the onboarding document-review step, HR coordinators use AI to extract required fields and flag missing information from submitted documents, while coordinators review exceptions and confirm accuracy, so new-hire readiness can be tracked more consistently."

This is clearer than "AI for onboarding." It tells stakeholders what work changes and what remains under human oversight.

## Slide 10/36 - Real-world example

A customer explains that employee onboarding is taking too long.

At first, this sounds like one broad problem: onboarding is slow.

But discovery shows that the delay is not caused by one single issue. It is the result of several points of friction across the workflow.

Employees submit information through multiple channels, which makes it harder for HR to check whether everything has been received.

HR teams then spend time reviewing and validating that information manually. Managers often wait for onboarding tasks to be completed before new employees can begin productive work.

At the same time, status updates are difficult to track across teams, so delays are not always visible until they have already affected the start date.

Once the workflow is clearer, several possible use cases begin to emerge.

One use case could focus on information extraction. AI could help pull key details from onboarding documents and prepare structured fields for HR review.

In this case, HR would still review the information and resolve exceptions, but the manual effort of finding and organizing the information could be reduced.

Another use case could focus on knowledge retrieval.

AI could help onboarding coordinators find the right policy, requirement, or process guidance more quickly. HR would still confirm that the guidance is appropriate before using it, but the team could spend less time searching across documents and systems.

A third use case could focus on workflow monitoring.

AI could help track onboarding progress and flag delayed or incomplete activities. Coordinators would still decide what action to take, but they would have better visibility into where the process is slowing down.

This example shows why use-case design depends on workflow understanding. The same workflow problem can create several valid AI opportunities, but each one changes a different part of the work.

**What you should notice**

The seller should not treat "onboarding is slow" as a single AI requirement. They should identify:

- The workflow: Employee onboarding across HR, managers, and related teams.
- The friction points: Multiple submission channels, manual validation, manager waiting time, and limited status visibility.
- The candidate use cases: Information extraction, knowledge retrieval, and workflow monitoring.
- The human role: HR coordinators still review, confirm, and act on the information.
- The next judgment: Which candidate use case is most useful and practical to validate first.

A strong use case is not just "AI for onboarding." It is a specific AI-supported improvement at a specific point in the workflow.

## Slide 13/36 - Introduction (Module: Evaluating candidate use cases)

Once several use cases are visible, the question becomes whether each one is worth pursuing. Evaluation helps you separate ideas that sound interesting from opportunities that are meaningful, practical, and ready enough to validate.

## Slide 14/36 - Does the use case solve a meaningful problem?

Not every use case is worth pursuing first.

Some ideas are interesting but too small. Others are valuable in theory but disconnected from the problem the customer cares about most.

Strong use cases address workflow challenges that matter to stakeholders and support outcomes the customer wants to improve.

To assess whether a use case solves a meaningful problem, ask:

- Does it address a clear workflow friction?
- Would it reduce manual effort, delay, rework, inconsistency, or risk?
- Is the affected work frequent, important, or painful enough to matter?
- Does a stakeholder care about the improvement?
- Could the improvement connect to a business outcome later?

Keep this discussion qualitative and evidence-based. At this stage, you are not building an ROI model or business case.

You are deciding whether the use case is meaningful enough to validate.

For example, a use case that saves a few minutes in an occasional task may be useful, but it may not be the strongest starting point.

A use case that reduces repeated manual review in a high-volume workflow may be more meaningful, especially if stakeholders already recognize the pain.

Impact should be evaluated in the context of the customer's workflow—not the sophistication of the technology.

## Slide 15/36 - Can the use case realistically be validated?

A strong use case should be practical to evaluate. This is where feasibility comes in.

In this course, feasibility means a practical business consideration: can the customer and partner realistically validate whether the use case improves the workflow?

It is not a technical architecture assessment.

| Factor | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Workflow clarity | The process step is understood well enough to evaluate. |
| Stakeholder engagement | The right people are involved and willing to participate. |
| Process maturity | The process is stable enough to compare current and improved ways of working. |
| Information availability | Required inputs, examples, or source materials can be accessed and reviewed appropriately. |
| Data readiness | Inputs are available, representative, and permissioned for validation. |
| Risk and control | Errors are reversible or manageable, and human review or approval is realistic. |
| Measurement clarity | The customer can observe whether the workflow improved. |
| Scope and complexity | Validation does not require excessive dependencies, enterprise-wide redesign, or unclear ownership. |

A use case can be high-impact but difficult to validate.

For example, a workflow that affects customer outcomes, compliance-sensitive decisions, or system actions might require more stakeholder confidence, oversight, and validation planning.

OpenAI's current Usage Policies prohibit automating high-stakes decisions in sensitive areas without human review. For use cases in areas such as insurance, legal, medical, employment, or financial activities and credit, AI may support analysis or recommendations, but the workflow must include qualified human review, and people remain accountable for the decision.

That does not mean the use case is bad. It's just not the best first use case.

## Slide 16/36 - Is this a viable candidate for validation?

A viable candidate is a use case that has enough promise and enough readiness to justify the next validation step.

The best starting use case is not always the most ambitious one.

Some opportunities offer significant long-term potential but require substantial workflow change, stakeholder alignment, data readiness, or risk controls before they can be pursued successfully.

When judging viability, consider:

- Are stakeholders ready to participate in validation?
- Is there organizational appetite for changing the workflow?
- Can the customer support validation activities with examples, users, or process details?
- Does the use case fit current workflow-improvement priorities?
- Are expectations realistic?
- Can the use case generate useful evidence quickly?

AI maturity also matters, but only as a selection consideration.

In this context, AI maturity means how ready the organization is to validate and adopt AI-supported workflow changes. It is not a formal maturity assessment.

A customer with limited AI experience may be better served by a use case with clear human review, observable outputs, and a narrow validation scope.

A customer with stronger AI experience and mature governance may be ready to validate a more complex workflow.

The practical question is:

"Does this use case offer enough value, practicality, and readiness to be worth validating next?"

## Slide 17/36 - Real-world example

Discovery reveals several challenges within a claims-processing workflow.

Claims handlers spend significant time reviewing submitted documents, prioritizing incoming claims, and validating information before work can progress.

At first, these challenges sound like one general problem: claims processing is too slow. But when the workflow is examined more closely, different use-case opportunities begin to appear.

One use case could focus on extracting claim details from submitted documents.

AI could help identify key information and prepare it in a more structured format for human review. This may reduce manual data entry and improve consistency because the input and output are relatively clear.

It may also be easier to validate because reviewers can compare extracted information against the original documents.

A second use case could focus on classifying claims by urgency and routing them to the appropriate queue.

This could improve prioritization and reduce delays for urgent cases. However, it may require more careful validation because routing can affect customer experience and operational outcomes.

Stakeholders would need confidence that the classification logic is reliable, explainable, and reviewed appropriately.

A third use case could focus on reviewing claim summaries for completeness.

AI could help check whether key information appears to be missing before a reviewer moves the claim forward. This may improve quality and reduce rework later in the process, while keeping human reviewers directly involved.

If the customer's main concern is inconsistent review quality, this may be a practical starting point.

The diagram below shows how one claims-processing workflow can reveal several use cases, each with different benefits and validation needs.

Each of the use cases may be helpful. The question is which one is most suitable for validation.

The extraction use case may be easier to validate because the task is clearer and the result can be checked against source documents.

The routing use case may be more sensitive because it could affect prioritization and customer experience.

The completeness-review use case may be a strong option when the customer wants to improve quality while keeping reviewers in control.

Because insurance is a sensitive high-stakes area, AI may support extraction, completeness checks, analysis, or routing recommendations, but any output that could affect a person's claim must receive qualified human review. The responsible claims professionals remain accountable for the decision under OpenAI's current Usage Policies.

**What you should notice**

This example shows that candidate use cases can all be valuable, but not equally suitable as a first validation step. When comparing options, you should ask:

- Which use case addresses the clearest workflow friction?
- Which output can reviewers check most easily?
- Which option keeps the human role clear?
- Which option introduces higher customer-impact, routing, or oversight risk?
- Which option can produce useful validation evidence fastest?

The lesson is not to select the most ambitious use case by default. The stronger starting point is often the one that is specific, observable, reviewable, and credible enough to validate with the customer.

Use this evaluation before comparing options against each other.

## Slide 20/36 - Introduction (Module: Prioritizing use cases)

Prioritization is where use-case design becomes a recommendation. The aim is to choose a starting point the customer can act on—not the biggest idea, the most advanced idea, or every possible idea at once.

## Slide 21/36 - Comparing candidate use cases

Once candidate use cases have passed the viability test, the next step is to compare them side by side.

Evaluation asks:

"Is this candidate use case meaningful and realistic enough to consider?"

Prioritization asks:

"Of the viable candidates, which should we recommend as the strongest starting point?"

This distinction matters because discovery often uncovers several useful opportunities. The customer still needs a practical place to start, and the strongest starting point is not always the biggest or most ambitious idea.

When comparing viable use cases, focus on the trade-off between:

- Expected business improvement
- Validation practicality
- Stakeholder support
- Speed to useful evidence
- Risk and control needs
- Readiness to expand later

A strong starting point creates credible evidence quickly while keeping the validation scope focused.

## Slide 22/36 - Compare opportunities consistently

When several candidate use cases appear promising, it can be helpful to compare them side by side.

The goal is not to create a formal scorecard or assign numerical ratings.

Instead, use a simple comparison to create a structured discussion and help both the partner and customer evaluate the options consistently.

| Comparison factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Business value | What improvement could this create for the customer? |
| Workflow readiness | Is the workflow understood well enough to validate? |
| Data readiness | Are required inputs available, representative, and permissioned? |
| Risk and control | What could go wrong, and can human review or oversight manage it? |
| Stakeholder support | Who cares about this use case, and are they willing to engage? |
| Measurement clarity | Can the customer observe whether the workflow improved? |
| Expansion potential | Could evidence from this use case support broader adoption later? |

Use short notes rather than detailed scoring.

For example:

- "Strong stakeholder owner; data access still needs validation."
- "High impact, but routing rules and oversight are unresolved."
- "Narrow scope, clear measurement, and visible workflow pain."

This keeps the conversation focused on practical trade-offs rather than abstract preferences.

## Slide 23/36 - Selecting a recommended starting point

Strong starting use cases are often:

- Clearly connected to a workflow problem.
- Easier to validate.
- Supported by visible stakeholders.
- Associated with measurable outcomes.
- Capable of producing useful evidence quickly.

A recommended starting point should balance impact, practicality, and readiness.

This matters because early success can create the confidence, stakeholder support, and evidence needed to explore broader opportunities later.

A smaller but well-chosen use case may be better than a broad transformation idea that cannot be validated.

A strong recommendation explains both what to start with and why:

"We recommend starting with knowledge retrieval and synthesis for support agents because it directly addresses the main search friction, keeps agents in control of customer responses, can be measured through search time and response consistency, and may create evidence for later improvements in quality review or escalation support."

This is stronger than simply saying, "Start with knowledge retrieval." It helps the customer understand why that use case was prioritized and what trade-offs were considered when comparing it to other options.

## Slide 24/36 - Real-world example

A customer-support organization explains that agents spend significant time searching across multiple systems before responding to customer questions.

During discovery, it becomes clear that the problem affects both efficiency and consistency.

Agents spend time looking for information, responses can vary between team members, and customers may wait longer while agents locate the right answer.

As the workflow is explored further, three candidate use cases emerge.

| Candidate use case | Potential benefits | Considerations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summarize support interactions | Reduces manual note-taking; improves record consistency; saves administrative effort | Useful, but it does not directly address the main search challenge |
| Retrieve and synthesize policy information | Helps agents find and synthesize information from approved knowledge sources; improves consistency; reduces search effort | Requires access to trusted knowledge sources; success measures should be agreed before validation |
| Recommend escalation actions | May improve consistency of escalation decisions; could reduce delays in complex cases | Greater risk and oversight requirements; additional stakeholder review may be needed; validation may be more complex |

At first glance, all three use cases appear valuable.

However, use-case prioritization is not about choosing every worthwhile idea. It is about identifying the best place to start.

To do that, we can return to the original workflow problem.

The customer's primary source of friction is that agents spend too much time searching for information before they can respond to customers. That means the strongest starting point is often the use case that addresses that problem most directly.

In this example, retrieving and synthesizing policy information is the strongest candidate.

It targets the workflow step where agents are currently losing time.

It helps agents find information more quickly and consistently, while still keeping people responsible for reviewing and using that information in customer responses.

It is also relatively straightforward to validate.

The customer can observe whether agents spend less time searching, whether responses are prepared more efficiently, and whether fewer issues arise because important information was difficult to find.

The other use cases may still be valuable.

Summarizing support interactions could reduce administrative effort, but it does not address the customer's main challenge.

If the goal is to improve response speed and reduce search effort, it is likely to have a smaller impact on the problem that motivated the opportunity.

Recommending escalation actions could potentially create significant value. However, it introduces additional considerations around oversight, stakeholder confidence, and validation.

Because escalation decisions can affect customer outcomes, stakeholders may require stronger evidence and governance before treating it as an appropriate starting point.

This does not mean the escalation use case is a poor idea. It may become a strong follow-on opportunity once the organization has gained confidence through earlier validation efforts.

The key lesson is that prioritization is not about selecting the most ambitious opportunity or the most advanced use case. It is about identifying the opportunity that offers the strongest balance of business value, practical validation, and readiness to move forward.

## Slide 27/36 - Introduction (Module: Defining success signals)

A selected use case still needs a way to show whether it is working. Success signals give the customer and partner shared language for what to observe during validation, before moving into deeper value or solution discussions.

## Slide 28/36 - What success signals are

Once a use case has been selected, the next question is:

"How will we know whether this is worth continuing to validate?"

That is where success signals help.

A success signal is an early indicator that a use case may be creating meaningful workflow improvement.

Success signals help customers and partners align on what to observe during validation.

They are not a value hypothesis, ROI claim, formal KPI model, or business case. Those come later.

At this stage, success signals help prepare future value conversations by making the expected improvement more concrete.

Strong success signals are:

- Relevant to the workflow problem being addressed.
- Meaningful to stakeholders.
- Observable during validation activities.
- Connected to desired business outcomes.

For example, if a support team is validating a knowledge-retrieval use case, "the model is advanced" is not a useful success signal. "Agents spend less time searching for policy answers" is more useful because it connects directly to the workflow problem.

## Slide 29/36 - Connect success signals to workflow outcomes

Success signals should relate directly to the workflow challenge identified during discovery.

Common success signals include:

- Reduced time spent on repetitive activities.
- Improved consistency of outputs.
- Faster access to information.
- Reduced backlog or delays.
- Improved decision quality.
- Fewer errors or rework activities.
- Positive user feedback about workflow fit.

The most useful success signals focus on workflow improvement rather than technology performance alone.

For each success signal, connect back to:

- The workflow step.
- The user or stakeholder.
- The AI-supported output.
- The human role.
- The desired outcome.

For example:

| Use case | Weak success signal | Stronger success signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI helps agents retrieve policy answers | "AI gives good responses." | "Agents can find relevant policy guidance faster and prepare customer responses with fewer avoidable escalations." |
| AI extracts fields from onboarding documents | "Extraction works." | "HR coordinators spend less time manually copying required fields and can identify missing information earlier." |
| AI reviews claim summaries for completeness | "The review is accurate." | "Reviewers identify missing information before cases move forward, reducing rework later in the process." |

A strong success signal makes the validation conversation easier because it gives stakeholders something concrete to look for.

## Slide 30/36 - Agree what should be validated

Different stakeholders may view success differently.

An affected user may care about reducing manual effort. A process owner may care about throughput, consistency, or backlog.

A governance stakeholder may care about human review, traceability, or appropriate oversight.

Before validation begins, align on what should be observed.

Ask:

- What improvement do stakeholders expect to see?
- Which outcomes matter most?
- What evidence would increase confidence in the use case?
- What should be observed during validation activities?
- Which stakeholder cares about each signal?

A simple validation statement can help:

"We will evaluate whether [use case] improves [workflow outcome] for [stakeholder] by observing [evidence]."

Example:

"We will evaluate whether policy retrieval and synthesis improves support response preparation for agents by observing search time, response consistency, avoidable escalations, and agent feedback."

This does not prove full value yet. It creates shared expectations and prepares the opportunity for a stronger value discussion later.

## Slide 31/36 - Real-world example

A customer-support organization has selected a knowledge-retrieval use case for further validation.

Discovery showed that support agents spend significant time searching across multiple policy documents and knowledge sources before responding to customer questions.

The partner asks:

"If this use case is successful, what would improve for the team?"

Stakeholders identify several desired outcomes:

Agents find information more quickly.

Responses become more consistent.

Escalations caused by missing information decrease.

New team members become productive more quickly.

The partner follows up:

"How would you know those improvements are happening?"

The discussion produces several success signals:

| Desired improvement | Success signal | Stakeholder who cares |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Faster information access | Less time spent searching for policy guidance | Support agents and support operations |
| Better response consistency | Fewer conflicting answers across similar customer questions | Support operations and quality leads |
| Fewer avoidable escalations | Fewer escalations caused by missing or hard-to-find information | Team leads and operations leaders |
| Better onboarding for new agents | New agents report that approved guidance is easier to find and use | Team leads and enablement owners |

These success signals come from the workflow problem and desired outcome—not from the technology itself.

That is the key. The customer and partner are not asking, "Did AI perform a task?" They are asking, "Did the workflow improve in a way stakeholders can observe and care about?"
