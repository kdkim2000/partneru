## Slide 4/43 - What discovery really means

Discovery is the process of understanding how work happens today so you can assess whether there is a real opportunity to explore.

Good discovery helps you uncover:

- Where friction exists.
- Who is affected.
- Who owns the outcome.
- Why the problem matters.
- What evidence supports deeper validation.
- What still needs to be clarified.

The goal is not to pitch a solution as quickly as possible. The goal is to understand the customer's current reality clearly enough to decide what should happen next.

## Slide 5/43 - Your role in the conversation

As a partner, your role is to create clarity.

Strong discovery helps the customer move from broad statements to specific evidence. Instead of accepting "we need AI" at face value, you help the customer explain the workflow, the pain, the people involved, the business impact, and the next validation step.

Effective discovery helps answer questions such as:

- What problem is the customer trying to solve?
- Where does the problem appear in the workflow?
- Who owns or experiences the problem?
- Why does the problem matter?
- What evidence supports deeper validation?
- What next step would improve understanding?

This course is about building that discovery discipline before the conversation moves into use-case design, value building, or solution mapping.

## Slide 6/43 - Introduction (Module: Discovering the customer problem and workflow pain)

Discovery becomes useful when a customer's AI request turns into a real workflow problem.

Start by slowing the conversation down: What work is happening today, where does it break down, and what evidence shows the pain is worth exploring?

## Slide 7/43 - Start with the customer problem, not the AI request

Customers often describe a desired AI solution before they have fully explained the business problem.

They might say:

"We need AI for operations."
"We want a chatbot."
"We need something to summarize documents."
"We want AI to help employees work faster."

These statements may be true, but they are not enough for strong discovery. They name an interest area or possible solution. They do not yet explain what is happening in the business today.

A strong discovery conversation starts by redirecting the conversation toward the problem behind the request.

A simple redirect question is:

"Before we talk about possible AI approaches, can we unpack the workflow problem you are trying to solve?"

This question matters because it keeps the conversation consultative. It signals that you are not there to force a product into the account. You are trying to understand the customer's work well enough to identify whether there is a real opportunity.

Good early discovery should clarify:

- What challenge the customer is trying to solve.
- Why does the problem matter?
- How the problem affects the business today.

For example:

"We need AI for operations" is too broad. A stronger discovery outcome would be: "Operations analysts spend significant time reviewing documents and creating summary reports before cases can move forward. This creates a backlog during peak periods and inconsistent output quality."

The difference is evidence. The first statement is an AI request. The second describes a workflow problem.

## Slide 8/43 - Map where the problem appears in the workflow

A workflow is the sequence of activities that turns an input into an output. It typically involves people, systems, decisions, handoffs, reviews, and approvals working together to complete a business task.

Understanding the workflow is important because business problems rarely affect every step equally.

Customers often describe the issue in broad terms, such as:

"The whole process is slow."

However, when you explore the workflow in more detail, the source of friction is often concentrated in one or two specific stages.

**Example**

Delays may occur when information is gathered, when documents are reviewed, when approvals are requested, or when employees need to search for answers across multiple systems.

Your goal is not to create a detailed process map or document every activity. Instead, you want to build enough understanding of the workflow to identify where the problem occurs, who is affected, and why it matters.

Once you can locate the friction within the workflow, it becomes much easier to understand the opportunity and determine whether further investigation is worthwhile.

The diagram below shows a simple way to think about workflow discovery. You are looking for the point where work becomes slow, inconsistent, expensive, risky, or difficult to complete.

The best way to identify the source of friction is to explore how the work actually gets done.

Useful workflow questions include:

- How is this work performed today?
- What happens first, and what happens next?
- Where do delays, bottlenecks, or inefficiencies occur?
- Which steps create the most friction?
- Who or what system does the work pass to?
- What output is produced at the end?

This matters because a broad problem like "our process is inefficient" is hard to validate. A workflow-specific problem is much easier to explore.

For example:

"Document review is slow" is broad.

"Analysts spend substantial time reading incoming documents, locating required information, and writing summary reports before a reviewer can approve the case" is more useful.

## Slide 9/43 - Identify evidence of pain

Discovery is not just about understanding the workflow. It is also about gathering evidence that the problem is important enough to solve.

At this stage, you are not trying to build a detailed business case or calculate a precise ROI.

Instead, you are looking for practical signs that the pain is real, recurring, and having a meaningful impact on the organization.

Evidence can appear in many forms.

The problem may be affecting:

- The amount of time people spend completing work.
- Costs caused by inefficiency, duplication, or rework.
- Quality, accuracy, or consistency of outcomes.
- Speed of execution or response times.
- Operational, regulatory, or business risk.
- Customer or employee experience.

The more specific the evidence becomes, the easier it is to understand whether the opportunity deserves deeper investigation.

For example, a customer might say:

"This takes too long."

While this suggests there may be a problem, it does not tell us enough about the impact.

A stronger description might be:

"During peak periods, review queues grow because analysts spend several hours per case manually reading documents and preparing summaries. Managers also struggle to see where work is backing up."

This version provides useful evidence. It highlights where effort is being spent, where delays are occurring, and how the problem affects both analysts and managers. It also gives us something concrete to explore further, such as review times, queue sizes, workflow visibility, consistency, and turnaround performance.

It is important not to overstate what the evidence tells us.

If a customer says a process "feels slow," avoid turning that into a specific time-saving claim without proof.

Instead, treat it as a discovery gap:

"The customer believes the workflow is slow, but baseline completion time still needs to be validated."

Good qualification separates what is known from what is assumed. Strong opportunities are built on evidence, while assumptions are treated as questions that still need to be answered.

## Slide 10/43 - Real-world example

A customer begins a meeting by saying:

"We need AI to improve our operations."

This is a common starting point. The customer is expressing interest in AI, but it is still unclear what part of the business needs improvement or what problem they are trying to solve.

Rather than jumping straight into potential AI solutions, the partner focuses on understanding the work itself.

They ask:

"Can you walk me through the part of operations you are hoping to improve?"

As the customer describes the process, a more detailed picture begins to emerge.

A team of analysts receives large volumes of documents that must be reviewed before work can move to the next stage.

Analysts spend considerable time reading information, identifying relevant details, and creating summary reports for downstream teams.

During busy periods, document volumes increase faster than the team can process them, causing review queues to grow.

As the conversation continues, several sources of friction become clear:

- Analysts spend significant time manually reviewing documents and creating summaries.
- Different analysts follow slightly different approaches, leading to inconsistent output quality.
- Review queues grow during peak periods, increasing turnaround times.
- Managers have limited visibility into backlog, workload distribution, and review status.

At this point, the conversation has changed significantly.

The problem is no longer described as "operations" in general. Instead, a specific workflow has been identified: document review and summarization. The sources of friction are visible, and the customer can explain how they affect day-to-day performance.

The customer and partner can now discuss the opportunity in much more practical terms.

- Could parts of the review process be accelerated?
- Could summaries be generated more consistently?
- Could managers gain better visibility into workload and backlog?
- Which activities require human judgment, and which tasks might be supported?

The opportunity can now be described more clearly:

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| Workflow | Document review and summarization. |
| Problem | Significant manual effort, inconsistent outputs, and growing backlogs. |
| Impact | Slower turnaround times and reduced operational efficiency. |
| Potential outcome | Faster reviews, improved consistency, and better visibility into workflow performance. |

None of these discussions would have been possible at the start of the conversation because the underlying workflow was not yet understood.

This does not mean the opportunity is fully qualified. There are still important questions to answer about stakeholders, success measures, business impact, operating context, and the next validation step.

However, the discussion has moved from a broad request for AI to a specific workflow problem supported by observable evidence.

That shift is the purpose of discovery. It turns general interest into something concrete enough to investigate, validate, and potentially develop into a real opportunity.

**What you should notice**

This example is useful because the partner does not stop at "AI for operations."

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| The workflow | Document review and summarization. |
| The pain | Manual review effort, inconsistent outputs, and growing backlogs. |
| The affected roles | Analysts and managers. |
| The business impact | Slower turnaround times and reduced operational efficiency. |
| The open questions | Stakeholders, success measures, business impact, and feasibility still need validation. |

The lesson is to translate broad AI language into workflow evidence. When a customer says "we need AI to improve operations," the next move is not to demo a solution. It is to ask questions that reveal the work, the friction, the affected people, and the evidence still needed to qualify the opportunity.

## Slide 13/43 - Introduction (Module: Discovering stakeholders and ownership)

Once the workflow is clearer, the next question is ownership. A real opportunity needs more than interest. It needs people who feel the pain, own the outcome, and can help validate what should change.

## Slide 14/43 - Why stakeholder discovery matters

Once a workflow problem begins to take shape, discovery needs to clarify who is connected to that problem.

Different stakeholders often experience the same workflow in different ways. One group may feel the day-to-day burden. Another group may own the business outcome. Another group may influence whether any future change can move forward.

Stakeholder discovery helps you understand:

- Who experiences the pain.
- Who owns the outcome.
- Who influences decisions.
- Who may need to be involved for validation.
- Who may care about adoption, approval, risk, or success measures.

This is an important step because opportunities become easier to validate when ownership is visible.

A single interested contact may help you start the conversation, but interest alone may not be enough to progress the opportunity. If that contact does not own the outcome, experience the pain, or have a path to the right stakeholders, the opportunity may stall.

The practical discovery question is:

"Who cares enough about this problem to help validate it?"

## Slide 15/43 - Identifying the outcome owner

An outcome owner is the person accountable for improving the workflow or business result.

The outcome owner may not be the person doing the work every day.

**Example**

Analysts may experience a review burden, while an operations leader owns turnaround time and quality. Support agents may feel the pain of searching for answers, while a support operations leader owns handle time, escalation rate, or customer experience.

Use discovery to clarify:

- Who owns the current process?
- Who is responsible for the result?
- Who would benefit if the problem were solved?
- Who can help define what "better" looks like?
- Who needs to be involved in a validation step?

Here is a simple way to separate common stakeholder roles:

| Stakeholder type | What they usually tell you | Why they matter |
|---|---|---|
| Affected users | What the work feels like day to day. | They reveal practical workflow friction. |
| Outcome owner | What business result needs to improve. | They help validate priority and success. |
| Influencers | What constraints, approvals, or risks may affect progress. | They help identify what could block or shape the opportunity. |
| Executive sponsor or senior stakeholder | Why the issue matters at a broader business level. | They can create urgency or visibility. |

You do not need a complete stakeholder map in the first conversation. You do need enough visibility to understand whether the problem has an accountable owner.

## Slide 16/43 - Understanding stakeholder perspectives

As you learn more about a workflow, it is important to remember that different stakeholders often care about different outcomes.

Even when everyone is discussing the same problem, they may view it through a different lens depending on their role and responsibilities.

For example, consider a document review workflow.

The analysts completing the work each day may be most concerned about the amount of manual effort required.

They want to spend less time on repetitive tasks and more time on activities that require expertise and judgment.

The managers responsible for the team may have a different perspective. They are often focused on consistency, productivity, and visibility.

They want to understand where work is getting stuck, how performance varies across the team, and whether service levels are being met.

Business leaders may be looking at a broader picture.

They may care about reducing turnaround times, lowering operational risk, improving customer outcomes, or increasing the organization's ability to scale.

At the same time, other stakeholders may have entirely different concerns:

- Operators may want less manual effort and fewer repetitive tasks.
- Managers may want greater consistency, visibility, and workflow control.
- Business leaders may want faster cycle times, lower risk, or improved business outcomes.
- IT, security, legal, and compliance teams may focus on data access, governance, approvals, oversight, and policy alignment.

These differences are not a problem. In fact, they are valuable discovery evidence.

They help you understand what success looks like from different perspectives and identify who may need to be involved as the opportunity develops.

Strong stakeholder discovery explores several areas:

- Operational concerns and day-to-day pain points.
- Business priorities and desired outcomes.
- Adoption considerations and change-management needs.
- Governance, security, legal, or approval requirements that may become important later.
- Success measures and how stakeholders will judge progress.

The goal at this stage is not to resolve every concern or align every stakeholder around a final solution. Instead, you are building a clearer picture of who is connected to the problem and what matters to each group.

This helps avoid a common discovery mistake: assuming that the first stakeholder you speak to represents the entire opportunity.

In reality, most meaningful opportunities involve multiple perspectives. The more complete your understanding of those perspectives, the easier it becomes to validate the problem, build alignment, and move the opportunity forward.

## Slide 17/43 - Real-world example

A customer explains that renewal preparation is inconsistent across account teams.
At first, the conversation focuses on the account managers who prepare for renewal calls.

The partner asks:

"Who is most affected when renewal preparation is inconsistent?"

The customer explains:

Account managers feel the day-to-day burden because they spend significant time gathering information before each renewal discussion. However, the impact extends beyond the account team.

Sales operations leaders are concerned about process consistency across teams, while revenue leaders are focused on renewal quality, account risk, and forecast confidence.

The partner follows up:

"How does each group define success?"

The customer explains:

- Account managers want to spend less time gathering information.
- Sales operations leaders want a more consistent preparation process.
- Revenue leaders want stronger renewal conversations and better visibility into account risk.

The partner summarizes:

"So while everyone is involved in the same workflow, each group is looking for a different outcome. Is that right?"

The customer agrees and identifies a sales operations leader as the primary owner responsible for improving the process.

This is stronger discovery because the conversation has moved beyond the first stakeholder. The partner now understands who experiences the pain, who owns the outcome, and which perspectives will matter as the opportunity develops.

**What sellers should notice**

This example shows why stakeholder discovery should go beyond the first person who describes the pain.

The partner identifies:

- Affected users: Account managers who spend time preparing for renewal calls.
- Outcome owner: The sales operations leader responsible for improving the process.
- Business stakeholders: Revenue leaders who care about renewal quality, account risk, and forecast confidence.
- Success perspectives: Each group defines "better" differently.

The lesson is to avoid treating one stakeholder's view as the whole opportunity.

Strong discovery clarifies who feels the pain, who owns the outcome, who cares about business impact, and who may need to participate in validation.

## Slide 20/43 - Introduction (Module: Using discovery questions to uncover evidence)

Good discovery is conversational, not scripted. The value comes from choosing the next useful question, listening for vague answers, and helping the customer make the current workflow more concrete.

## Slide 21/43 - Characteristics of effective discovery questions

The quality of your questions shapes the quality of the evidence you uncover. Effective discovery questions help the customer describe the current workflow before anyone discusses possible solutions.

The most effective conversations are:

- Curious rather than prescriptive.
- Workflow-centered rather than product-centered.
- Evidence-seeking rather than assumption-based.
- Collaborative rather than interrogative.

The goal is to understand the customer's current reality well enough to identify the problem, not work through a checklist of questions.

For example, a product-centered question might be:

"Would you like AI to summarize those documents?"

A workflow-centered question would be:

"What happens after the documents arrive, and where does the work usually slow down?"

The second question is more valuable because it helps uncover how the process works before discussing possible solutions.

## Slide 22/43 - Use questions to understand the workflow

Discovery questions should help you understand how work moves from start to finish.

A useful approach is to:

- Understand the current workflow.
- Identify where friction occurs.
- Clarify who and what is involved.
- Explore evidence of impact.
- Agree on a next step for validation.

This is not a script. It is a way to keep the conversation focused on the customer's work rather than jumping to solutions.

Useful questions might include:

- "Can you walk me through how this work happens today?"
- "What people, systems, or information are involved?"
- "Where does the work usually slow down?"
- "What is the final output?"
- "Where do handoffs occur?"
- "What makes the process difficult, inconsistent, or time-consuming?"
- "How do you measure whether this process is working well?"

The key is to follow the customer's answers. If they say, "The process takes too long," explore where the time is being spent. If they say, "Quality varies," ask what quality means in that workflow and how it is measured.

## Slide 23/43 - Listen, clarify, and follow up

Strong discovery conversations rarely stop at the customer's first answer.

Customers often begin with broad or incomplete statements because they are describing a familiar process quickly. Your job is to slow down the conversation enough to make the useful details clear.

Use four simple techniques:

**Listen for vague or incomplete answers:**

Words like "slow," "manual," "inconsistent," "risky," or "inefficient" are important clues, but they need detail.

**Ask follow-up questions:**

- "Can you tell me more about that?"
- "What happens next?"
- "Where does that usually break down?"
- "Who gets involved when that happens?"

A follow-up question helps the customer explain what they mean.

**Reflect back what you heard:**

- "It sounds like the biggest challenge may be the review step, not the intake step."
- "Am I understanding correctly that the work is slow because information is spread across several places?"

Reflection confirms your understanding and gives the customer a chance to correct you.

**Test a hypothesis when a problem begins to emerge:**

"It sounds like the workflow may be creating both efficiency and consistency challenges. Is that fair, or would you describe it differently?"

A hypothesis is a tentative interpretation of what you are hearing. It should invite correction, not force agreement.

These techniques often uncover more useful evidence than moving immediately to the next prepared question.

## Slide 24/43 - Explore impact and readiness

Once the workflow problem becomes clearer, the conversation can begin to shift from understanding the problem to understanding the opportunity.

At this stage, you want to explore:

- Why the problem matters.
- Who is affected by it.
- What business impact it creates.
- Whether the customer is willing to help validate it further.
- What next step would improve understanding.

The goal is not to build a business case or calculate ROI. Instead, you are trying to determine whether the problem is important enough to justify deeper investigation.

Useful questions include:

- "What happens when this workflow slows down?"
- "Who feels the impact most directly?"
- "How does this affect time, quality, speed, risk, cost, or experience?"
- "What would be useful to validate next?"
- "Who should be involved in a workflow review?"
- "Would your team be willing to share examples or process details?"

Pay attention to what the customer does, not just what they say. A customer who agrees to involve a process owner, share workflow examples, or schedule a discovery session is showing stronger evidence than someone who simply says, "This sounds interesting."

In many cases, readiness is revealed through action. The more willing a customer is to help validate the problem, the stronger the opportunity signal becomes.

## Slide 25/43 - Real-world example

A legal operations manager tells a partner:

"Contract review is too slow."

At first, that sounds like a clear problem. But "contract review" is a broad process, and it is not yet clear where the delays are occurring or what is causing them.
Instead of immediately discussing AI solutions, the partner focuses on understanding the workflow.

They ask:

"What part of the review process tends to take the longest?"

Rather than moving on, the partner digs a little deeper.

"Can you tell me more about where those delays usually occur?"

As the conversation develops, the picture becomes clearer. The customer explains that document intake is generally efficient and contracts reach reviewers without major issues. The real delays tend to happen later in the process, once reviewers begin evaluating documents and comparing clauses against company standards.

The partner summarizes what they are hearing:

"So it sounds like the challenge isn't the entire contract process. The biggest delays occur during review and approval. Is that right?"

The customer agrees and provides more detail.

They explain that:

- Reviewers spend significant time locating information and comparing clauses across documents.
- Different reviewers use different approaches, leading to inconsistent review quality.
- Managers often do not realize work is backing up until review queues become visible.
- Business teams experience delays because contracts take longer to reach approval.

At this point, the conversation has shifted from a broad complaint to a much clearer workflow problem.

The partner tests their understanding:

"It sounds like this workflow is creating both efficiency and consistency challenges. Would it be useful to look more closely at how reviewers perform this work today?"

The customer agrees and suggests involving both the review team and the process owner in a dedicated workflow review session.

By the end of the conversation, the opportunity looks very different.

The customer started with a broad statement:

"Contract review is too slow."

Through discovery, the partner uncovered a specific workflow, identified where friction occurs, understood who is affected, and agreed on a next step for validation.

That is what strong discovery looks like. The partner did not assume they understood the problem. They listened, clarified, reflected back what they heard, tested their understanding, and helped the customer move from a general concern to a problem that could be explored and validated further.

## Slide 28/43 - Introduction (Module: Assessing readiness and maturity through discovery)

Not every clear problem is ready to progress. Readiness shows up in the customer's actions, context, and willingness to validate—not only in how excited they sound about AI.

## Slide 29/43 - Discovering AI maturity signals

Discovery conversations can also reveal how familiar a customer is with AI projects and business change initiatives.

Understanding this can help you decide how to guide future conversations. It is not about assigning a maturity score or judging the customer. Instead, it helps you understand how much experience they have and what level of support they may need.

Some useful signals to look for include:

| Signal | What it may tell you | How it may shape the next step |
|---|---|---|
| Previous AI experimentation | The customer has tried AI before and may have lessons, concerns, or expectations. | Ask what worked, what did not, and what would need to be different this time. |
| Executive interest | Leadership may care about AI or workflow transformation. | Clarify whether executive interest is connected to a specific business problem. |
| Existing transformation initiatives | The customer may already be changing a workflow, system, or process. | Explore whether the AI opportunity supports or conflicts with that initiative. |
| Familiarity with workflow improvement | The customer may already understand process mapping, metrics, or change management. | Use that familiarity to discuss current-state workflow and validation needs more precisely. |

A customer's level of experience can influence how an opportunity should be explored and validated.

For example, a customer who has already experimented with AI may have a good understanding of which workflows are worth investigating.

However, they may also have questions about adoption, trust, governance, or how to scale successful initiatives.

A customer with little or no AI experience may need more time to understand the workflow problem, identify stakeholders, and clarify what they want to improve before discussing validation or potential solutions.

The goal is not to label the customer or place them into a maturity category. Instead, use what you learn to adapt your discovery approach.

Understanding what the customer has already done, what they already know, and how ready they are to explore the problem will help you ask better questions and guide the conversation more effectively.

## Slide 30/43 - Discovering readiness signals

Readiness is about whether the customer is prepared to take a meaningful next step.

Readiness does not mean every question is answered. It means the customer shows enough ownership, problem clarity, and willingness to validate the opportunity to justify deeper engagement.

Readiness signals may include:

- Clear ownership.
- Stakeholder engagement.
- Defined challenges.
- Willingness to participate in validation activities.
- Agreement to share workflow details, examples, or process context.
- A concrete next meeting with the right people involved.

Readiness is often revealed through behavior rather than direct statements.

A customer might say:

"This is a priority"

But if they cannot identify an owner, involve stakeholders, or agree to a next validation step, readiness may still be weak.

Another customer might say:

"We are early"

But they can name the workflow owner, explain the challenge, and schedule a review session. That opportunity may be more ready than it sounds.

## Slide 31/43 - Discovering governance and operating context

Discovery should also surface operating-context considerations that may affect future opportunity development.

Operating context means the practical environment around the workflow: the data involved, the systems used, the approval paths, the stakeholders, and any oversight expectations.

Governance considerations may include:

- Data sensitivity.
- Approval requirements.
- Compliance obligations.
- Human review or approval expectations.
- Visibility, monitoring, or oversight needs.
- Internal policy constraints.

At this stage, your goal is not to design controls, define permissions, or plan deployment architecture. Your goal is to understand whether these considerations may affect whether the opportunity can progress.

Useful discovery questions include:

- "What types of information are involved in this workflow?"
- "Are there approval steps we should understand before any validation activity?"
- "Who would need visibility into how this process is evaluated?"
- "Are there policy, compliance, or oversight considerations that shape how this workflow can change?"
- "Where is human review required today?"

These questions help you avoid advancing an opportunity without understanding important customer realities.

Strong discovery does not ignore governance. It identifies the considerations early enough for the right people to validate them later.

## Slide 32/43 - Real-world example

A sales operations leader explains that account research before renewal calls is often inconsistent. At first, the problem appears straightforward:

"Our account teams spend too much time researching customers before renewals."

Rather than assuming the issue is simply a lack of efficiency, the partner uses discovery to better understand the workflow and operating context.

The partner asks:

"How do account teams prepare for renewal conversations today?"

As the discussion continues, the customer explains that information is gathered from multiple sources, including CRM notes, call summaries, customer communications, and account plans.

The partner follows up:

"What makes preparation difficult for the team?"

The customer explains that information is often fragmented, different account teams use different approaches, and preparation quality varies significantly between individuals.

The partner reflects back what they have heard:

"So the challenge is not just the amount of research. The information exists, but it is spread across multiple sources and difficult to bring together consistently. Is that correct?"

The customer agrees and provides additional context:

- A sales operations manager owns the process.
- Team leaders are interested in improving consistency across account preparation.
- The customer is willing to participate in a workflow review session.

- Stakeholders want to understand how any future solution would align with current data-access requirements.

This discovery conversation reveals both readiness signals and operating-context considerations.

The customer is showing ownership, engagement, and willingness to validate the problem. At the same time, the customer is surfacing approval and data-access considerations that may influence future opportunity development.

Do not jump to product features, integrations, or solution design. At this stage, the useful outcome is a clearer understanding of the workflow, readiness, and operating context.

**What you should notice**

This example includes two different kinds of evidence.

**Readiness signals**

- A sales operations manager owns the process.

**Operating-context considerations**

- Customer information is governed by internal approval processes.

The lesson is to capture both.

Readiness signals show whether the customer is willing to move forward. Operating-context considerations show what must be understood before the opportunity can progress responsibly.

## Slide 36/43 - Organizing discovery findings

A good discovery summary lets someone who was not part of the conversation quickly understand what was learned, what remains unclear, and what should happen next. Focus on the evidence that explains the opportunity and its current level of readiness.

Rather than recording every detail, focus on the evidence that helps explain the opportunity and its current level of readiness.

Useful areas to capture include:

| Area | Question |
|---|---|
| Business problem and workflow pain | What challenge is the customer trying to solve, and where does it occur in the workflow? |
| Stakeholders and outcome owners | Who experiences the problem, and who is responsible for improving the outcome? |
| Initial use-case direction | Which workflow or use case appears worth exploring further? |
| Customer engagement and next steps | What has the customer agreed to do next? |
| Early impact evidence | What signals exist around time, cost, quality, speed, risk, or experience? |
| Readiness or governance considerations | Are there approval processes, security requirements, or other factors that may influence validation? |

The goal is not to create a detailed business case. It is to organize what you know, highlight what still needs validation, and make it easier for others to understand the opportunity.

A simple discovery evidence summary can use the following structure:

| Evidence area | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Customer problem | What challenge is the customer trying to solve? |
| Workflow pain | Where does the problem appear in the workflow? |
| Stakeholders and owner | Who experiences the pain, who owns the outcome, and who may influence progress? |
| Impact signal | What evidence suggests the problem matters? |
| Readiness or operating context | What customer behaviors, approvals, data considerations, or governance needs affect progression? |
| Next validation step | What should happen next, with whom, and why? |

This is not a separate qualification methodology. It is a practical way to make discovery useful.

A strong summary reduces ambiguity. It helps the customer, partner team, and OpenAI team understand whether the opportunity is clear enough to keep moving.

## Slide 37/43 - Strong versus weak discovery outcomes

Strong discovery outcomes provide clear evidence and a clear direction for what should happen next. Weak discovery outcomes often leave important questions unanswered.

Consider the difference between these examples:

| Weak discovery outcome | Stronger discovery outcome |
|---|---|
| "The customer wants AI for onboarding." | "HR onboarding coordinators manually chase approvals across multiple teams, causing delays before new hires are fully ready." |
| "The process is inefficient." | "The delay occurs after forms are submitted, when coordinators manually check status across HR, IT, and facilities." |
| "The customer is interested." | "The Head of People Operations owns the onboarding workflow and agreed to a workflow review with HR operations and IT." |
| "AI could save time." | "The customer reports repeated manual coordination, delayed readiness for new hires, and inconsistent status visibility." |
| "We should follow up." | "Next step: validate the current onboarding workflow, identify the highest-friction handoffs, and confirm what data or approvals are involved." |

Notice that the stronger examples are not longer because they contain more detail.

They are stronger because they contain better evidence.

They describe a specific workflow, identify where the friction occurs, clarify who is involved, and define a meaningful next step.

The quality of your evidence directly affects how confidently an opportunity can be qualified.

If the problem is vague, the owner is unclear, and the next step is undefined, the opportunity may still be interesting, but it is difficult to validate or progress.

When the workflow pain, stakeholder ownership, impact signals, and next validation step are clear, the opportunity becomes much easier to understand and evaluate.

## Slide 38/43 - Real-world example

An HR team wants to improve onboarding.

The raw discovery notes look like this:

- Onboarding takes too long.
- Lots of manual work.
- HR wants better coordination.
- IT and facilities are involved.
- New hires sometimes are not ready on day one.
- Follow-up needed.

These notes contain useful clues, but they are not yet strong qualification evidence. They do not clearly explain the workflow pain, ownership, impact, readiness, or next validation step.

A stronger discovery evidence summary would look like this:

| Evidence area | Summary |
|---|---|
| Customer problem | HR onboarding coordinators spend significant time manually coordinating tasks across HR, IT, facilities, and hiring managers. |
| Workflow pain | Delays occur when coordinators chase approvals, confirm equipment readiness, and track task completion across teams. |
| Stakeholders and owner | The HR operations leader owns onboarding. Coordinators experience the day-to-day burden. IT and facilities influence readiness. |
| Impact signal | Delays can affect new-hire readiness and employee experience. Status visibility is inconsistent across teams. |
| Readiness or operating context | The customer is willing to review the onboarding workflow and involve HR operations and IT. Data and approval requirements still need clarification. |
| Next validation step | Run a workflow review to identify the highest-friction handoffs, confirm ownership, and validate what information is needed to assess improvement. |

The second version is easier to act on. It shows what the customer problem is, where it appears, who is involved, why it matters, and what should be validated next.

This is how discovery findings become usable qualification evidence.
