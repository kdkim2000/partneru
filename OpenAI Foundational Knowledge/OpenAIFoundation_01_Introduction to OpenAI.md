## Slide 5/48 - Introduction (OpenAI's mission and vision)

## Slide 6/48 - OpenAI's mission

OpenAI's mission is:

"To ensure that artificial general intelligence (AGI) benefits all of humanity."

Artificial General Intelligence, or AGI, is a way of describing highly capable AI systems that are evolving to support a wide range of valuable work.

You do not need to become an AGI expert for this course. The important point is that OpenAI's mission is long term, broad, and focused on benefit—not simply on building more capable technology for its own sake.

This mission gives long-term direction to OpenAI's research, deployment, safety work, and product development, and helps guide how the organization approaches increasingly capable AI systems.

It also provides context for how OpenAI approaches research, product development, safety work, and real-world deployment. It helps explain why OpenAI invests not only in model capability, but also in safety, governance, product guidance, and learning from how AI is used in practice.

## Slide 7/48 - What OpenAI's mission means for partners

For partners, the mission shapes how OpenAI should be represented in customer conversations. It gives you a clear starting point for explaining why OpenAI exists and what its work is oriented toward.

It should not be used as a guarantee. OpenAI's mission does not mean every customer initiative will automatically create value or produce a specific outcome.

Customer value still depends on the use case, workflow, data, governance, adoption plan, and the way the solution is implemented.

A useful partner explanation might sound like this:

"OpenAI's mission is to ensure that advanced AI benefits humanity. For customers, that means the conversation should focus not only on what AI can do, but also on how it can be applied responsibly to real business needs."

## Slide 8/48 - OpenAI's vision in practice

Customers are usually not asking about AI research for its own sake. They are asking practical questions:

"How can AI help our employees work more effectively?"

"Can AI help us make better decisions?"

"Can AI improve customer experiences?"

"Can AI support innovation?"

"How do we adopt AI responsibly?"

OpenAI's vision becomes practical when it connects to these customer needs.

Organizations are exploring AI because it is becoming an increasingly important business capability across industries.

They may want to improve productivity, support decision-making, accelerate innovation, or create better experiences for customers and employees.

At the same time, responsible adoption matters as much as technical advancement.

Customers need to understand not only what AI can do, but also what must be considered when AI is applied in real workflows.

As a partner, your role is to keep both customer relevance and responsible adoption visible.

AI should be discussed in terms of customer value, but not in a way that ignores responsibility, safety, governance, or human oversight.

## Slide 9/48 - The partner role in extending OpenAI's mission

Partners help translate OpenAI's capabilities into specific customer contexts.

That translation work is an essential partner skill. A customer may understand that AI is powerful, but still be unclear about where to begin.

They may have broad interest, a list of possible use cases, or pressure from leadership to "do something with AI." Without structure, those conversations can become feature-led, vague, or overconfident.

Partners help by connecting OpenAI's mission and capabilities to customer-recognized problems.

That means asking questions like:

- What business problem is the customer trying to solve?
- Which workflow, team, or process is affected?
- What outcome would the customer recognize as meaningful?
- What needs to be validated before recommending a solution path?
- What responsibilities belong to OpenAI, the partner, and the customer?

## Slide 10/48 - From mission to responsible exploration

OpenAI provides models, platform controls, safety work, product guidance, and official documentation. Partners and customers shape how AI is designed, integrated, governed, adopted, and measured in the customer's environment.

A practical test is useful here:

> If you cannot connect the proposed OpenAI path to a customer outcome the customer recognizes, the conversation is not ready to move deeper.

That does not mean every early conversation needs a complete solution. It means partners should avoid jumping from OpenAI's mission directly to a product recommendation. The stronger path is:

**Flow diagram:** Mission → Customer problem → Workflow context → Responsible exploration → Possible OpenAI path

Use this flow to keep early OpenAI conversations grounded: start with OpenAI's mission, connect it to the customer problem, clarify the workflow context, explore responsibly, and only then discuss a possible OpenAI path.

## Slide 14/48 - OpenAI's evolution and operating posture

OpenAI has evolved as AI capabilities and real-world adoption have grown.

## Slide 15/48 - OpenAI's evolution

OpenAI began as an AI research organization focused on long-term impact. As AI capabilities advanced and adoption grew, OpenAI evolved to support larger-scale research, infrastructure, product development, and deployment.

This context helps because customers often ask how OpenAI develops and improves its technology. They may want to understand whether OpenAI is mainly a research lab, a product company, a platform provider, or an enterprise AI partner.

The most useful answer is that OpenAI is both a research organization and a deployment company.

Research helps OpenAI advance the capabilities and safety of AI systems. Deployment helps OpenAI learn from real-world use, understand practical opportunities and risks, and improve products over time.

That deployment work also connects OpenAI's mission to the broader portfolio you will explore across PartnerU, including products, platforms, and capabilities that support different kinds of customer work.

For partners, this means OpenAI should not be described only as a model provider or only as a consumer product company. OpenAI's work spans research, models, products, platforms, safety practices, and real-world adoption.

## Slide 16/48 - Research and deployment

Research and deployment play different but connected roles.

**Research**

Helps OpenAI advance what AI systems can do and understand how they work.

**Deployment**

Helps reveal how those systems are used in real environments, what customers need, and where products, guidance, and safeguards can improve.

Safety work connects both. As AI systems become more capable, they need thoughtful evaluation, documentation, controls, and responsible-use practices.

The relationship between research, deployment, and safety is easier to understand as a loop: research advances what AI systems can do, deployment reveals how they work in real environments, and safety practices help guide responsible progress across both.

**Loop diagram:** Research (Advances capability and understanding) ↔ Deployment (Generates real-world feedback) ↔ Safety (Helps guide how increasingly capable systems are evaluated, released, and improved) — the three connect in a continuous loop.

## Slide 17/48 - OpenAI's operating posture

OpenAI's approach can be understood as a balance between four connected activities:

**Research advancement**

OpenAI develops and studies increasingly capable AI systems.

**Deployment and product learning**

OpenAI brings capabilities into products and platforms so people, developers, and organizations can use them in real settings.

**Safety and governance learning**

OpenAI works to understand risks, improve safeguards, and provide guidance for responsible adoption.

**Customer and user feedback**

OpenAI learns from how people and organizations use AI, where they succeed, where they struggle, and what support they need.

For partners, the key is to explain this at the right level of detail. Most early customer conversations do not require a deep dive of research methods, model training, or technical architecture. They do require a clear, credible explanation that OpenAI improves through a combination of research, deployment, safety work, and real-world learning.

A clear customer-facing explanation might be:

"OpenAI combines AI research with real-world deployment. Research advances what the systems can do, while deployment helps OpenAI and customers learn how AI can be applied usefully and responsibly in practice."

## Slide 21/48 - Why organizations are investing in AI

Organizations are investing in AI because they see opportunities to improve how work gets done.

Common motivations include:

- Improving productivity.
- Streamlining workflows.
- Improving access to information.
- Enhancing customer experiences.

For some organizations, the first step is individual productivity: helping employees draft, summarize, analyze, research, or brainstorm more effectively.

This distinction shows that AI value does not come only from isolated pilots or individual time savings.

For example, a customer may start with a simple question:

That can lead to a deeper workflow conversation around where information lives, who needs access, what decisions the information supports, what governance is required, and how success will be measured.

## Slide 24/48 - A high-level view of the OpenAI ecosystem

OpenAI's ecosystem includes several major parts. A useful high-level view separates them into three areas: the product surface or experience, the capability or architecture needed, and the deployment or operating model.

At this stage, you only need an awareness-level understanding. Later courses will go deeper into each area.

**Product surfaces and experiences**

*ChatGPT Chat*

Supports collaborative thinking and creation in everyday work.

Employees can use ChatGPT to ask questions, find and work with information, draft content, reason through problems, analyze material, collaborate, and get support across common workflows.

This is often the most familiar starting point for people who want to use AI themselves.

*ChatGPT Work*

Supports delegated, multi-step knowledge work. A user defines an outcome, provides context and constraints, and reviews or steers the work as it progresses.

At this level, remember the distinction: ChatGPT Chat is collaborative; ChatGPT Work is delegative.

*OpenAI API*

Helps organizations build AI into their own products, applications, systems, operations, and customer experiences.

This is often relevant when a customer wants AI to appear inside software they already use or build, such as a customer support portal, internal workflow tool, or customer-facing application.

*Codex*

Codex helps software teams use AI across software development and engineering work.

It can support work across the software development lifecycle, such as understanding code, implementing changes, testing, reviewing, documenting, and maintaining software.

This is relevant when the customer's need is connected to engineering productivity, software delivery, or developer workflows.

**Capabilities and architecture**

This layer asks what the system must do. Capabilities such as agents and multimodal inputs can appear across different product surfaces and operating models.

Agents can help AI move from answering questions toward completing work within defined goals, tools, context, and boundaries. Multimodal capabilities expand the types of information AI can work with, such as text, images, and audio, depending on the product or platform.

**Deployment and operating model**

This layer asks where and how the solution will run and be controlled. It can include hosted or embedded approaches, identity, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, human review, and where relevant, customer-managed open-weight models.

Open-weight models can give customers more flexibility over where and how some models run. They may be relevant when a customer has specific needs around control, deployment environment, customization, or customer-managed operation.

These layers can combine, but they answer different questions. A customer might use ChatGPT Chat or ChatGPT Work as the primary experience, an application built with the OpenAI API for an embedded workflow, or Codex for software engineering.

Capabilities such as agents or multimodal capabilities can support those experiences, while the operating model defines where the work runs and how it is controlled.

The right approach depends on three questions: where the person or workflow will primarily operate, what the system must do, and where and how it will run and be controlled.

## Slide 25/48 - Understanding AI in enterprise workflows

As organizations adopt AI more broadly, four concepts help explain how intelligence operates within a business.

**Context**

What the AI knows about the business. This can include company knowledge, policies, documents, systems, workflows, permissions, customer information, or other relevant inputs.

**Runtime**

Where and how AI does work. This may involve the workspace, application, tools, systems, and steps through which work is carried out.

**Observability**

How the organization monitors and traces AI activity over time. It helps organizations understand what AI is doing, how it is performing, and what happened across a workflow.

**Governance**

The policies, roles, permissions, controls, and approval processes that define how AI may be used and who is accountable. Governance sets the rules and responsibilities. Observability provides evidence about activity and performance; it does not replace governance.

These ideas help partners avoid treating AI as a standalone tool. In enterprise environments, customers need to think about what the AI knows, where it does work, how activity is monitored and traced, and which policies, controls, approvals, and responsibilities govern its use.

Use this simple map to remember how AI fits into enterprise workflows: context, runtime, observability, and governance.

**Flow diagram:** Context (What AI knows) → Runtime (Where and how AI works) → Observability (Monitoring and tracing) → Governance (Policies and controls)

## Slide 26/48 - Getting clear on the customer goal first

OpenAI capabilities matter because they can help customers solve business problems and improve workflows. But the capability itself is not the goal.

A customer might say:

"We want to use ChatGPT."

Useful questions a partner should ask:

- What problem is the customer trying to solve?
- Who experiences the problem today?
- What workflow is affected?
- What would improve if the problem were solved?
- How would the customer know the AI initiative is working?
- What needs to be governed, validated, or reviewed?

Capabilities such as agents may be relevant across these surfaces when the work requires goal pursuit, multiple steps, tool use, or action.

Then clarify the deployment and operating model: where and how the solution will run, what it can access, and how identity, permissions, approvals, logging, and human review will be handled.

The partner role is to help customers move from "We want AI" to "We have a clearer understanding of what needs to improve and can start exploring whether AI might help."

## Slide 33/48 - Building customer trust

Customer trust is built through clarity, relevance, and honesty.

Effective partners use clear and accessible language.

They avoid unnecessary technical complexity, especially when speaking with business stakeholders who need to understand value, risk, and next steps rather than architecture details.

Effective partners also focus on customer goals and context.

They do not assume that a product is the answer simply because it is powerful or new. They take time to understand what the customer is trying to change, what constraints exist, and what success would look like.

Trust also depends on recognizing when deeper expertise is required. A partner does not need to answer every question on the spot. In fact, trust can increase when a partner says:

"That depends on the customer environment and requirements. Let's verify that with the right current source or bring in the right specialist before we commit."

That kind of response is more credible than guessing.

## Slide 34/48 - Responsible partner behavior

Responsible partner behavior starts with clear and considered communication.

Partners should avoid:

- Overclaiming
- Guaranteeing outcomes
- Treating uncertainty as certainty
- Making broad privacy, compliance, safety, or performance claims without verification
- Using outdated product information.
- Recommending a path before understanding the customer's problem

Partners should:

- Recognize uncertainty when it exists.
- Use approved and current sources.
- Confirm customer-specific requirements.
- Explain the difference between what OpenAI provides and what the customer or partner must design, govern, and adopt.
- Keep the conversation focused on outcomes, context, and responsible next steps.

This is especially important in early customer conversations. A customer's first impression of OpenAI may be shaped by how clearly and responsibly the partner explains it.

## Slide 40/48 - Introduction (Trust, accuracy, and communication discipline)

## Slide 41/48 - Why accuracy matters

Some AI topics need extra care because they affect customer trust and decision-making.

Customers may ask about:

- Product capabilities.
- Data use and privacy.
- Security and compliance.
- Safety and responsible AI.
- Expected business outcomes.
- Whether AI can replace or automate a workflow.

These topics require care because product details can change, customer environments differ, and requirements vary by use case, industry, region, and deployment approach.

Accurate communication helps build credibility. It also protects the customer, the partner, and OpenAI from misunderstandings.

A helpful rule is:

Use confident language for what is verified, qualified language for what depends on context, and escalation language when deeper review is needed.

For example:

**Verified**

"OpenAI's mission is to ensure AGI benefits all of humanity."

**Qualified**

"This approach may help reduce manual effort, depending on the workflow and adoption plan."

**Escalation**

"We should confirm the current plan details and privacy requirements in official documentation before making a commitment."

## Slide 42/48 - Common overclaims to avoid

Overclaims can sound persuasive in the moment, but they weaken trust over time. Avoid making absolute statements about:

**Guaranteed business outcomes**

Do not promise that a customer will achieve a specific ROI, productivity gain, cost reduction, or revenue increase unless it has been validated for that customer context.

Instead of: "This will cut your processing time in half."

Say: "This may reduce processing time if the workflow, data, and adoption conditions are a good fit. We should define success measures and validate the impact."

**Absolute privacy protections**

Do not make broad statements such as, "Your data is always handled the same way in every OpenAI product."

Instead, use current official sources and plan-specific language. For business offerings, OpenAI provides enterprise privacy commitments, but details should still be verified against the customer's product, plan, region, and requirements.

**Universal compliance coverage**

Do not say that OpenAI automatically satisfies every compliance requirement for every customer.

Instead, say: "Compliance depends on the customer's requirements, environment, use case, and the specific OpenAI offering. We should verify the current documentation and involve the right specialists."

**Automatic safety or risk elimination**

Do not say that AI risks are eliminated.

Instead, say: "Risks can be reduced, managed, evaluated, and monitored through the right design, controls, guidance, and governance."

Careful language is not weaker. It is more credible.

## Slide 43/48 - Verify before committing

Partners should verify before making commitments that depend on current product details, customer requirements, or trust-sensitive topics.

Verification is especially important when discussing:

- Privacy and data use
- Security and compliance
- Product availability
- Regional requirements
- Model or product capabilities
- Pricing, plan details, or limits
- Safety controls or governance features
- Customer-specific implementation requirements

A practical communication pattern is:

**Acknowledge the question**

"Good question. That's an important detail to confirm."

**State what you know carefully**

"At a high level, OpenAI provides business and enterprise privacy commitments, but the exact answer depends on the product, plan, and customer requirements."

**Commit to verification, not guessing**

"Let's verify the current official documentation and involve the right specialist before we make a commitment."

This approach helps you stay useful without overstating certainty.
